🌱 Morphology: Prefixes: over- (+ sub-/under- review)
Prefix over- + review of sub- and under-

This week we add a new prefix: over- means above, across, or too much. We’re also reviewing sub- (under, below) and under- (below). All three are about position.

📌 Examples

overlook = over- + look (miss; look across)  |  overcoat = over- + coat (a coat over your other clothes)  |  submarine = sub- + marine (under the sea)  |  underground = under- + ground (below the ground)

“Diana was enraptured when she heard the name Anne picked for the playhouse.”
Anne tells Diana she has named the playhouse Idlewild. The author says Diana was enraptured when she heard it. Anne is delighted by the name — she stayed up half the night thinking it up. So enraptured must describe a strong happy feeling.
2
Look at the Word Parts
Do you see a root, prefix, or suffix you recognize?
en- + rapt + -ured
Three parts: en- (cause to be) + rapt (carried away by feeling) + -ured (state of). So enraptured = caused to be carried away by feeling — usually joy.

✏️ Grammar: Concrete & Abstract Nouns
Learn it, then spot the irregular plural in sentences from Avonlea.

A noun is a person, place, animal, or thing. The subject of a sentence is the noun the sentence is about.

A concrete noun names something you can see, hear, touch, smell, or taste (apple, river, slate, brooch). An abstract noun names an idea or a feeling — something real but you can’t touch it (friendship, anger, hope, courage).

Concrete (5 senses): dress, brooch, schoolhouse, lake, ribbon
Abstract (idea/feeling): friendship, hope, sorrow, courage, freedom
Both in one sentence: Diana’s friendship (abstract) gave Anne chocolates (concrete) to share.

Anne felt deep disappointment about her dress.
Diana’s friendship became Anne’s greatest treasure.
Mr. Phillips disliked Anne’s temper.

🔠 Spelling: Schwa in Unstressed Syllables
Discover the pattern by listening.

Both syllable types are everywhere. Look at these two groups of words:

Group A — clear vowel sound (stressed)

cAt · bEd · tEAch · bOAt

Group B — soft, lazy “uh” vowel sound (unstressed)

sofa · pencil (the i is uh) · about · circus

🔍 What do you notice?

Group A: say each word out loud. Where is your mouth open clearly? Which vowel do you hear loudly?

Group B: say sofa. Which sound is louder — SO or fa? What does that a sound like?

Now look at pencil, about, circus. The bolded letters all sound the same lazy “uh.” What sound is that?

The schwa is a lazy “uh” sound. It shows up in unstressed syllables (the quieter part of a word).

Any vowel can make the schwa sound: a in sofa, e in taken, i in pencil, o in lemon, u in circus.

When you can’t hear the vowel clearly, it’s probably a schwa — and that’s why those words are hard to spell.

Growing-Up Watch: FRIENDSHIP
One aspect of growing up Anne shows us this week
💎 What is friendship?

Friendship is choosing one person to share your life with — your secrets, your imagination, your hopes, your worst days.

🪶 Anne’s moment:

Anne has invented Katie Maurice and Violetta because no one stayed. In Ch. 12, she clasps hands with Diana over an imaginary running stream and they vow to be bosom friends forever. It is half-pretend and entirely real. By the end of Ch. 15, when Anne quits school over the slate-and-Gilbert disaster, the deepest grief is leaving Diana. Friendship isn’t the story’s subplot anymore. It is the story.

💭 Think: Who is the most important person in YOUR character’s life? How will their friendship show up in your story?

Today’s focus: Today: CONFLICT. A story needs a problem — something getting in the way of what your character wants. Use the STORY-STRUCTURE map to find your story’s shape.

👀 Watch How — Meet Sam, our example writer

Sam now knows his story will have a problem. Today, Sam maps the parts of his story using the Parts of a Narrative tool: strong beginning, strong middle, strong ending.

BEFORE
Sam’s rough plan: Mateo goes to tryouts. He’s scared. He makes the team.
AFTER
Sam’s mapped plan: Beginning: Mateo wants to be on the team but is scared to try out. Middle (problem): Best friend Diego signs up; Mateo almost quits twice; coach yells ‘Faster!’; Mateo trips. End: Mateo gets up and finishes — even before he sees the list.

Notice: Sam’s rough plan was just events. His mapped plan names the WANT, the PROBLEM (with 3-4 things that make it bigger), and an ENDING that shows growth. That’s the shape of a real story.

📋 Today’s Tool: Parts of a Narrative

A strong narrative has three parts. Each part has its own job.

Sam’s narrative map (filled):

🟢 Beginning
Hook the reader. Introduce character + setting.
Mateo wants to make the team but is scared.
🟡 Middle
Tell events in order. Use transitions. Show feelings.
Diego signs up. Mateo almost quits twice. He trips during sprints.
🔴 End
Rephrase opening. Tell feelings. Share a lesson.
Mateo gets up and finishes — before he even sees the list.
