About the Book

Prince Caspian (1951) is the second book C. S. Lewis published about Narnia. A year after their first adventure, Peter, Susan, Edmund and Lucy are pulled out of a train station and back into Narnia. But in Narnia, hundreds of years have passed. Their castle is a ruin, the talking animals and trees have gone into hiding, and a king named Miraz rules a land that no longer believes the old stories. His young nephew, Prince Caspian, believes them, and he needs help.

We read it because it is a great adventure, and because it asks big questions in a way nine-year-olds can hold: what it takes to lead, why it’s hard to trust what you can’t see, and how to stay loyal when it costs you something. It also teaches readers to track several groups of characters at once, and to read a fantasy setting for clues.

The book is still under copyright, so the lessons quote only short lines. Your student needs the book itself.

“This must have been an orchard — long, long ago…”Susan, Chapter 1

How a Week Works

Every week has four lessons, one fluency reading, and a Check-In. Each lesson follows the same path, so your student always knows what comes next. Across the four days, the skills build:

Day 1Learn it: define and explain
Day 2Find it: identify and sort
Day 3Use it: write and apply
Day 4Prove it: revise and explain
  • 📚What Good Readers Do: one reading habit to practice in the chapter, with an on-page box for reading notes
  • ✏️Daily Oral Language: fix a sentence by copying a correct model
  • 🔤Word study: prefixes, roots, spelling patterns and vocabulary from the chapters
  • 🏛️Seven Pillars Watch: after each chapter, your student decides which of the seven OAO pillars (honesty, respect, courtesy, responsibility, courage, service, perseverance) real moments from the book show, and names the week’s strongest pillar on Day 4
  • 🎧Nell, the guide: a bookbinder who mends old books and has read the Narnia books every winter since she was nine. She introduces lessons in short clips and leads a short Word Study film each day. (She is made up for our lessons; she is not a character in the book.)
  • 🗝️Fill the Treasure Chamber: the word-parts game. In Chapter 2 the children find their castle’s old treasure chamber; each word your student builds sets a treasure back in its niche
  • 🎙️Fluency: a short passage read three times, then recorded
  • ✍️Writing Connection: a short piece of writing every day, and one full paragraph each week

By Heart: The Unit Poem

The stones still stand where towers fell,
The river hums a half-lost spell,
The trees lean close as if to say,
“We knew you’d find your way back someday.”“The Forest Remembers,” a short poem written for our Prince Caspian lessons

Four lines about a land that remembers the people who loved it, just as Narnia remembers the four children. Your student meets the poem in Week 26 and learns it by heart, with a few more words fading from the screen each time.

How to help: Ask to hear it now and then. By Week 30 they should be able to say all four lines to you.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

The whole unit · battles
Narnia is at war. There are sword fights and a battle at the end, told for young readers without gore. A few characters die, mostly in battle.
Faith and Aslan
Aslan, the great lion, is at the heart of the book. Lewis was a Christian, and many readers see Christian ideas in Aslan. The lessons treat the book as literature: trust, belief and courage, in the story’s own terms.
Week 26 · Chapter 3
Two soldiers are about to drown a tied-up Dwarf. Susan shoots an arrow that knocks one into the water; nobody is killed.
Week 27 · Chapters 4–5
Caspian’s tutor tells him that his uncle Miraz murdered Caspian’s father to take the throne, and that Caspian is now in danger.
Week 29 · Chapter 12
Some of Caspian’s own side try to call up an evil witch to help them. It is a short, dark scene, and a fight follows.
Week 30 · Chapters 13–15
Peter fights Miraz one on one, and a battle follows. The ending is joyful.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “What is this chapter mostly about?” and “How do you know?”
  • Keep a simple list of who is on Caspian’s side and who is with Miraz. There are a lot of characters.
  • Ask which pillar a character showed today, and why.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the poem.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Telling them what happens next. The lessons ask for predictions before each chapter.
  • Showing the movie first. It changes many parts of the story, and the questions are about the book.
