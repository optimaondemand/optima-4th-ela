This Week at a Glance

The biggest week of the book so far. Harriet slowly heals, works, saves her money and marries. When Master dies and everyone in the cabins fears being sold, she runs. She follows the North Star, the Underground Railroad passes her from house to house, and she crosses into Pennsylvania free. In Philadelphia she earns her own pay for the first time, but she is alone, and she promises to bring her family north. In Writer’s Workshop, your student gathers notes for the research report.

💛 A heads-up for families
Some parts this week are hard. Chapter 5 opens with Harriet badly hurt from the blow at the end of Chapter 4, and the injury leaves her with sudden sleeping spells for the rest of her life. Buyers come to look her over, poking and measuring her the way they would a horse. Her brothers start out with her, then turn back. On the road, patrollers (men who ride the roads hunting runaways) and slave catchers come close to finding her. Harriet carries a knife on her escape for protection, and in Chapter 9 she buys a pistol in Philadelphia. She never uses either one in these chapters. The book uses the words of its time, such as “master” and “slave catchers.” Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
Keeping events in time order. Your student watches for time words like “by spring” and “by midnight,” asks what caused what, and on Day 4 sorts what happens now from what Harriet remembers.
Big idea
Freedom takes courage, and help. Harriet leaves alone, but people help her at every step. Once she is free, she finds that freedom feels empty without the people she loves.

“There are two things I have a right to, liberty or death.”Harriet, Chapter 6

Words to Know

Use them at home!
chronologythe order in which events happened, from first to last
testimonya person’s own account of what they have seen or done
autographa person’s own signature (“self-writing”)
emancipationthe act of being set free
Word parts
tele- means “far.” auto- means “self.”telephone · telescope · autograph · autobiography
Spelling
The suffix -ion turns a verb into a noun. Sometimes it just adds on; sometimes the end of the verb changes first.act → action · decide → decision · permit → permission
Grammar
Comparing adjectives compare two things (-er or more) or three or more (-est or most). Never use both.darker · more dangerous · the bravest · safer, not “more safer”

New in the Lessons

A few things carry on from last week, and a few are new. Look for these:

🎧 Marcus is back
Three short clips from Marcus in every lesson: a welcome, the day’s Freedom Watch, and what to notice before reading. This week’s Freedom Watches: the strength nobody planned for, the moment of decision, the help of strangers, and free but not yet whole. Each clip has a “Read Marcus’s words instead” option.
🧵 Sew the Quilt: Harriet’s own quilt
The word-parts game goes on, and this week the quilt is Harriet’s. In Chapter 5 she sews a patchwork quilt as warm as her mother’s. In Chapter 6 she leaves it on the Quaker lady’s doorstep as a thank-you and farewell. Each right tele- or auto- word sews a patch, four a day, and on Day 4 the quilt is finished with a North Star across its middle.
🎵 Songs With a Hidden Meaning
On Day 2, a card shows “Bound for the Promised Land,” the farewell song Harriet sings as she walks past the cabins. Someone passing would hear a church song about heaven. Her family heard goodbye: she was going north, and the traders had not taken her. Students think first, then reveal the meaning.
✎ Copia: Say It Three Ways
On Day 1, your student rewrites one sentence, “Harriet saved her pennies,” three ways: a new opening, a stronger verb, and a where or a how. There are no wrong answers, only more ways to say it.
📝 Note boxes on the Reading tab
Each day’s note box now sits right where your student reads: “What happened, and when?”, “Before, turn, after,” “Clock and map,” and “Now and then.” Questions marked “Use your reading notes” draw on them.

Your Student’s Research Report

Writer’s Workshop
Wk 20 · Choose a questionWk 21 · Gather notesWk 22 · DraftWk 23 · Add facts and examplesWk 24 · Revise and editWk 25 · Publish and present

This week your student gathers facts, one source a day, in short bullet notes with one fact each. Day 1: notes from the book, each with a page number. Day 2: notes from a website, such as the National Park Service’s Underground Railroad page, in their own words. Day 3: a third source, ideally a primary source (Harriet’s own words), or a library book or documentary. Day 4: sort all the notes into 2–3 idea groups. Next week each group becomes a paragraph. The lesson suggests keeping the whole project in one document called My Research Report.

By Heart: the Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Days 2 and 4 your student practices the same four lines, with a little less help on the screen each time. The whole poem, read aloud, is still a tap away.

How to help: Ask your student to say the lines to you without looking. Then ask how Harriet is like the caged bird this week, and how she is different by the end.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Two traders said Harriet was “not worth sixpence.” Were they right?Listen for: no. She pretended to sleep when buyers came, worked hard, saved her money, bought a pair of steers, and grew strong again.
  2. Why did Harriet decide to run when she did?Listen for: Master died and everyone feared being sold; then Old Cudjoe’s grandson warned her the men were coming for her and her brothers that night.
  3. How did Harriet find north when the North Star was gone?Listen for: Daddy Ben had taught her to look for the side of the trees where the moss grew thick. She followed the Choptank River and waded in it so the dogs couldn’t track her.
  4. Who helped Harriet on her way north?Listen for: the Quaker lady, the woman at the cabin, Ezekiel and John Hunn, the guide who brought her men’s clothes and a rake, and Thomas Garrett, who hid her behind a bookcase.
  5. Harriet was free in Philadelphia. Why did she still feel like a stranger?Listen for: her family was still in Maryland and no one was there to welcome her, so she promised to bring them north.
  6. What sources did you take notes from?Listen for: the book, a website, and a third source, in short notes with page numbers or the site’s name.
