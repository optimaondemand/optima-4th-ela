## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> It’s Marilla. Imagination as a trap, the question says. Well. I could have told you that a year ago, and I believe I did. So watch for the moment a made-up thing stops being fun. Then there’s the last of the week’s word work, and it’s the checking kind, where some of what you see is already right. Don’t go fixing what isn’t broken. I’ve had enough of that from Anne.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> That’s me in the top sentence, and unfeelingly is the author’s word, not mine. I was feeling plenty. Look at how the talking is held in: the marks around it, the comma tucked inside before the said. The bottom one is me too, and it’s lost all of that. Put the marks where my words start and stop, the comma inside the last one, and a capital letter on my name. Then a period.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four boxes and then you’re done with words for the week. I never had much use for busywork, so I’ll tell you how to be quick about it. Two are matching, tap a thing, tap its partner. One is a choice: pick one, do it, don’t dither. Two are checking, and in those some of what you see is already right. Tapping a right one costs you. Read every word before you touch it.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Four chunks that all mean not, and each one is particular about where it will go. Like Rachel Lynde. Each has its own place, and the place is decided by the letter that comes right after it. So read the little words in the brackets, out loud, and look at what letter starts each once the chunk is off. Match by that letter. The meaning is no use to you here.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Five choices and you do one. Pick it before you’ve read them a third time. Choose the one you can finish with the book open beside you, and finish it. Half of two is nothing. If you take the one about your own story character, mind the words fit the person, not just the sentence. If you take the teaching one, teach it plain. A child won’t sit still for a lecture. Neither would I.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> One of those sentences has me watching from a window, and I’ll not say whether I do. That isn’t what you’re checking. Some sentences are right and some aren’t. Tap only the wrong ones. Find the person doing the thing first. One, or more than one? Then look at the end of the doing word and see if it agrees. Say it aloud if you’re not sure. Wrong ones jar. Leave the right ones be.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Some of those words are spelled right and some aren’t, and a wrong one can sound perfectly right, which is what makes it sneaky. Say each word. Then look at the letter making the sound, and the letter after it, and ask whether that’s how the rule spells it. Sound right, letter wrong: tap it. Right both ways: leave it alone. I’d like to catch you tapping a good one.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> The courage box is about the night Anne saved the Barry baby. Matthew told me. I never saw a case of croup in my life, and I’ll say plainly she knew what to do and I wouldn’t have. So that’s one kind of courage, being afraid of a real thing and going anyway. There’s another kind this week. Being afraid of a thing you made up yourself. Watch for which one this chapter is.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> The story keeps you inside Anne’s head. It never once puts you in mine. I only get the outside, the dishes and the patchwork. So do what the box says and tell it from my side for once. Then look across the whole week, the tea, the baby, the bed, the wood, and ask whose side you were kept on every single time. That tells you what kind of book this is.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 20

> One stop, at the end, and it asks how an imagination can be a gift and a problem at once. Well, I could answer that, but I’m not the one reading. Read the chapter through, and when you reach the part after dark, slow down. Ask what she’s afraid of, and who put it there. Then decide what she’s learning. Mind, learning and being told are two different things.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> I sent her through that spruce grove for a lesson and a warning, and I said I’d risk it, and I did. She came back over the bridge with her teeth chattering. Whether I was right you can decide. I’ve decided already. I usually have. Now say what you noticed, and show it from the chapter. Not from what you’d have done. Nobody asked what you’d have done. They asked what happened.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about what turns a game frightening. The hint tells you when they made it up; look at when they stopped enjoying it. The second is about me. I’m the one on trial, so argue it with the book, not with my feelings. Use what I said and did. The third is the whole week. Look back to the tea, forward to the bridge, and put those two Annes side by side.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your vocabulary answer is saved. I’ll tell you one thing about this week’s words. The author uses one of them for me, on the bridge, and it isn’t flattering. Well. It isn’t wrong, either. Look at your answer and ask whether the word you chose fits the person you put it on the way that one fits me. A word that fits is a word you’ve learned. A word that nearly fits is a guess.

## `marilla\g4ela-10-4-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Her gift is her imagination and her problem is her imagination, and I’ve been saying so since she came. Sam’s boy is the same: caring too much is what makes him want to quit. So name your kid’s gift as a thing people like about them. Then find the one moment it makes the wall taller. Read your two scenes out loud first, and count your words. Fix anything you stumbled on. Then write.

**How she stays in character.** Brisk, dry, warm underneath. She has no patience with imagining, sorting, or dithering, and gives exact instructions for all three. She admits she might be wrong about the wood and then says she has already decided. Her sarcasm is the Ch. 20 kind (“Headaches always left Marilla somewhat sarcastic”), never cruelty, and she says the kind thing sideways.

**What she knows, and when.** Before the reading she knows everything through Ch. 19, and the connector uses only what a reader of Ch. 1–19 would know: it is spring, it is nearly a year since Matthew brought Anne home, and Marilla has had doubts about the imagination throughout (she says so in Ch. 4 and again in Ch. 20). She knows about the croup night from Matthew (Ch. 18: “Matthew has been telling me about last night. I must say it was fortunate you knew what to do”). She does not know the wood is haunted until Anne tells her, so the pre-reading clips never mention ghosts, the white lady or the spruce grove; the read clip says only “the part after dark.” Her verdict, the risk, the march and the chattering teeth come out only from the Assignment tab on. She never hints at Ch. 21.
