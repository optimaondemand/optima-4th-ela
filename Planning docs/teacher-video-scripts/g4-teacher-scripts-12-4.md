## Lesson video · g4ela-12-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week twelve, and it's a day for stepping back. This week Anne dyed her hair green, floated off as the Lily Maid in a boat that sank, and took a big trip to town with Diana. Today you'll read Chapter 30, where Anne stops and looks at where her life is headed. Then you'll do the same kind of looking yourself. In reading, you'll sum up the whole week in just two sentences. In your questions, you'll pick one of Anne's mistakes and follow it all the way to what it taught her. ## Welcome · g4ela-12-4-welcome

Chapter 30 is different from most of the chapters we read this week. Chapters 27 and 28 were scrapes, with green hair and a sinking boat and Anne in a lot of trouble. This chapter doesn't have a big disaster in it. It's quieter, and a lot of it is people talking about the future. But quiet chapters can matter just as much as exciting ones. In real life, too, some of the biggest changes in a person happen when nothing dramatic is going on at all. You just look up one day and notice you're different. As you read, notice which parts of Anne seem the same as always and which parts seem new.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-4-dol

You might wonder about the book in the top sentence. Ben-Hur was a real and very popular adventure novel in Anne's time, so it gets capital letters like any title. When you type a book title, you can put it in italics, and when you write it by hand, you underline it. Now for a tricky case with the word Latin. Languages always get a capital, so English, Spanish, and Latin do. But most school subjects don't, so math and science stay lowercase. And when you fix the bottom sentence, you have two good choices. You can end the first thought with a period, or you can join the two thoughts with a comma and a joining word, just like the model does.

## Before the word work · g4ela-12-4-guide1

You've already met two of today's grammar words, and you might not have noticed. In the warm-up, Anne began to study and taught herself new words. Began and taught are both irregular past-tense verbs, and you used them without even thinking. That's a good sign. The grammar and spelling reviews today have something in common. Each one has a rule that works most of the time, like adding -ed to make a verb past or dropping the e before a suffix. And each one also has places where that rule stops working. A careful writer knows the rule and also knows where it ends. That's what both of these reviews are really checking.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-4-morphology

These two roots are hiding all over the place once you start looking. A porter at a hotel is someone who carries your bags. An airport is where people and things get carried in and out. And when you report something, you carry the news back to someone. The other root shows up on farms and in math class. A tractor is the machine that pulls the plow, and when you subtract, you pull some numbers away. A good book can distract you from your work, so keep that word in mind when Ben-Hur shows up in today's chapter. One warning, though. A word can have those letters without the meaning. A portion of pie isn't carrying anything.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-4-vocabulary

Two of this week's words came straight from the chapter titles. Chapter 27 is called Vanity and Vexation of Spirit, and that's the green hair chapter. Chapter 29 is called An Epoch in Anne's Life. Epoch is said EP-uck, and it's a time you'll always remember as a turning point. Why do you think the author thought that trip to town was big enough to be called an epoch? If you choose the word parts option, look at implicitly. It has a partner word, explicitly. If someone tells you something explicitly, they say it right out loud. If you trust someone implicitly, you trust them so much that nothing needs to be said at all.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-4-grammar

Some irregular verbs have three forms instead of two. There's the plain one, the past one, and a third one that goes with helpers like has, have, and had. You'd say she ate lunch, but she had eaten lunch. He wrote a letter, but he has written a letter. So the past form and the helper form aren't always the same word. A quick test helps when you're unsure. Say the verb after yesterday, and then say it after I have. Two different words might come out, and both are correct in their own spot. You'll need this in your own story whenever it jumps back to something that happened even earlier, like what your character had done before the big moment.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-4-spelling

Words like courage and change came into English from French a long time ago, and French writers use the very same trick. In French, the word for "we eat" is spelled with a g, then an e, then an o, just so the g stays soft. So this rule has been working for hundreds of years. One odd word bends it in a different way. Singe means to burn something just a little, like the edge of a piece of toast. When you add -ing, it keeps its e and becomes singeing. Without that e, it would look exactly like singing, and a reader would picture someone belting out a song. Sometimes a spelling holds on to a letter just so two words don't get mixed up.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-4-guide2

The mistake in the Growing-Up Watch box didn't start at the pond. It goes all the way back to Anne's first days at the Avonlea school, when Gilbert called her Carrots and she broke her slate over his head. Anne has held on to that anger for a very long time. So this week's mistake isn't really one bad moment. It's holding on to something long after it stopped helping her. That's a kind of mistake people make in real life, too, and it's a good one for a story. If you're thinking about your own character's mistake, try one that grows out of something they care about, like being right or being proud.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-4-wgrd

A summary of a week is different from a list of what happened. If your two sentences start with first, then, and next, you're probably still retelling. A strong summary shows how things connect. Try it with a story you already know, like The Three Little Pigs. Two pigs built houses in a hurry, and the wolf blew them both down. The third pig worked harder and built with bricks, so the wolf couldn't get in. Look at the little words that hold that summary together, like and, so, and because. Those joining words show cause and effect. When you sum up Anne's week, try to use them the same way.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-4-read

School worked differently when Anne was growing up. Back then, a lot of children on Prince Edward Island finished their schooling at a small country school like the one in Avonlea. Going further usually meant passing hard tests and leaving home to study somewhere else. So when you read about Miss Stacy's plan, think about how big a step that would be for a girl Anne's age. Also watch for time clues as you read. This chapter covers months, not one afternoon, and it ends on the last day of the school year. Noticing when time jumps will help you with your summary, too, because it shows you what the author decided to skip.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-4-guide3

For the second and third questions, you'll use exact words from the book, so let's talk about how to bring a quote in smoothly. Start with a few words that set it up, like "The narrator says" or "Anne admits." Then use only the part of the line you need. You don't have to copy a whole paragraph. A short quote that fits right into your own sentence is stronger than a long one that just sits there. If you need help finding a line for the second question, the Growing-Up Watch box on the Reading tab has a passage you can go back to. Choose your words carefully.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-4-chapter

Each of these questions has two parts, and strong answers do both. For the first question, think back to the solemn vow Anne and Diana made to be friends forever. That will help you explain why the list of names matters so much to Anne. For the second question, there may be more than one reason Anne keeps her feelings a secret. Think of two possible reasons, then choose the one the book gives you the best evidence for. For the third question, pick the mistake you can trace most clearly. Try using the words "before" and "after" to show how she's changed. If you want a challenge, ask whether she could have learned that lesson any other way.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-4-workshop

Why not fix anything today? When you stop to fix every little thing, you lose sight of the whole story. You need to feel it the way a reader would, all in one go. As you read, pay attention to how you feel. If you get bored or want to skip ahead, that's useful information. It's often a sign that a scene needs work. When you name your strongest part, say why it works, too. Look at Sam's Scene 3. It works because of the small, real details, the grass and the gate twenty steps away. And finding a weak scene doesn't mean you failed. It means you know exactly what to work on next week.
