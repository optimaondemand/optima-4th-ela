# Lesson 12.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 30 · The Queen's Class Is Organized. Guide this lesson: Miss Stacy. Each script plays right after Miss Stacy's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-12-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week twelve, and it's a looking-back day. Over the week, Anne went from green hair to a boat sinking under her on the pond. In Chapter 30 things finally calm down, and she takes stock of where her life is headed. You'll do some looking back, too, summing up five chapters and reading your own story from start to finish. You already know how to do all of it.


## Welcome · g4ela-12-4-welcome

I like that Miss Stacy admits she can't see everything. Readers sometimes catch more than teachers do. Earlier this week the chapters were full of scrapes, and Chapter 30 is calmer, with a lot of talk about the future. Quiet chapters still matter. Sometimes a person changes without anything dramatic happening, and only notices later. As you read, ask which parts of Anne seem the same as always, and which seem new.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-4-dol

Miss Stacy sounds pleased to show up in a warm-up sentence, and I don't blame her. You've mended a run-on like this before. In lesson two you learned two ways to do it, and the model uses one of them. Either way works on the bottom sentence, so pick the one that sounds best. One small extra about Latin. Languages always get a capital, but most school subjects, like math and science, stay lowercase.


## Before the word work · g4ela-12-4-guide1

Traps is the right word, and it's kind of Miss Stacy to warn you ahead of time. You've already used two irregular verbs today, in the warm-up sentence about Anne learning Latin. In grammar, think about why a writer might make a verb sound wrong. In spelling, think about what that quiet e does for a c or a g. If you can say the rule to yourself before you tap, you'll know what you're hunting for.


## Morphology · g4ela-12-4-morphology

I'd have loved those fern afternoons, though I probably would have named one too soon. You've lived with these two roots all week, in words like tractor and report. As you match, give the front parts some attention. Export carries things out of a country and import carries them in, so the front part changes which way the carrying goes. Watch for look-alikes, too. A portion of pie has those letters, but it isn't carrying anything.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-4-vocabulary

Anyone with a little brother or sister knows Miss Stacy is right about all those questions. These four words have been with you since the first lesson, and two of them came right out of chapter titles. Vanity named the green hair chapter, and epoch named Anne's trip to town in Chapter 29. Whichever option you pick, show what the word means in a real moment, the way those chapter titles did.


## Grammar · g4ela-12-4-grammar

Miss Stacy has a generous way of looking at mistakes, and I agree with her. You already know why these happen. Earlier this week we talked about little kids who learn the regular rule and then use it on every verb. That's the thinking to describe in your box. One spot to watch is a helper word like had. Some verbs change shape after a helper, the way she ate becomes she had eaten.


## Spelling · g4ela-12-4-spelling

Giving a silent letter a job to do is such a nice way to think about it. Think back to the start of the week, when you sorted words by what happens to that e. The clue is the letter right after the c or g, because some vowels keep those letters soft and some don't. If you're stuck, test the rule on a word that isn't in this draft, like peaceable, then come back to the six.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-4-guide2

Forgiving a mistake and never bringing it up again is one of the kindest things about Miss Stacy. Earlier this week we wondered whether Anne would act differently after a mistake. This box is where you start to find out. Read Anne's moment slowly, especially where she wishes she hadn't been so proud. Her anger at Gilbert goes all the way back to the slate she broke over his head in her first days at school.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-4-wgrd

Squeezing twelve months of history into a couple of lines sounds painful, and I can picture her older pupils groaning. You already have a tool for this from the start of the week, the somebody, wanted, but, so, then frame. Today you stretch it across five chapters. Ask what Anne wanted over all of them, not in a single scrape. Keep checking for your opinion sneaking in, and use words like so and because to hold it together.


## Before you read · g4ela-12-4-read

Readers getting to know more than the characters do is one of my favorite parts of a novel. Chapter 30 covers months, not one afternoon, so watch for clues about time. When the author skips ahead, that's the same leaving-out move you practiced in your summaries. Also think about what Miss Stacy's plan meant for a girl back then. Going on past the little Avonlea school took hard work and some very hard tests.


## Before you write · g4ela-12-4-guide3

I'd hang Miss Stacy's advice about rushing right over my desk. Bring along the habits from earlier this week. Turn each question into the start of your first sentence, and use names instead of only she. When you add a quote, set it up with a few words of your own, like Anne admits, and copy only the part you need. The Growing-Up Watch box on the Reading tab is a good place to look back.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-4-chapter

Miss Stacy claims the book knows more than she does, but I suspect she guesses more than she lets on. You know to check every question for two parts. For the second one, the hint underneath gets you started, so spend most of your effort on the why. For the third, words like before and after can show how Anne changed. For a challenge, try the extra question about whether she could have learned it another way.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-4-workshop

Leaving everything alone is harder than it sounds, and Miss Stacy knows writers love to tinker. All week you've been revising, adding in one lesson and cutting in another. Today you see what that work did. When you name your strongest part, say why it works. Sam's Scene 3 is the fall he slowed down at the start of the week. If a scene still feels thin, that's okay. Sam leaves his ending alone, because Week 14 is for endings.
