## Welcome · g4ela-16-2-welcome

Kirsti wanting a fairy tale with a king in it is so relatable. I asked for the same story every night when I was little. What I find interesting is how different the two kings are. In a fairy tale, the king lives far away in a castle. Denmark's real king rode right down the street where people could see him. That's part of why the Danes felt he belonged to them. As you read, notice how Annemarie talks about him. Does she sound like she's describing a fairy tale, or a neighbor? Listen for the details that tell you.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-16-2-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

The gap between then and now. I like that, because it turns a memory into a clue. Try it with something from your own life. Think of a place you used to go all the time, like a park or a store, that changed. When you notice the difference, you also start wondering why it changed. That's the move in this chapter. When Annemarie remembers something ordinary, don't just enjoy it. Ask what happened to make it feel so far away. Then write the change in one sentence. A clear sentence now saves you time when you answer the questions.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-16-2-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

A small mark before the word and. It's one of the most useful marks you'll ever learn. Let me show you the check on a different sentence first. Cover the word and with your finger. Now read each side by itself. If both sides could be their own sentence, with a who and an action, the comma goes right before the and. If one side has no who of its own, it doesn't need a comma at all. Then look for any name, of a person, a city, or a people. Each one stands tall with a capital. Now try yours.


## Before the word work · g4ela-16-2-guide1

Slow and careful wins. That might be the first time a game has told me not to hurry, and I'm here for it. Hunting is different from sorting, because now the wrong answers are designed to look right. A good habit is to say each word in a little sentence before you tap. If it sounds odd, look again. And if you miss one, read the hint all the way through. The hint usually tells you which rule you skipped, and that's the part you want to remember. Missing one is how you learn where the trap is.


## Morphology · g4ela-16-2-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

The handle of the Big Dipper, and maybe a sneaky little star hiding beside it. Keep your eyes open. Try this before you choose a root. Cover the end of the word and read only the meaning on the card. Does it talk about something breaking, falling apart or going wrong? Or does it talk about leading, taking or making something? If two meanings seem close, read them both twice, slowly. Decide what the word is about first. Then the root almost chooses itself. And keep an eye on the sky as each new star comes out.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-16-2-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: conduct]

Saying the word in a sentence about Annemarie is such a good test. If it fits her story, you've got it. Conduct is a sneaky one, because it has two meanings on the card. One is about how someone behaves, and one is about leading. Try a fun thing. Say it out loud both ways. When it's a thing, like her conduct was calm, the push is at the front. When it's an action, like to conduct a band, the push moves to the end. Your voice can show which meaning you're using. Try both in a sentence about Annemarie.


## Grammar · g4ela-16-2-grammar

More than one owner-word in each sentence. That's a lot of hunting, so let's sharpen your eyes. Owner-words answer the question whose. Practice with words that aren't in today's paragraph. Whose bike? My bike. Whose lunch? Ours. So for each word you're thinking about tapping, ask whose. If the word answers it, tap. If it names the person but doesn't show what they own, like I or we, leave it. And watch for the look-alikes with apostrophes. Read those as two words before you decide. If two words make sense, it isn't an owner-word, so let it go.


## Spelling · g4ela-16-2-spelling

Every part coming out clear and strong. That's the opposite of the hiding vowels you sorted last time, so this is a fun twist. To hear it better, clap each part of the word as you say it. On every clap, ask yourself, did that vowel sound like itself, or did it melt into uh? A word with a melting part has a schwa, so skip it. A word where every clap sounds crisp is the one you're looking for. Say each word in your normal voice, not your spelling voice, or every word will sound crisp.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-16-2-guide2

Refusing to forget. That gives me chills a little. A real piece of history goes with Grete's grandmother. In the summer of 1940, not long after Denmark was taken over, huge crowds of Danes gathered in parks just to sing Danish songs together. Nobody was fighting. They were singing. But everyone understood what it meant. Your Courage Watch box today is about remembering. Is there a family tradition, a song, or a story that your family keeps going no matter what? That's a kind of remembering too. Write one sentence about it in the box.


## Before you read · g4ela-16-2-read

[ON SCREEN: Stop 2]

A story inside a story. Authors do that when they want you to look twice. It's like opening a box and finding a smaller box. To find where the stories switch, watch the details. A made-up fairy tale sounds dreamy and far away. A real memory has specific details, like a place, a horse, or something someone said. When the details get real, Annemarie has stopped making things up. One more thing. Use the Then and Now slider at the top of the tab before you start. It'll help you picture the streets she's remembering.


## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-16-2-rwm

Holding a moment open. You can feel it when you read this one slowly.

[QUOTE ON SCREEN: press 6, read the line, press 6 again]

The sentence comes from Chapter 2: "She shook her head, waiting to hear the answer."

Notice the comma. Everything before it is the action. Everything after it is the waiting. The writer could have just said she waited. Instead, she shows the head shake first, so you see the girl before you feel the pause. For your Mimic, pick a moment from your own life when you were waiting to hear something, like whose name would be called. Show one small action first, then the waiting.


## Before you write · g4ela-16-2-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

The question about Lise needing care. Grete is so thoughtful about that, and so am I. When a question is about sad feelings, it's okay to keep your answer simple and honest. You don't have to write a lot to show you understand. For the question about the king, use what happens in the chapter, not what you remember from last week's reading. And if you're stuck on the famous answer, read that part out loud. Hearing it often helps more than reading it silently. Then explain it in your own words, in one or two sentences.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-16-2-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Explaining what it means, not just what it says. That's the difference between a good answer and a great one. Try it in two steps. First, write what the words say, in your own words. Then add a sentence that starts with, This shows that. That second sentence is where your thinking lives. On the question about Lise, two feelings can be true at the same time. Think about how you can feel excited and nervous before a big game, both at once. Look for the detail that shows each feeling, one at a time.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-16-2-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Sam sharpening his claim reminds me of sharpening a pencil. A dull claim makes a fuzzy point. Watch for one trap. A claim isn't a summary. Annemarie talks to a soldier is just something that happened. A claim takes a side about what it shows. Try reading yours to someone and ask if they could disagree. If no one could, it's a fact, not a claim yet. Add the part that says what you think it means. One sentence, clear and strong, is all you need today. Read it out loud and see if it sounds like something you'd argue for.
