# Lesson 15.1 teacher video scripts

Bridge to Number the Stars, Day 1 · How World War II began. Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-15-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week fifteen. This week is a bridge. We just finished Anne of Green Gables, and next week we open a new novel called Number the Stars. It takes place in Denmark during World War II, so before we start it, we're spending four lessons on the real history underneath it. Today's reading tells how the war began, with Germany, Adolf Hitler and the Nazis, and the Allies who fought back. As you read, collect the names of countries and leaders, so they already feel familiar when the story starts. In Writer's Workshop, you'll begin a brand new opinion project. This is hard history. It's okay if it feels uncomfortable, and it's worth staying with.


## Welcome · g4ela-15-1-welcome

Saying goodbye to Anne is a little sad. I'm glad Grete gave us a moment to notice it before we move on. Number the Stars is a kind of book called historical fiction. That means the main characters are made up, but the time and place around them are real. A common mix-up is thinking that because a book is fiction, none of it happened. In historical fiction, the war, the countries, and the dates are true, and the author builds a story inside them. That's why we read real history first. The novel takes place in Denmark in 1943, right in the middle of the war. So we'll start at the very beginning, and the story will make more sense when we get there.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-15-1-wgrd

I love Grete's suitcase. I'm the kind of packer who forgets a toothbrush, so I'll be making a list. A good suitcase isn't stuffed with everything, though. You don't need to memorize every date in the passage. Pack the facts the story will lean on, like who was fighting and which side each country was on. Then try the third step in the box. After each fact, picture a kid your age. If your country were at war, what might change? Maybe school, maybe food, maybe whether your family could stay together. On today's page there's a real photo of children in London in 1940, walking to a train that took them to the countryside to keep them safe. Facts like that are worth packing.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-15-1-dol

Grete's right that every capital in the model has a job. I like treating them like little clues. Months get capitals because each one is a name, like April in the model. Seasons don't. Spring, summer, fall, and winter stay lowercase unless they start a sentence, and that one trips up plenty of grown-ups. For finding where one thought ends, try this. Read the sentence out loud and listen for each complete idea. Each one needs its own who and its own action. When you hear a second who doing a second action, a new sentence has started, and it needs a stop in front of it. Then check your fix the same way you checked the model, one capital at a time.


## Before the word work · g4ela-15-1-guide1

I agree with Grete. The rules I figured out myself as a kid are the ones I still remember. Some of today's word work might feel strange at first, because you're asked to sort or guess before anybody explains. A wrong guess isn't a failure here. It's part of the job. The mix-up to avoid is jumping straight to the check box. If you peek first, your brain just copies, and the rule slides right back out again. In grammar and spelling, you'll also name the groups yourself. Your names don't have to match anyone else's. They just have to fit what you noticed. Then the Show What You Know boxes ask you to explain your thinking, and that's where the learning really sticks.


## Morphology · g4ela-15-1-morphology

A bridge that grows one plank at a time is a lovely picture, and I can't wait to see ours reach the far shore. Lots of word parts at the front of words are about direction or position, and you already know some. Sub means under, like a submarine that travels under the water. Pre means before, like a preview you watch before a movie comes out. Watch out for one mix-up. Pre and pro look almost the same, but they don't mean the same thing, so read every letter. And when someone calls a great player a pro, that's just a short way to say professional. It's a whole word, not the word part on this page. So always check the meaning, not just the first few letters.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-15-1-vocabulary

Grete's right that guessing first makes a word yours. It's like finding the trail instead of being handed a map. One of today's words has a second meaning you might already know. Occupation can mean a person's job. If a form asks for your parent's occupation, it wants to know their work, like nurse or teacher. That isn't the meaning in the sentence on your card, so let the sentence about Denmark steer you. Notice that Resistance in the example sentence has a capital R. That's because it's the name of a real group of people, the same way the Allies is a name. You'll hear more about that group this week. When you flip each card, check whether your guess was close, even if your wording was different.


## Grammar · g4ela-15-1-grammar

I like that Grete wants you to name the groups. Names you make up yourself really do stay put. These little words cause a famous mix-up. People say things like, Me and my friend went to the park. Should it be me or I? The trick is to cover up the friend. You'd never say, Me went to the park. You'd say, I went to the park. So it's, My friend and I went. The same trick works the other way. Grandma drove my friend and I sounds fancy, but cover the friend, and you'd never say, Grandma drove I. When you sort today, use that same cover-up thinking. Put each word into a short sentence of your own before you decide where it goes.


## Spelling · g4ela-15-1-spelling

Your ear knows more than you think. I love that, and it's true. You've been hearing these sounds since you were a baby. A vowel team is two letters sharing one sound, and sometimes one of those letters is a w or a y. The tricky part is that letters don't promise a sound. Think about the pair o and w. In snow it says one thing, and in cow it says something completely different. So the mix-up to avoid is sorting with your eyes. Say each word at a normal speed, the way you'd say it to a friend, not stretched out like a robot. Then ask yourself what sound you actually heard, and sort by that sound, not by the letters you see.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-15-1-copia

I like Grete's idea that each new version shows something the first one left out. That's my favorite part of this practice, because there's never just one right answer. When you pick a stronger verb, make sure it still tells the truth. A verb like exploded or vanished might sound exciting, but wars don't vanish. Choose a verb that's strong and still accurate. For the new beginning, try moving a piece that's already in the sentence to the front, or start with a word about time. Read it aloud afterward to make sure it still sounds like one smooth sentence. For the detail, ask yourself where or how, then check the passage so your detail is a real fact, not a guess.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-15-1-guide2

Calling it a small brave thing is just right. Reading hard history really does take a little courage. Courage doesn't have to be big to count. It can be telling the truth when you broke something, or sitting with the new kid when nobody else does. In both cases, you feel nervous and you do it anyway. The Courage Watch box asks you to name one small moment when you were brave and scared at the same time. Think of a real one, even if it seems tiny. All quarter, we'll watch people in real history and in our novel make choices like that. If something in today's reading makes you sad or worried, that's normal. You can always talk about it with a grown-up you trust.


## Before you read · g4ela-15-1-read

Picturing a child your age is such a good way to read history. I'll be doing it right along with you. As you make your two lists, watch for one country that shows up in both of them. That isn't a mistake in the passage. A country can be taken over and still have people fighting to win it back. Remember too that a country's name stands for millions of people. When an army takes over a country, the families who live there didn't choose that. Most of them were ordinary people, like the London children in the photo on today's page, just trying to stay safe. The passage is adapted from a museum in Washington, D.C., that helps people remember this history, so you can trust its facts.


## Before you write · g4ela-15-1-guide3

Grete's advice to keep the passage open is the best tip I know. Even grown-up writers check their sources. When a question asks for details from the passage, a strong move is to name where your proof comes from. You can start with words like, The passage says, or, According to the passage. Then give the exact fact, a name or a date or a country. A common mix-up is copying a whole paragraph and calling it evidence. Pick only the part that answers the question, then add a sentence in your own words about what it shows. Check your spelling of names, too. Countries and leaders are names, so each one gets a capital letter, just like in your Daily Oral Language sentence.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-15-1-chapter

I like how Grete puts the three questions together into one shape, like three puzzle pieces. Each question also has a challenge line, so read it before you answer. One asks how long the war lasted, so you'll need two dates and a little subtraction. Another asks what the invasions tell you about Hitler's goal. That's a thinking question. The passage gives you the facts, and you explain what they add up to. On question two, make sure every country you list is one the passage says was invaded, so read that paragraph carefully. For question three, the challenge asks why it mattered that so many countries joined together. Think about what changes when many stand together instead of one standing alone.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-15-1-workshop

Grete makes it sound simple, and Sam's two questions really do the work. Watch for one sneaky mix-up. A fact isn't always true. If I said the moon is made of cheese, that's still the fact kind of sentence, because you can check it. It just turns out to be wrong. An opinion is different. No amount of checking can prove Sam's pizza sentence right or wrong, because it's about what someone likes. Little words can be clues, too. Best, worst, fun, and should usually mean you're looking at an opinion. When you write your own, pick something you really care about, like your favorite game or your pet's funniest habit. Opinions are much easier to write when you actually mean them.
