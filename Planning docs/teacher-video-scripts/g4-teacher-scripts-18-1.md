## Lesson video · g4ela-18-1

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Hi, friends, welcome to week eighteen. The photo beside me shows the real coast near a Danish fishing town called Gilleleje. See that faint gray line far out across the water? That is Sweden, a country that stayed out of the war. On a clear day, people in Denmark could look right across and see it. Keep that view in your mind as you read this week. Your word game is new, too. It is a dark path from a farmhouse door all the way to the sea, and every right word lights up one more stretch of it. On the Read tab, open the Museum Case to see a real armband from the war years. In Writer's Workshop, you'll learn to stretch one line of explanation into something stronger.


## Welcome · g4ela-18-1-welcome

Straight out. That is such a good way to put it, and it is exactly what Annemarie does. Before you start, think about a time you could tell a grown-up was keeping something from you. Maybe it was a surprise party, or a present hidden on a high shelf. How did you figure it out? Annemarie has been gathering clues for a while: a great-aunt nobody in the family ever mentioned, and a whole household suddenly talking about her. This chapter is where she stops gathering and starts asking. Watch how steady her voice is when she does it. It takes real nerve to tell someone you love that they are not being honest with you.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-18-1-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

A tally beside an idea. That is an old farmer's trick, really. People have kept count of cows and sacks of grain with quick marks for a very long time: four lines, then one across. You can do the same thing in your journal. This chapter has one idea that comes back again and again, and Uncle Henrik is not the only one who returns to it. Annemarie keeps thinking about it much later, even while she stands in a quiet room full of strangers. So keep your tally going all the way to the last page, not just through the barn scene. When one line has the most marks, ask yourself why the author kept circling back to it.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-18-1-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Two names standing too small. What an image! I'll fix a different sentence first so you can watch. The cows were hungry but the barn door was locked. That has two whole thoughts. The cows were hungry is one. The barn door was locked is the other. They are joined by the word but, so a comma goes right before but to give the reader a short breath. Then a capital letter at the start and a period at the end. Now look at your sentence. Find the two whole thoughts first, then the joining word. After that, hunt for the names that need to stand up tall, and the word that has been squeezed together.


## Before the word work · g4ela-18-1-guide1

Pieces that go missing. That sounds like a farm mystery, like a barn cat nobody can find. For grammar, read each squeezed word slowly, and then say out loud the two words it came from. Put your finger on the tiny mark and ask which letters are tucked away underneath it. For spelling, clap every long word, but clap softly, and listen to the very last beat all by itself. Try it with a farm word like saddle. How many beats do you hear, and which sounds land in the last one? Collect a few words like that before you decide on any rule. A pattern you find yourself is a pattern you keep.


## Morphology · g4ela-18-1-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

No lights at all, not even one lamp in a window. Farm nights in Denmark were very dark back then. During the war, windows also had to be covered after sunset, so no light could leak out for airplanes to spot. That is why this path matters so much. Before you tap, say each word out loud and listen hard to how it ends. Does it sound like the word seed, or like the word guess? That sound is your first clue to the spelling. Then read the meaning on the card and imagine what the word is doing. Go slowly. On a dark path, careful steps get you where you need to be.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-18-1-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: proceed · concede · access · symbol]

Guess first, then flip. That small moment of guessing is when a word starts to belong to you. One of these words will matter all week long, even after the matching is done: symbol. A good way to test whether something in a story is one is to ask, would this object still matter if it were a plain, ordinary thing? A flag is just cloth, until you know what it stands for. As you read the next few chapters, keep an eye out for small objects that the characters treat very carefully, or hide, or hold on to. For the other three words, take each one apart and look for the go-root before you match it.


## Grammar · g4ela-18-1-grammar

Why there, and nowhere else? That is the whole lesson in five words. I'll show you one squeezed word that is not in your activity. Who is can be pressed together into who's. Say who is slowly, then who's. Which letter disappeared? Now look at where the tiny mark landed. Is it sitting between the two words, or right on the spot where the letter used to be? Try that same test on every squeezed word you find. Write the long version beside it in your notebook, and circle the letters that went missing. After three or four, check whether the mark follows the same rule every single time.


## Spelling · g4ela-18-1-spelling

Clap it and listen to the last clap. That works beautifully with farm words, because so many of them end softly. Think of a horse's saddle, or a field full of cattle. Clap them both. Then say each one again, very slowly, and stop just before the final beat. Notice which sounds you are holding when you stop, and which sounds are left over. Jot both words in your notebook with a line where you stopped. Then try the same slow stop on the words in your activity. One warning: a few words in the list are long but end a different way. Being long is not a reason to tap a word.


## Courage Watch · g4ela-18-1-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 1]

Three days to work up the courage to ask your own father. I believe it, and I'm glad you asked him. Asking something hard out loud can feel scarier than doing something brave with your hands, because you might not like the answer. Annemarie knows that feeling too. Pay attention to what she does with her voice and her eyes while she talks to Uncle Henrik. Then notice what he does with his body after she answers him. He changes where he is, so the two of them are face to face. In your journal, write one sentence about how a grown-up's actions can make a hard talk easier.


## Before you read · g4ela-18-1-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

A cow being milked at sunset. It sounds peaceful, and it is, for a page or two. One thing to know before you start: on a farm like this, cows were milked twice a day, every day, no matter what was happening in the world. Even on the most frightening nights, the animals still needed care. So when Uncle Henrik keeps milking steadily while Annemarie says something sharp to him, that steadiness tells you something about him. Later, when the house grows dark and quiet, listen for every sound. Lois Lowry makes you hear this night, not just see it. Stop at each pause before you read on.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-18-1-copia

A different spot in the barn each time. I like to think of it as moving a camera. The verb in this sentence is knelt, and it is a soft, careful one. Uncle Henrik is very tall, and he lowers himself on purpose. When you choose a stronger verb, look for one that keeps that care. You want a word that shows him coming down to Annemarie's level, not one that sounds rough or rushed. For your detail about where or how, think about the barn itself: the hay, the light through the windows, the cow still chewing behind him. Write all three, read them aloud, and keep the one that feels kindest.


## Before you write · g4ela-18-1-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q1]

Three questions that build like rungs on a ladder. Start with the first one, because it is the easiest to prove. When Annemarie tells her uncle he is lying, she gives him a reason, and her reason is very specific. Find it on the page. Then notice the word never, and count how many times she says it. That repeated word is one of your best clues that she is sure. When you write your answer, quote just a few of her words, not the whole speech. Then explain in your own words what those words show. A short quote with a clear explanation beats a long quote every time.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-18-1-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Did knowing less help her then? That gives me chills, because Annemarie wonders about the very same thing in this chapter. The third prompt asks you to judge Uncle Henrik's choice. There is no single right answer, but there is a strong way to answer. First, say what he decides. Then think about who else in the chapter is kept from the full truth. Later that night, Annemarie makes a choice about her best friend, and it looks a lot like her uncle's choice. If you point that out, your answer grows much stronger, because now you are comparing two characters instead of describing just one.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-18-1-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Three small moves. I'll show you with something that has nothing to do with the book. Take this flat explanation: The farmer was brave because she went out in the storm. Now stretch it. Most people stay inside when the wind is howling and the rain blows sideways. But the farmer pulled on her boots and walked out to the barn, because her animals were frightened. Imagine her holding a lantern against the wind, one slow step at a time. Do you hear how the reason suddenly feels real? Try those same three moves on one of the reasons in your own essay, using your character and the book.
