Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 32 · The Pass List Is Out. Guide this lesson: Diana. Each script plays right after Diana's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-13-2

Hi, friends. Welcome to the second lesson of week thirteen. In Chapter 31, Anne noticed herself growing up, and she said out loud that growing up is a serious thing. Today, in Chapter 32, all of Anne's hard work for the Entrance exam finally pays off, and we find out how she did. You'll start with theme, which is the big idea a chapter is trying to teach. The word work today is all about describing words, the verbs that link a person to a description and the words we use to compare. Then you'll read, answer three questions, and keep editing your story. Today you'll fix the dialogue, which is the talking in your story, and this chapter has some wonderful talking to learn from.

## Welcome · g4ela-13-2-welcome

In Anne's time, the results of a big test like the Entrance were printed right in the newspaper for everyone on the island to read. Imagine your test score in the paper for your whole town to see. No wonder everyone in Avonlea is waiting so anxiously. As you read, notice how waiting can feel worse than the test itself. Anne got through the exam, but she can't do anything now except wait. Think about a time you waited for big news. How did you fill the time? Keep that feeling in mind, because it'll help you understand Anne today.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-2-wgrd

The title of today's skill has two words you may not know, stated and implied. A stated theme is one a character or the narrator says right out loud. Yesterday, Anne said growing up is a serious thing, and that was stated. An implied theme is never said. You figure it out from what the characters do. Today's theme is implied, so you'll have to work for it. Watch out for one common mistake. A theme isn't what happens in the chapter. Anne tying with Gilbert is an event. A true theme should still make sense if you took Anne's name out of it, because it's about real people, too, including you.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-2-dol

Look at the comma between "dearest" and "loveliest" in the model. When two describing words sit side by side in front of a noun, you can test whether they need a comma. Put the word "and" between them. "The dearest and loveliest spot" sounds fine, so a comma goes there. But "a big red barn" wouldn't work with "and" in the middle, so it gets no comma. Now for the sentence you're fixing. To check whether you've got two sentences jammed into one, look for two people doing or being something. Each one needs its own sentence, or a joining word to hold them together. That's a slip you'll want to catch in your own story this week.

## Before the word work · g4ela-13-2-guide1

Have you noticed that each day this week, the word work asks something a little different? On Day 1 you noticed a pattern. Today you identify it, which means finding it out in the wild, in real sentences. Tomorrow you'll build with it yourself, and on the last day you'll catch mistakes. That order isn't an accident. It's the way people learn anything, like a new card game. First you watch, then you point out the moves, then you play, and finally you can spot when somebody cheats. So if today feels easier than building, good. It's supposed to. Pay extra attention to the Show What You Know boxes, though. Explaining why in your own words is what proves you really know it.

## Morphology · g4ela-13-2-morphology

Most prefixes work like little arrows. They point the action in a direction. You already know some that aren't in today's game. Ex- points out, like exit, the door you go out of, and export, sending things out of a country. Sub- points under, like a submarine that goes under the water, or a subway that runs under a city. So when you look at the four cards, notice that some of them are actions, like looking and building, and some of them are arrows that tell which way the action goes. Sorting them that way in your head first makes the matching a lot less confusing. Then check each pair with a word from the examples box to see if it makes sense.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-2-vocabulary

Some of this week's words have smaller words hiding inside them, and those can help you match. Think about the word "careful." It's care plus -ful, which means full of care. So when you see a word ending in -ful, ask what that person is full of. When you see a word made of two words stuck together, ask what each part means on its own. Words also come in families. If you have ambition, you're ambitious. If you keep your composure, people say you stayed composed. Knowing the family helps you use a word in your own writing. And notice that these words describe what a person is like on the inside, which is exactly what a good story character needs.

## Grammar · g4ela-13-2-grammar

Some verbs can switch jobs, and that's where this gets tricky. Take the word "felt." If I say "Anne felt the smooth paper," she's touching it with her hands, so that's an action. But if I say "Anne felt nervous," nobody can watch her do that. "Felt" is just connecting Anne to the word nervous, so it's a linking verb. It's the same word doing two different jobs. Words like smelled and sounded can switch the same way. So don't decide by the word alone. Decide by what it's doing in that sentence. One more clue to try: a linking verb works almost like an equals sign. The word after it describes the person before it.

## Spelling · g4ela-13-2-spelling

You might wonder why long words use "more" and "most" instead of an ending. Try saying "wonderfulest." It's a mouthful, and English speakers have smoothed those away over hundreds of years. One mistake shows up a lot, even with grown-ups. You compare a word only once. You can add an ending, or you can use "more," but never both together, like "more bigger." And a few comparing words break every rule. Good doesn't become gooder. It becomes better and best. Bad becomes worse and worst. Those you just have to know. When you write your own story, these are easy to slip on, so listen to how each one sounds.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-13-2-guide2

The last line in the Growing-Up Watch box says the payment Anne wants is somebody else's face when they hear the news. Think about how different that is from the Anne who broke her slate over Gilbert's head back in Chapter 15. Back then, everything was about her own hurt feelings. Since then, she and Gilbert have been racing each other at school for a long time. So the pass list is a big moment in that rivalry. That makes it really interesting to see what she cares about once the moment finally comes. When you're done reading, try the question at the bottom of the box for your own story character. It'll tell you a lot about who they are.

## Before you read · g4ela-13-2-read

The Entrance was the test Anne had to pass to get into Queen's, a school in Charlottetown where students trained to become teachers. Students from all over Prince Edward Island took it, so Anne wasn't only competing with her own class. Notice how the author handles time in this chapter. Weeks of waiting go by in just a few pages. Authors do that when nothing much happens, so they can save the slow, detailed writing for the moments that matter. As you read, watch for the spot where the writing slows way down and gives you every little detail. That's usually the author telling you, this is the moment to pay attention to.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-13-2-rwm

Did you notice the little marks around "pass high" in this sentence? Those are single quotation marks, and they're inside the regular ones. The author is showing us that "pass high" is Anne's own way of saying it. When you quote a sentence that already has a quote inside it, the inside one gets single marks. You'll see that again whenever you copy a line from the book for your answers. Now for your mimic sentence. Make sure the person you single out at the end really is the most important one, and that your reader can tell why. For example: Leo practiced every night for the sake of his mom and his coach, especially his coach.

Find that spot in Chapter 32 and read the sentences around it. Then you'll understand what the quote means, and you can explain it in your own words. Also, check the list at the very bottom of the page before you submit. It asks for your three Read Like a Writer answers from the Reading tab. ## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-13-2-chapter

Question one has two parts, and most people stop after the first. Finding where Anne's name appears is the easy part. The harder part is what it means, so think about everything that has happened between Anne and Gilbert since that first day of school. In question two, look at the word "nobler." It's a comparing word, just like in today's spelling. Nobler than what? The author is putting two of Anne's reasons side by side, so your answer should name the one being compared. For question three, choose one word from what Matthew says and explain it. A single word can tell you a lot about how someone sees a person, especially when that someone hardly talks at all.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-2-workshop

Let's look at a tricky case. Sometimes the speaker tag lands in the middle of what someone says. "You came," he said, "even in the rain." Notice that "even" starts with a small letter, because it's still the same sentence. The tag gets a comma on each side of it. Also, don't worry about using "said" over and over. Lots of students think they need fancy words like exclaimed or declared every time. But readers barely notice "said," and that's a good thing, because it keeps their attention on what the characters are saying. Save the other tag words for when they really matter. Then check your story for one line that would be stronger with a split tag.
