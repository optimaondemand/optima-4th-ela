## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-2-wgrd

Diana's a little hard on herself. Success is a fine place to start, and she found it fast. Today's skill has two words you may not know, stated and implied. A stated theme is one a character or the narrator says right out loud. Yesterday, Anne said growing up is a serious thing, and that was stated. An implied theme is never said. You figure it out from what the characters do. Today's theme is implied, so you'll have to work for it. Watch out for one common mistake. Anne tying with Gilbert is an event, and a theme isn't an event. A true theme still makes sense if you take Anne's name out, because it's about real people, including you.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-2-dol

Diana would vote for Orchard Slope, of course, and I can't blame her for loving her own home. Look at the comma between "dearest" and "loveliest" in the model. When two describing words sit side by side in front of a noun, you can test whether they need a comma. Put the word "and" between them. "The dearest and loveliest spot" sounds fine, so a comma goes there. But "a big red barn" wouldn't work with "and" in the middle, so it gets no comma. Now for the sentence you're fixing. To check whether two sentences are jammed into one, look for two people each doing or being something. Each one needs its own sentence, or a joining word to hold them together.

## Before the word work · g4ela-13-2-guide1

## Morphology · g4ela-13-2-morphology

I'm glad Diana admitted that mix-up, because those two little parts trip up lots of readers. Most prefixes work like little arrows. They point the action in a direction. You already know some that aren't in today's game. Ex- points out, like exit, the door you go out of. Sub- points under, like a submarine that goes under the water. So when you look at the four cards, notice that some of them are actions, like looking and building, and some of them are arrows that tell which way the action goes. Sorting them that way in your head first makes the matching a lot less confusing. Then check each pair against the examples box.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-2-vocabulary

Choosing words like a dress is such a Diana thing to say, and it actually works. Some of this week's words have smaller words hiding inside them, and those help you match. Think about "careful." It's care plus -ful, which means full of care. When a word is two words stuck together, ask what each part means on its own. Words also come in families. If you have ambition, you're ambitious. If you keep your composure, people say you stayed composed. Knowing the family helps you spot a word when it changes shape in a book. And notice these words describe what a person is like on the inside. As you read Chapter 32, ask which ones fit the people waiting for that pass list.

## Grammar · g4ela-13-2-grammar

Diana's test works for most verbs. A few sneaky ones can switch jobs, though, and that's where this gets tricky. Take the word "felt." If I say "Anne felt the smooth paper," she's touching it with her hands, so that's an action. But in "Anne felt nervous," the word is connecting Anne to nervous, so it's a linking verb. It's the same word doing two different jobs. Words like smelled and sounded can switch the same way. So don't decide by the word alone. Decide by what it's doing in that sentence. One more clue: a linking verb works almost like an equals sign. The word after it describes the person before it, the way nervous describes Anne.

## Spelling · g4ela-13-2-spelling

Diana's little-child test made me smile, because small kids really do say things like "gooder." You might wonder why long words use "more" and "most" instead of an ending. Try saying "wonderfulest." It's a mouthful, and English speakers smoothed those away over hundreds of years. One mistake shows up a lot, even with grown-ups. You compare a word only once. You can add an ending, or you can use "more," but never both, like "more bigger." And a few comparing words break every rule. Good becomes better and best. Bad becomes worse and worst. Those you just have to know. Chapter 32 is all about who came out on top, so listen for comparing words as you read.

## Before you read · g4ela-13-2-read

Eleven times is a lot of rereading, but that's what good friends do with a letter. The Entrance was the test Anne had to pass to get into Queen's, a school in Charlottetown where students trained to become teachers. Students from all over Prince Edward Island took it, so Anne wasn't only competing with her own class. Notice how the author handles time in this chapter. Weeks of waiting go by in just a few pages. Authors do that when nothing much happens, so they can save the slow, detailed writing for the moments that matter. Watch for the spot where the writing slows way down and gives you every little detail. That's the author telling you to pay attention.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-13-2-rwm

Diana's right to love that dash. Grown-up writers use it exactly this way, to stop and point. Did you notice the little marks around "pass high" in this sentence? Those are single quotation marks, tucked inside the regular ones. The author is showing us that "pass high" is Anne's own way of saying it. When you quote a sentence that already has a quote inside it, the inside one gets single marks. You'll see that again whenever you copy a line from the book for your answers. Now for your mimic sentence. Make sure the person you single out really is the most important one, and that your reader can tell why. For example: Leo practiced every night for the sake of his mom and his coach, especially his coach.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-2-workshop

Diana and I agree on this one. Dialogue is where a story really comes alive. Let's look at a tricky case. Sometimes the speaker tag lands in the middle of what someone says. "You came," he said, "even in the rain." Notice that "even" starts with a small letter, because it's still the same sentence. The tag gets a comma on each side of it. Don't worry about using "said" over and over, either. Lots of students think they need fancy words like exclaimed every time. But readers barely notice "said," and that's a good thing, because it keeps their attention on what the characters are saying. Then check your story for one line that would be stronger with a split tag.
