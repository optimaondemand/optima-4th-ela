Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 35 · The Winter at Queen's. Guide this lesson: Gilbert. Each script plays right after Gilbert's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what he says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

Today, in Chapter 35, she's living in town and studying all winter, and you'll get to see how much the girl who arrived at Bright River has grown. ## Welcome · g4ela-14-1-welcome

Gilbert is being modest about winning. He's been neck and neck with Anne for years. Think back to Chapter 28. Anne's boat sank, she clung to the bridge, and Gilbert rowed by and helped her. Then he asked if they could be friends, and she said no. Afterward, she felt an odd kind of regret about it. That was a crack in a very old wall. In real life, you see this with athletes all the time. Two runners push each other in every race, then shake hands at the finish line, because each one made the other faster. As you read today, decide whether Anne and Gilbert are getting closer to that kind of rivalry.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-1-wgrd

Gilbert mentions the slate so calmly now, which tells you something has changed for him too. One mix-up to watch for: growing older and taller isn't the change this box is asking about. Every character gets older in a long book. You're looking for a change on the inside. So start with something Anne did early on. When Mrs. Lynde made fun of her looks and her red hair in Chapter 9, Anne stamped her foot and shouted at her. Hold that next to how she handles things at Queen's. Your sentence frame on the page, "Anne used to ___, and now she ___," works best when both blanks are actions, things you could picture her doing.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-1-dol

It's nice to hear that even Gilbert gets nervous about exams. Even a tiny sentence like "I will worry" can point to the future, and the helper word is what puts it there. Take it away and you get "I worry," which is about right now. Now look at the sentence you're fixing. Its two halves happen at different times. The first half reaches back over months of studying, and the second half points ahead to graduation. That's part of why it can't stay as one sentence. Once you've fixed it, read your version aloud. You should hear a little stop in your voice right where the two times meet.


## Before the word work · g4ela-14-1-guide1

Gilbert's professors sound like good teachers. I care more about your reasons than your guesses, too. You might notice the morphology box says "Final week roots." This is the last week of word study for the year, so everything today is review, built from mistakes and words that turn up in real writing. Each Show What You Know box asks you to name the groups and explain the test you used. That's harder than sorting, and it's the part that sticks with you. If you can explain a rule to someone else, you'll catch that mistake on your own later. So if you get one wrong, go back to the "Now check your thinking" box and figure out what fooled you.


## Morphology · g4ela-14-1-morphology

Gilbert studying Latin at Queen's makes sense, because these roots come straight from it. They're hiding in lots of words outside of school. When a doctor gives you a prescription, she writes down what medicine you need. A P.S. at the bottom of a letter stands for postscript, something written after. The throwing root is just as busy. A projector throws a picture onto the wall, and an injection is medicine pushed into you. One spelling tip: when scribe gets the ending -tion, it changes to script, so describe becomes description. That's why the two spellings belong to one family. Anne is writing a lot of exams at Queen's, so this root fits her winter.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-1-vocabulary

Gilbert is wise not to say which word fits Anne. She'd never let him forget it. Consolation has a cousin you might know, the verb console, which means to comfort someone. If someone is so sad that nothing helps, we say they're inconsolable. That's the "not" prefix you learned back in week ten, stuck right on the front. In sports, a consolation prize is what you get for trying when you didn't win. Sanctifying is related to the word sanctuary. An animal sanctuary is a safe place set apart for animals, where nothing can hurt them. So when you see sanct in a word, think of something set apart and treated as special.


## Grammar · g4ela-14-1-grammar

I like Gilbert's picture of helpers lined up next to the main verb, like friends at a race. Helping verbs can be hard to spot for two reasons. First, they hide inside contractions. In "I won't be late," won't is short for will not, so there's a helper tucked inside. And "He'd come anyway" means he would come. Second, little words can squeeze in between the helper and the main verb. In "Anne had never studied so hard," never isn't a verb at all. It's just sitting in the middle. So find the word that shows the action first, then look in front of it for helpers.


## Spelling · g4ela-14-1-spelling

I'd have loved to see those old spelling matches. My guess is they traded wins for years. The doubling rule has a reason behind it, and once you know it, you'll stop guessing. Look at taping and tapping. One p keeps the a long, like in tape. Two p's keep it short, like in tap. The same thing happens with diner and dinner. So the double letter is like a fence that protects the short vowel sound. The y rule has a reason too. If cry plus -ing changed its y, you'd get two i's in a row, which English almost never allows, so it stays crying.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-14-1-copia

Gilbert's teasing Anne about long sentences, but she really did love a long one. Beechwood is Miss Barry's house in town, the same Aunt Josephine that Anne and Diana jumped on in the spare room bed years ago. Back then she was furious, and now Anne visits her in her spare hours. That's a nice little change to notice. Look at the verb in this sentence, too. We say people spend time, the same way they spend money, as if hours were coins in your pocket. We save time and waste it, too. When you swap the verb, try one that changes how the hours feel. Each choice tells the reader something different.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-1-guide2

I believe Gilbert that they're still racing. Neither of them knows how to do anything halfway. The Growing-Up Watch box uses the word bitterness. Have you ever tasted a lemon peel or plain baking chocolate? Bitter tastes stay in your mouth long after you've swallowed. Bitter feelings work the same way. They hang around long after the thing that caused them is over. That's what Anne's grudge against Gilbert was like for years after the slate. As you read, watch for the moment the box describes, when Anne hears about Ruby Gillis. Notice what she misses, and whether it's the contest at all.


## Before you read · g4ela-14-1-read

Gilbert isn't used to being one step behind, so enjoy being ahead of him for once. This chapter moves fast. It covers a whole winter at Queen's in just a few pages, so watch for the small words that tell you time has passed. Phrases about the season or the weather are clues. Queen's is a little like college. Students lived in town, went to classes all week, and studied for big exams at the end of the year. Anne is working toward a medal, and she's not the only one. You'll also meet the model sentence from your warm-up. Jane Andrews says it, and you'll see why.


## Before you write · g4ela-14-1-guide3

Gilbert sounds like he's been checking his work since he was six, and it shows. That sentence frame from What Good Readers Do, "Anne used to ___, and now she ___," helps in more than one answer today. Question two asks you to put the old Anne beside the new one, and question three can use that move too. A strong answer usually has a then sentence and a now sentence, with a clear word in between, like but or instead. Watch your verbs while you do it. Since you just studied helping verbs, check that your then sentence stays in the past and your now sentence matches it.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-1-chapter

Gilbert says he didn't know, and I believe him. That's exactly why question one is tricky. The challenge asks whether Gilbert would want the same thing, and we mostly see him through Anne's eyes in this chapter. So look for anything he does that you can actually point to, and use it as your clue. For question two, the challenge asks you to pick one word from your quote. Choose the one that carries the most feeling, then explain why the author picked it. For question three, think back to Chapter 3, the night Anne found out Matthew and Marilla had wanted a boy. What did she want more than anything then? Put that wish next to her dream at the window.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-1-workshop

I love Gilbert's idea of a first line and a last line that sound like old friends. That's a good test for any ending. Look at Sam's before version. "He was happy. The end." Lots of writers end that way because they don't know how else to stop. But Sam's after version never uses the word happy, and you still know exactly how Mateo feels. Also avoid wrapping up every problem perfectly. Mateo still expects to be scared next year, and that's what makes it feel true. And don't bring in anything brand new at the end, like a new character or a new problem. If you're stuck, try the frame "The same ___ that ___ now ___."
