## Welcome · g4ela-14-1-welcome

Think back to Chapter 28. Anne's boat sank, she ended up clinging to the bridge, and Gilbert rowed by and helped her. Then he asked if they could be friends, and she said no. Afterward, she felt an odd kind of regret about it. That was a crack in a very old wall. Now Gilbert's welcome tells you something has changed again this winter. In real life, you see this with athletes all the time. Two runners push each other in every race, and then they shake hands at the finish line, because each one made the other faster. As you read today, decide whether Anne and Gilbert are getting closer to that kind of rivalry.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-1-wgrd

One mix-up to watch for: growing older and taller isn't the change this box is asking about. Every character gets older in a long book. You're looking for a change on the inside. So start with something you know Anne did early on. When Mrs. Lynde made fun of her looks and her red hair in Chapter 9, Anne stamped her foot and shouted at her. Then hold that next to how she handles things at Queen's. Your sentence frame on the page, "Anne used to ___, and now she ___," works best when both blanks are actions, things you could picture her doing. And don't skip the last part of the box. Finding something about Anne that stayed exactly the same is just as interesting as finding what changed.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-1-dol

Even a tiny sentence like "I will worry" can point to the future, and the helper word is what puts it there. Take it away and you get "I worry," which is about right now. Now look at the sentence you're fixing. Its two halves happen at different times. The first half reaches back over months of studying, and the second half points ahead to graduation. That's part of why it can't stay as one sentence. When two ideas sit in different times, they usually work better as separate sentences. Once you've fixed it, try reading your version aloud. You should hear a little stop in your voice right where the two times meet.

## Morphology · g4ela-14-1-morphology

These two roots are hiding in lots of words outside of school. When a doctor gives you a prescription, she writes down what medicine you need. When someone adds a P.S. at the bottom of a letter, that stands for postscript, something written after. And a person who signs up for a magazine subscribes, which once meant writing your name underneath. The throwing root is just as busy. A projector throws a picture onto the wall, and an injection is medicine pushed into you. One spelling tip: when scribe gets the ending -tion, it changes to script, so describe becomes description. That's why the two spellings belong to one family. Anne is writing a lot of exams at Queen's, so this root fits her winter.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-1-vocabulary

Let's follow two of these words into other places. Consolation has a cousin you might know, the verb console, which means to comfort someone. And if someone is so sad that nothing helps, we say they're inconsolable. That's the "not" prefix you learned back in week ten, stuck right on the front. In sports, a consolation prize is the one you get for trying when you didn't win, which fits Anne's line about trying and failing in today's chapter. Sanctifying is related to the word sanctuary. An animal sanctuary is a safe place set apart for animals, where nothing can hurt them. So when you see sanct in a word, think of something set apart and treated as special.

## Grammar · g4ela-14-1-grammar

Helping verbs can be hard to spot for two reasons. First, they like to hide inside contractions. Look at Sam's new ending in Writer's Workshop. When Mateo says "I won't be scared," won't is short for will not, so there's a helper tucked inside. And "He'd come anyway" means he would come. Second, little words can squeeze in between the helper and the main verb. In "Anne had never studied so hard," never isn't a verb at all. It's just sitting in the middle. So when a phrase looks confusing, find the word that shows the action first, then look in front of it for helpers. They might be stretched apart or squished together.

## Spelling · g4ela-14-1-spelling

The doubling rule has a reason behind it, and once you know it, you'll stop guessing. Look at hoping and hopping. One p keeps the o long, like in hope. Two p's keep it short, like in hop. The same thing happens with diner and dinner. So the double letter is like a fence that protects the short vowel sound. The y rule has a reason too. If cry plus -ing changed its y, you'd get two i's in a row, which English almost never allows, so it stays crying. And when a vowel comes right before the y, as in play, the y stays put: played. Watch for these in your own story before you publish it.

## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-14-1-copia

Beechwood is Miss Barry's house in town, the same Aunt Josephine that Anne and Diana jumped on in the spare room bed years ago. Back then she was furious, and now Anne visits her in her spare hours. That's a nice little change to notice. Look at the verb in this sentence, too. We say people spend time, the same way they spend money, as if hours were coins in your pocket. English does that a lot. We save time, we waste time, and we say time is running out. When you swap the verb, try one that changes how the hours feel. Did Anne fill them, or did she drift through them? Each choice tells the reader something different.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-1-guide2

The Growing-Up Watch box uses the word bitterness. Have you ever tasted a lemon peel or plain baking chocolate? Bitter tastes stay in your mouth long after you've swallowed. Bitter feelings work the same way. They hang around long after the thing that caused them is over. That's what Anne's grudge against Gilbert was like for years after the slate. Now look at the question at the bottom of the box, about what your character wants now. It's there for a reason. Today you rewrite your story's ending, and the best endings show that a character wants something a little different than they did on page one. Think about your character's answer while you read about Anne.

## Before you read · g4ela-14-1-read

This chapter moves fast. It covers a whole winter at Queen's in just a few pages, so watch for the small words that tell you time has passed. Phrases about the season or the weather are clues. Queen's is a little like college. Students lived in town, went to classes all week, and studied for big exams at the end of the year. Anne is working toward a medal, and she's not the only one. While you read, you'll also meet the model sentence from your warm-up. Jane Andrews says it, and you'll see why. When you get to each Pause and Think stop, really stop. Put your finger on the page and think before you keep going.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-1-guide3

That sentence frame from What Good Readers Do, "Anne used to ___, and now she ___," is going to help you in more than one answer today. Questions two and three both ask you to put an old Anne beside a new one. A strong answer usually has a then sentence and a now sentence, with a clear word in between, like but, instead, or now. Watch your verbs while you do this. Since you just studied helping verbs, check that your then sentence stays in the past and your now sentence matches it. Mixing times in the middle of a sentence is one of the easiest ways to confuse a reader, and one of the easiest to fix.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-1-chapter

For question one, the challenge asks whether Gilbert would want the same thing. That's tricky, because we mostly see Gilbert through Anne's eyes in this chapter. So look for anything he does that you can actually point to, and use it as your clue. For question two, the challenge asks you to pick one word from your quote. Choose the one that carries the most feeling, then explain what that word means and why the author picked it. For question three, think back to Chapter 3, the night Anne found out Matthew and Marilla had wanted a boy. What did she want more than anything then? Put that wish next to her dream at the window.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-1-workshop

Look at Sam's before version for a second. "He was happy. The end." Lots of writers end that way because they don't know how else to stop. But the page points out something about his after version. It never uses the word happy, and you still know exactly how Mateo feels. One more thing to avoid is wrapping up every problem perfectly. Mateo still expects to be scared next year, and that's what makes it feel true. Your ending also shouldn't bring in anything brand new, like a new character or a new problem. If you're stuck, try the frame "The same ___ that ___ now ___." It's built for circling back, and it works with almost any story.
