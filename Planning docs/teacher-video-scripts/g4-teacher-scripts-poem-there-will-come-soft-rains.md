## Whole poem · poem/there-will-come-soft-rains-full

Hi, friends. Every book we read this year comes with a poem, and today you get to meet the one that goes with Number the Stars. It's called "There Will Come Soft Rains," and it was written by an American poet named Sara Teasdale near the end of the First World War. Under the title she wrote two small words in parentheses: War Time. Keep those words in mind, because they matter.

Today we're not memorizing anything. Today we just meet the poem. So before I read it, let me show you how I read a poem when I see it for the first time.

First, I listen to the whole thing before I worry about any one word. Second, I read it out loud, slowly, and I pause at the commas and periods, not at the end of every line. If a line doesn't have any punctuation at the end, I keep right on going into the next line. Third, I notice the gaps. This poem comes in pairs of lines, and each pair paints one picture. When I reach a gap, I stop and let myself see that picture. And fourth, I watch for the turn, the place where a poem changes direction.

Now listen to the whole poem, and try to see each picture.

There will come soft rains and the smell of the ground,
And swallows circling with their shimmering sound;

And frogs in the pools singing at night,
And wild plum-trees in tremulous white;

Robins will wear their feathery fire
Whistling their whims on a low fence-wire;

And not one will know of the war, not one
Will care at last when it is done.

Not one would mind, neither bird nor tree
If mankind perished utterly;

And Spring herself, when she woke at dawn,
Would scarcely know that we were gone.

Did you feel the turn? The first lines are full of spring: rain, birds, frogs, plum trees covered in white blossoms. Then, right in the middle, the poem says the birds won't know about the war at all. Nature just keeps going. The poet is wondering how small a war might look to the rest of the world, and that's a big, quiet thought to sit with.

Now look at the lines in gold on your page, the ones about the robins and the war. Those four lines are yours. Over the next few weeks, you'll learn them by heart, a little at a time, and by the end of the book you'll be able to say them without looking.

A few words to know before you go. Tremulous means trembling, like blossoms shaking in the breeze. Feathery fire is the robin's bright red-orange chest. And whims are little sudden ideas, so the robins are whistling whatever comes into their heads.

Now it's your turn. Read the whole poem out loud once or twice, pausing at the punctuation, and think about the question under the poem. Why would a poet fill a war poem with rain, birds, frogs, and trees?
