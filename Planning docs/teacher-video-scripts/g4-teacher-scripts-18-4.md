## Welcome · g4ela-18-4-welcome

What it feels like to wait. That might be the hardest thing Grete has asked all week. Waiting is tricky to write about, because almost nothing happens on the outside. So watch the inside instead. Each time Annemarie pictures where someone is right now, put a small mark in your journal. By the end, you'll see how far a mind can travel while a body sits still in a rocking chair.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-18-4-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

This might mean, because. Careful words like that make a strong reader. Try it with the clock in the hall. Point to the line first. Then give the clock one idea, maybe time running out. Now give it a second idea that's just as fair, maybe someone being counted home. Two good maybes beat one quick guess.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-18-4-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Two sentences in a pile. That happens to every writer in a hurry. I'll fix a different one while you watch, about a boat waiting at a dock. First I find where one thought ends and give it a period. The next thought gets a capital to begin. Last, I look for two words pressed together and set the small mark where the missing letter used to be. Your turn.


## Before the word work · g4ela-18-4-guide1

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule · Spelling rule]

Almost right is the sneakiest kind of wrong. Think of a harbor master checking boats before they sail. He does not take every boat apart. He looks closely and stops only the ones with a real problem. Do the same. If a line is already fine, letting it sail is the right call.


## Morphology · g4ela-18-4-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Someone waiting at the harbor. I've been hoping we'd get there. This is the final stretch, so slow down on the spelling. Two of these roots sound exactly alike, so your ears won't help you. Your eyes have to. Before you type, picture the word on a page you've read. When the boat lights up, look back at the whole path you walked this week.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-18-4-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: symbol]

No misses is a fine goal, and Grete's harder challenge is even better. A symbol can be small. In this chapter, think about the path Annemarie keeps watching from the window. Write your sentence, then read it aloud. Does your second word really belong, or did you squeeze it in? If it feels squeezed, try a different word.


## Grammar · g4ela-18-4-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

The long way and the short way. That trick works every time. Read slowly, like a lighthouse keeper scanning the water, one sentence at a time. Say the two full words wherever you see the mark. If the sentence still makes sense, the mark belongs. If it suddenly sounds silly, the mark has to go.


## Spelling · g4ela-18-4-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Clap it out. Your hands are better spellers than you think. A ship's bell rings in steady beats, and words do too. Clap the word first, then count the pieces on the screen. When the numbers don't match, you've found one to fix. Fix it, then clap it once more to be sure.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-18-4-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 3]

Mending the same sock over and over. That picture will stay with me. Busy hands and a waiting heart. As you read, notice what Annemarie does with her body and her mind while the clock moves. She rocks, she pictures the path, she counts. Ask yourself which of those helps her stay calm, and which might make the waiting harder.


## Before you read · g4ela-18-4-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Read the last page twice. Good advice for a chapter this quiet. One thing worth knowing first: many Danish homes in those years had a tall clock like the one in the Museum Case, and its chimes carried through the whole house. So when the clock strikes, count along with Annemarie. When you reach the end, stop and sit with it a moment before you write.


## Before you write · g4ela-18-4-guide3

Three prompts and then a paragraph. Pace yourself. Answer while the chapter is fresh in your mind. Keep your first two answers short and exact, like a ship's log: what happened, when, and what it shows. Save your longer thinking for the third one. That one has room to stretch.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-18-4-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q1]

Your answer can change. It helps to hear Grete say that out loud. Before the big one, though, take care with the first two. For what wakes Annemarie, be exact. The book tells you, and the detail is small. For why Mama's absence worries her, use times. Annemarie keeps careful track of them, so put her numbers in your answer. Numbers make strong evidence.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-18-4-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Complete, not perfect. Put that on a sticky note. Pick the body paragraph you like least, not the one you like most. That one has the most room to grow. Stretch your so-what until it says why the moment matters. Then tie a knot back to your claim, so the paragraph sails home to where it started.
