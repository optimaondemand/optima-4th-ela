# Lesson 13.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 34 · A Queen's Girl. Guide this lesson: Anne. Each script plays right after Anne's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-13-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week thirteen, and what a week it's been for Anne. In Chapter 31 she grew two inches and got quieter. In Chapter 32 she tied with Gilbert for first place on the pass list, and her first thought was Matthew. In Chapter 33 she recited at the hotel concert and told her friends they were already rich. Today, in Chapter 34, she gets ready to leave Green Gables for Queen's. Your word work reviews linking verbs and comparing words. ## Welcome · g4ela-13-4-welcome

Think back to the very first days Anne spent at Green Gables. She asked Marilla to call her Cordelia, because she thought Anne was such an unromantic name. If she had to be Anne, she wanted it spelled with an e. That little girl wished she could be somebody else. Today you'll read about a young woman who is about to leave home, and she's working hard to hold on to who she already is. That's a big turn for one character to make across a whole book. As you read, ask yourself when you think Anne stopped wishing to be someone else. There isn't one right answer, but it's worth wondering about.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-4-dol

Look at a small word in the model, herself. Words ending in self point straight back to the subject of the sentence. Anne found herself alone means Anne found Anne, which is why we need the self. Pronouns like she and he work in a similar way. Whenever a new sentence starts with she, your reader looks back for the last girl you named. That's usually fine, but if two people were just mentioned, your reader can get lost. Try this one: Anne and Ruby walked in, and she looked tired. Which girl looked tired? Nobody can tell. In today's Writer's Workshop, notice that Sam's after version swaps one he for a name. When you read your story aloud, listen for every she and he.

## Before the word work · g4ela-13-4-guide1

The mistakes in today's review aren't random. They're the slips real fourth graders make all the time, which is why they're worth studying. As you go, try to notice which kind of mistake you have the hardest time catching. Maybe you trust anything that sounds fancy. Maybe you forget that a verb can switch jobs. Everybody has a couple of trouble spots, even grown-up writers. Once you know yours, write them down somewhere you'll see them, like the back of your notebook. Next week you publish your story, and a short list of your own usual slips is the best editing tool you can have. It's a lot faster to hunt for two things you know you do than to hunt for everything.

## Morphology · g4ela-13-4-morphology

A few weeks ago, you learned that the prefix in- can mean "not," like in "incapable" and "inexorable." Today it shows up again, but in "inspect" and "instruct," it means something different. So the same two letters can do two different jobs. How can you tell which one you've got? Try taking it off. If what's left is a whole word, and the prefix turns it into its opposite, it probably means "not." Capable and incapable are opposites. But "spect" isn't a word by itself, and inspecting isn't the opposite of looking. When the leftover part is a root like these, think about direction instead. That's the job this kind of in- usually does.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-4-vocabulary

Words can carry a feeling along with their meaning. Ambition is a good example. In some stories, ambition is what pushes a hero forward. In others, it's what makes a villain grab for too much. The word itself means the same thing in both, but the story decides whether it's good or bad. So if you choose to explain ambition, it's worth saying what kind. Composure works a little differently. You almost never hear it used for something bad, but people usually only notice it when things are hard. Nobody praises your composure while you're eating a sandwich. If you pick the choice about teaching a younger student, give an example from real life before you give a definition. Kids remember stories better than meanings.

## Grammar · g4ela-13-4-grammar

A lot of people think an action verb has to mean moving around, like running or jumping. But action verbs can be quiet. Thinking, wishing, studying, and remembering are all actions, even if you're sitting perfectly still. That trips up many readers. A linking verb doesn't do anything at all. It connects the subject to a word that tells more about it. Some verbs can switch jobs, depending on the sentence. In "Marilla smelled the bread," she's doing something with her nose. In "The bread smelled fresh," the bread isn't doing anything. It's just being described. Look at what comes after the verb, and ask whether it describes the subject or is something the subject did.

## Spelling · g4ela-13-4-spelling

The model sentence in today's warm-up used the word "lonesomer." It sounds a little old-fashioned now. Lonesome has two syllables, and two-syllable words are the tricky middle ground. Some take -er, like quieter, and some sound better with more, like more careful. Most people today would say "more lonesome," but the author chose -er, and writers are allowed to bend a rule on purpose. A couple of words break the rules completely. You say good, better, best, not gooder, and bad, worse, worst. And watch short words that end in one vowel and one consonant, like big. They double the last letter before the ending, so big becomes bigger and biggest.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-13-4-guide2

Do you remember the title of Chapter 31? It was "Where the Brook and River Meet." The author used a picture from nature to show Anne growing up, a little stream widening into a river. In this chapter, Anne uses a nature picture too, about a tree. If you've never seen a gardener prune, they snip off some branches on purpose so the tree can grow strong in a good shape. Why do you think Anne picked a tree to describe herself? You'll get to explain that in question one. For your own story, think about a picture that fits your character. What in nature, or in your character's world, changes but stays itself?

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-4-wgrd

When you test a theme, the size of your sentence matters a lot. If it's too big, like "Life is hard," it fits every book ever written, so of course it passes the test, but it tells us nothing about this one. If it's too small, like a sentence about one thing Anne does, it's really an event, and it won't fit a second chapter at all. You want something in the middle. A good way to shrink a sentence that's too big is to add the word when or because. "Practice is boring" is too big and not even true. "Practice feels boring until the day it pays off" is something you could actually test. That one's mine, so build your own from Chapters 31 through 34.

## Before you read · g4ela-13-4-read

Queen's is in Charlottetown, the capital city of Prince Edward Island. It was too far for Anne to go back and forth every day, so she had to live in town while she studied. Think about that. Anne hasn't lived anywhere but Green Gables since she arrived as an eleven-year-old, and now she's leaving the people and places she loves. Feeling homesick is normal, even for someone who's excited. Also think back to Josie Pye. She's the girl who dared Anne to walk the ridgepole of the Barrys' roof, and Anne fell and broke her ankle. So when Josie shows up in this chapter, notice how Anne feels about seeing her.

## Before you write · g4ela-13-4-guide3

Question three asks you to look across four chapters, so you'll need to find your way around the book. The chapter titles can help. Chapter 31 is "Where the Brook and River Meet." Chapter 32 is "The Pass List Is Out." Chapter 33 is "The Hotel Concert," and today's is "A Queen's Girl." Flip back and skim the beginnings and endings of each one, because big moments often happen there. When you finish all three answers, read them out loud to yourself. That's the same thing you'll do with your story in Writer's Workshop. If a sentence of yours makes you stumble, a reader will probably stumble too.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-13-4-chapter

For the first question, the challenge asks whether Anne is right that she hasn't really changed. You might find two sides here. Think back to Chapter 31, where she grew taller and started using fewer big words. Does that prove she changed, or only the outside of her? A strong answer can agree with part of what Anne says and question another part, as long as you explain why. For question two, the scholarship is announced near the end of the chapter. Read that part slowly and notice what it would let Anne do next. Then compare it to what she hoped for at the start of the week. Has her dream gotten bigger?

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-4-workshop

Reading aloud catches things your eyes miss, because your eyes are used to skimming. Your ears hear every single word. Look at the checklist item about saying a word that isn't on the page. When that happens, your mouth isn't always wrong. Sometimes the word you said is actually better than the one you wrote. Give your dialogue its own read, too. Try saying each line the way your character would really say it. Real people use short sentences and words like "can't" and "won't." When you write what you're proud of, be specific. Instead of saying you like your story, name the exact moment you think works best, and say why.
