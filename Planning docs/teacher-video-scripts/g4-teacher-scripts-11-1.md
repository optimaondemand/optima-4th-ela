Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 21 · A New Departure in Flavorings. Guide this lesson: Marilla. Each script plays right after Marilla's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-11-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week eleven. Last week ended with Anne running out of the Haunted Wood and deciding she'd had enough of imagining scary things. This week, some new people come to Avonlea. Mr. Phillips leaves the school, and a new minister arrives with his wife, Mrs. Allan. Our big idea for the week is being seen, which means having someone notice who you really are, even on a day when things go wrong for you. Today you'll start by thinking about how a place shapes a story. Then you'll do your word work and read Chapter 21. In Writer's Workshop, you'll begin the middle of your own story, and you'll find out that the order you tell things in matters a lot.

## Welcome · g4ela-11-1-welcome

Take a second look at today's chapter title, "A New Departure in Flavorings." That's a pretty fancy title for a chapter about a cake. A departure is a leaving, like a train departing from the station. But it can also mean doing something different from the usual way. So the title is a little joke. Something about this cake is going to be very different from the usual, and not in a good way. Authors pick chapter titles on purpose, and this one is having fun with you before you've read a word. One more thing to watch for: one of today's word parts is hiding inside that title. See if you can find it once you get to Word Study.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-11-1-wgrd

Setting is also when a story happens, not only where. Anne lives more than a hundred years ago, and in a small village back then, the minister was one of the most important people in town. Having the minister and his wife come to tea was a very big deal. Families brought out their best dishes and their best cooking. So in this time and place, a cake is more than a cake. It's a way of showing respect. Think about last week, too, when one afternoon tea with Diana turned into trouble with Mrs. Barry. And when you write your own story, notice your setting. Sam put Mateo on a field full of boys, so every mistake has an audience.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-11-1-dol

The mistake in today's sentence has a name. It's called a run-on sentence, and it's one of the most common mistakes writers make, even grown-up ones. To find where one sentence should end, try this test. Look for a chunk that tells who did something and what they did, and could stand all by itself. When you find two of those chunks crammed together, they need to be separated. Now look back at the model sentence for one tricky thing. There's a period after "Mrs.," right in the middle of the sentence. That period doesn't end anything. It just shows "Mrs." is a short way of writing a longer word, so the sentence keeps going after it.

## Before the word work · g4ela-11-1-guide1

You might wonder why the page asks you to name the groups yourself instead of just telling you the rule. It's because a rule you figure out sticks in your head far better than a rule someone reads to you. Scientists work this way, too. They look at examples first and then figure out the pattern. So when you sort, slow down and ask what the cards in each group have in common. Today's grammar and spelling also connect to each other. When you write about something that already happened, you often add an ending to a word, and sometimes that ending changes the spelling. If you get stuck on the typing activity, use the hint before you guess.

## Morphology · g4ela-11-1-morphology

These two word parts help you with science words, and you'll see them a lot in middle school. An antiseptic is something a nurse puts on a cut to fight against germs. When you defrost the freezer, you're taking the frost away. And if your bike tire deflates, the air goes out and down. So when you meet a long, scary-looking word, check the front of it first. Sometimes one small chunk tells you which direction the meaning goes. Be careful, though. Not every word that starts with the letters d and e has this prefix. In a word like "desk," those letters are just part of the word. Cover the chunk and see if a real word or root is left behind.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-11-1-vocabulary

Notice that each of these sentences comes straight from the book, so the author has left you clues on purpose. One kind of clue is a word nearby that means almost the same thing, like "eager" sitting next to "vivacious" in the example. Another kind is what's happening in the scene, like someone balancing or wobbling. With "kindred," look for a smaller word hiding inside it. If you know what your kin are, you're already halfway there. Also look at the word "another" in that same sentence. It tells you Anne has found people like this before. Once you've matched them all, try using one of these words to describe someone in your own life.

## Grammar · g4ela-11-1-grammar

Once you've named your groups, watch for two things in your own writing. Many past-tense verbs end in -ed, but some of the most common ones don't follow that pattern at all. Think about a minister who came to tea. He didn't "comed." The same goes for ate and ran. Your ear usually knows these, so trust it. The other thing to watch is staying in the same time. If your story starts in the past, with Mateo tripped and Mateo fell, it should stay in the past. Switching back and forth between past and present is one of the most common slips in a draft, so check for it when you reread your scenes.

## Spelling · g4ela-11-1-spelling

After you've sorted and named your groups, try your rule on some words that aren't on the page. Check what your rule says should happen to "funny" when you make it "funnier," to "monkey" when there's more than one, and to "study" when you add -ing. If your rule works on words you've never sorted, you've really got it. This rule also matters for your grammar today. Lots of past-tense verbs end in a y before you add -ed, like "cry" and "worry." There are a few rule breakers, too. "Say" has a vowel before the y, so you'd expect it to keep the y, but its past tense is "said." English has a handful of those, and they just have to be memorized.

## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-11-1-copia

When you change the verb, think about how the new word changes the feeling. Girls who sniffled all the way home feel very different from girls who wailed all the way home, even though the rest of the sentence is the same. Pick the verb that fits how sad you think they really were. When you move a when or a where to the front, put a comma after it, before the main part of the sentence starts. And notice that starting with a when is exactly what you'll practice in Writer's Workshop today. Words like "after school" or "that afternoon" at the front of a sentence help the reader know where they are in time.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-11-1-guide2

Think about where Anne came from before Green Gables. In the homes she lived in, she was mostly useful. She helped with the work and looked after babies. People noticed what she could do, but not very many noticed who she was. That's why being seen matters so much to her. Notice something about the box, too. Being seen isn't the same as being praised. The person who truly sees you doesn't pretend your mistake didn't happen. They just don't let the mistake be the only thing they see. That's harder to do than it sounds. When you answer the question about your character, think about who in their life could do that for them.

## Before you read · g4ela-11-1-read

This chapter is funny, and part of what makes it funny is that the author lets you notice things before Anne does. Authors often plant a small detail early on that seems unimportant, and then it turns out to matter a lot later. As you read the part before the tea, keep an eye out for anything that seems a little off, even if Anne doesn't pay attention to it. If you spot it, you'll enjoy the rest of the chapter even more. At the second stop, think about what you know about Anne from the whole book so far. She's made lots of mistakes, but has she made the same one twice? That will help you decide whether her idea is a comfort or a worry.

## Before you write · g4ela-11-1-guide3

Before you start typing, read each question slowly and count how many parts it has. The first question asks two things, and so does the third. The most common reason a good answer loses points is that it answers only the first part and stops. A trick that helps is to look for the word "and" in the question. That often tells you there's a second part waiting. When you finish an answer, go back and check that you answered every part. And when you use someone's words from the book, put them in quotation marks and say who said them, just like the sentences we practiced last week with Marilla's words.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-11-1-chapter

Question one is really a chain of events, where one thing leads to another. The strongest answers tell the chain in order, using words like "because" and "so" to connect each link. If you skip a link, your reader won't see how the mistake happened. For the second question, Anne's own explanation is your best evidence. Use her words in quotation marks, then add a sentence of your own that explains why her words answer the question. For the third question, remember that it has two halves. Lots of readers write a great answer about Mrs. Allan and then forget to tell how Anne responds. Look at how Anne feels at the very end of that scene.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-11-1-workshop

Some transition words in the bank do a special job. "Suddenly" and "Just then" tell the reader something surprising is about to happen, so save one of them for your worst moment. If every scene starts with "suddenly," nothing feels sudden anymore. The same goes for "then." It's a fine word, but when every sentence starts with it, your story starts to sound like a list. Notice how Sam mixed his up. Also, think back to last week, when you named your kid's gift and the problem it causes. The middle scenes are where that problem grows. Each scene should feel a little harder for your character than the one before it.
