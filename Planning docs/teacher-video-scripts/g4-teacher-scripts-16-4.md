Number the Stars, Day 4 · Ch. 4, It Will Be a Long Night. Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each. A gold 🎬 On screen line is a suggestion for that moment: click a button in it and that card goes up on your video. It is never read aloud.

## Welcome · g4ela-16-4-welcome

Paper dolls and grumpy shoes. I have a feeling Kirsti and I would have argued about shoes too. Notice how fast this chapter changes mood. Keep track of the exact moment it stops feeling like a regular evening.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-16-4-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

Adding up the clues is a great way to finish the week. Make a quick list in your journal of every sign you've noticed since Chapter 1. Then read it from top to bottom. You might be surprised by what the list says all together.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-16-4-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Grete named the names that need capitals, so here's a twist. The word synagogue stays lowercase in this sentence, because it means any synagogue. But the Great Synagogue in Copenhagen, the one in today's Museum Case, gets capitals, because that's the name of one building.

Review day. You've sorted, hunted, and built, so you know these rules. Read each one twice, like Grete says. If you feel unsure, use the tricks from earlier this week. Read it as two words, and ask whose.

No misses at all? I believe in you. And the one-sentence challenge is tricky. If you can't fit all four, try two in one sentence and two in another. That still counts as a win.


## Grammar · g4ela-16-4-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

Grete's rule is the one to keep. Here's the word that trips people up most. When something belongs to it, like a city and its lights, there's no apostrophe. It's with an apostrophe always means it is or it has. Try both readings before you tap.


## Spelling · g4ela-16-4-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Last check of the week for our hiding vowel. Say the related word, then your spelling voice if you need it. If both agree, you can trust your answer.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-16-4-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 4]

Frightened and proud at the same time. That's a big feeling for a ten-year-old. Being trusted with something important is a kind of courage too. Think about a time a grown-up trusted you with something. How did it feel?


## Before you read · g4ela-16-4-read

[ON SCREEN: Stop 3]

Asking why the author put the funny part first is a great question. Here's a hint. Think about how a scary scene feels when it comes right after a laugh. As you read, notice what the grown-ups say, and also what they don't say.


## Before you write · g4ela-16-4-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Grete gave you the questions, so here's one tip. When a question asks how Annemarie's life is different, compare. Say what it was like at the start of the chapter, then what's true by the end.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-16-4-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

The difference between the polite word and the truth. That's a sharp way to put it. Look at how carefully Papa speaks. When grown-ups choose their words that slowly, it's usually because the truth is hard to say. Use the chapter to explain what Annemarie figures out.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-16-4-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Proof that anyone could find. That's the key. Write the chapter number next to each piece of evidence. That way, if someone doubts your reason, you can send them straight to the page.
