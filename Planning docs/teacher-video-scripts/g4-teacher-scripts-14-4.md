# Lesson 14.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 38 · The Bend in the Road. Guide this lesson: Anne. Each script plays right after Anne's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-14-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of our Anne of Green Gables unit, and today you read the last chapter of the book. It's been a big week for Anne. In Chapter 36 she won the Avery scholarship, and in Chapter 37 she lost Matthew. Now, in Chapter 38, "The Bend in the Road," she has to make a hard choice about her future. Your word work reviews helping verbs and suffix rules, and most of it asks you to catch mistakes. After you read, your thinking stretches across the whole novel, from the first chapters to the final page. And in Writer's Workshop, your story is finished. You'll read it out loud to someone and write about how you've grown as a writer.

## Welcome · g4ela-14-4-welcome

Do you remember how this book began? The first chapter isn't about Anne at all. It's about Mrs. Rachel Lynde, sitting by her window and wondering why Matthew Cuthbert is driving off in the middle of the afternoon. Nobody in Avonlea expected an orphan girl, and Mrs. Lynde thought taking one in was a terrible idea. Now look at the model sentence in today's Daily Oral Language. Those are Mrs. Lynde's words in the very last chapter, and this time she's bringing Anne good news. Authors often circle back to where a story started, so the ending feels a little like coming home. As you read, notice who else from the early chapters shows up again, and how they treat Anne now.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-4-dol

Look at who's doing the deciding in each sentence. In the model, it's the trustees, which is more than one person, so the helper is have. In the one you fix, it's Anne and Gilbert. Back in week ten, you learned that two names joined by and count as more than one, so they get have too, not has. Helpers also tell you when something happens. Have decided means it already happened. Will work means it hasn't happened yet. So this one little line holds the past and the future side by side, which is a lot like this whole chapter. When you write your reflection today, pay attention to your helpers. Has grown and will keep trying are good ones to borrow.

## Before the word work · g4ela-14-4-guide1

Every one of today's checking activities ends with a Show What You Know box, and each one asks you to name a rule. That gives you a good test to use before you tap anything. If a sentence or a word looks strange, stop and ask yourself which rule it breaks. If you can name the rule, go ahead and tap. If you can't, it might just be something you don't see very often, and unusual isn't the same thing as wrong. This is the same test you used while editing your story last week. A good editor doesn't change something because it feels funny. They change it because they can say exactly what's wrong with it.

## Morphology · g4ela-14-4-morphology

Since this is our last day with these roots, let's try a few that aren't on the cards. If you've ever called out an idea while someone else was talking, you interjected. You threw your words into the middle. A dejected person feels thrown down, which is a good way to describe being really disappointed. Back in Chapter 3, when Anne found out the Cuthberts had wanted a boy, she was about as dejected as a person can get. Even scribble comes from the writing root. It's just quick, messy writing. Now look at the two front pieces on the cards. Once you know what they mean, try sticking each one onto the other root and see what new word you can build.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-4-vocabulary

Two of this week's words make a good pair. Obstinate means stubborn, and Anne was obstinate about Gilbert for a very long time. She wouldn't forgive him for years after he called her Carrots. A consolation is comfort when you're disappointed. You might have heard of a consolation prize, the small prize someone gets for trying hard and not winning. If you choose the option about word parts, sanctifying is a good one to try. Its beginning, sanct, also shows up in sanctuary, which means a safe place that's set apart from everything else. A sanctuary can be a church, or a place where animals are protected. Knowing one of those words helps you figure out the other.

## Grammar · g4ela-14-4-grammar

Some verb forms can't stand on their own. Try this one, which isn't on the page: Diana coming over tomorrow. You know what's happening, and the word tomorrow even tells you when, but it still doesn't sound finished. It needs is in front of coming before it's a real sentence. Words like taken and seen work the same way. They always need a partner. But don't tap a sentence just because it has one of those words. Check whether a helper is already standing in front of it. The Show What You Know box asks what a phrase can't do without its helper, and that question fits this chapter. Chapter 38 is all about Anne looking back and looking ahead, and helpers are the words that keep the times straight.

## Spelling · g4ela-14-4-spelling

The Show What You Know box asks for something extra today. Besides naming the rule, you get to say how you'll remember it. Anne would be great at that part. On her very first ride to Green Gables, she gave a road full of blooming apple trees a brand new name, the White Way of Delight, so she'd never forget how it made her feel. You can give a spelling rule a picture, too. For bake and baking, you might imagine the e stepping out to make room for the ending, like someone giving up a seat on a bench. Make your picture a little silly, because silly things stick. Then test it on a word you write in your reflection today.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-4-guide2

The Growing-Up Watch box says the hardest and bravest part of growing up is choosing love over what you thought you wanted. Think about what Anne wanted at the start of this week. She studied all winter at Queen's, and in Chapter 36 she won the scholarship that would send her to college. That was the dream. Watch for the moment when love and that dream pull in different directions. Pay attention to how quickly Anne decides, and to whether she seems sad about it. Then look at the question at the bottom of the box about your own character. An unexpected turn like that doesn't have to be a sad thing. Sometimes it's the moment a person finds out what matters most.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-4-wgrd

When you name what changed in Anne, try to go deeper than the outside. She grew taller in Chapter 31, and that's true, but it's the easy kind of change. The changes worth writing about are on the inside, like how she handles being angry or how she treats someone who once hurt her. For the part that never changed, look for something Anne loved or cared about on her very first day that she still cares about at the gate. The page gives you a sentence to use: Anne used to blank, and now she blank. If you can fill in both blanks with something you could point to in the book, you've found a strong change.

## Before you read · g4ela-14-4-read

A little background will help with this chapter. In Anne's time, doctors could do much less for people's eyes than they can now. If an eye doctor told you to rest your eyes, it was serious, and you listened. Going to college was also a big deal back then, especially for a girl. Not many young women from a small farm town got that chance, so think about how much Anne is weighing. While you read, keep the Pause and Think question in mind, the one about the bend in your own road. You don't have to share your answer with anyone. Just let yourself think about it when you get to the last page of the book.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-4-guide3

Look at the hint under question three. It lists a few things to compare, like Anne's love of big words when she first arrived. Those are there to get you started, but your answer will be stronger if you add details you found yourself. For each of your two changes, you'll want a then and a now. Write what Anne was like early on and give one detail that shows it, and then do the same for the end of the book. These questions don't ask for a quotation today, so you can explain the details in your own words. Just make them exact enough that someone who has read the book would know which scene you mean.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-4-chapter

For question one, notice that there are two parts. A lot of answers explain what Anne gives up and forget to say what she plans instead, so check that you have both. The challenge asks why Anne says it isn't a sacrifice. Think about whether you agree with her. For question two, the challenge sends you back to Chapter 15 and the slate. Before you write, think about whether Gilbert ever tried to make peace with Anne between that day and this one. If he did, ask yourself why it didn't work then and why it works now. The scene you need is near the end of Chapter 38, so reread it slowly before you answer.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-4-workshop

Look at Sam's two reflections. The before version could have been written by anyone about any story. The after version could only have been written by Sam, because it names Mateo and the fear that got in his way. That's a good test for yours. When you finish reading aloud, ask your listener one question: which part do you remember most? Whatever they say tells you what worked, and that's a detail you can use in your reflection. Then look at the tracker at the top of the workshop. It has eight steps, from Voice all the way to Publish, and today you reach the last one. Anne grew up across thirty-eight chapters, and you grew as a writer across eight weeks.
