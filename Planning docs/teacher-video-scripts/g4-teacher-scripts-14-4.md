Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 38 · The Bend in the Road. Guide this lesson: Anne. Each script plays right after Anne's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-14-4

Hi, friends. This is it, the last chapter of Anne of Green Gables. Earlier this week Anne spent a winter at Queen's, won the Avery scholarship, and then lost Matthew. ## Welcome · g4ela-14-4-welcome

Anne says she might not judge herself fairly, and I like her even more for admitting it. The model sentence in today's Daily Oral Language comes from Mrs. Rachel Lynde. In the very first chapter, she sat at her window and decided taking in an orphan was a mistake. Now, in the last chapter, she's the one bringing Anne good news. Authors often circle back to where a story began, so watch for familiar faces today.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-4-wgrd

Anne would rather you decide, and that's a real compliment. She trusts your eyes more than her mirror. You already practiced this move at the start of the week with the frame on this page, Anne used to ___, and now she ___. Keep both blanks as things Anne actually does. The new part today is the half about what never changed. Give that one real thought, because it's often the more interesting answer.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-4-dol

I can just picture eleven-year-old Anne talking a mile a minute without stopping for breath. In the trustees' sentence, have decided tells you the deciding is already done. The sentence you're fixing has two halves at different times, one finished and one still ahead. You know from earlier this week what that usually means for where a sentence should stop. Read your version aloud and listen for the pause.

## Before the word work · g4ela-14-4-guide1

Anne owning up to all those years of being wrong about Gilbert might be the most grown-up thing she's said yet. Everything on this tab comes from the week you just had. The roots, the four words, the helping verbs, and the suffix rules all showed up in the first two lessons. If one activity feels shaky, think back to the reason behind that rule and use the reason as your check.

## Morphology · g4ela-14-4-morphology

Of course Anne adores word parts. Anyone who loves big words as much as she does would. You've met these roots in both earlier lessons this week, from the projector on the wall to a manuscript written by hand. If a match gives you trouble, look at the examples box, because each example word already shows its parts split apart. The writing root fits Anne's ending, too, since she's about to teach a classroom of her own.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-4-vocabulary

Anne wanting every option on the menu is so like her. I'm proud of her for settling on just one. Obstinate is the word we haven't talked much about. It means stubborn, and Anne was obstinate for years about forgiving Gilbert. If you choose the card about finding a word in Chapter 38, keep ambition in mind as you read, and notice what Anne decides to do with her big plans.

## Grammar · g4ela-14-4-grammar

Anne's ear catching what her daydreaming eyes missed made me smile, because plenty of us read that way. Find the main verb first, then look to its left for a helper. Keep in mind from earlier this week that a verb like has or had can stand alone as the main verb, so a short sentence can still be complete. When you fix one in Show What You Know, say what time the helper puts the action in.

## Spelling · g4ela-14-4-spelling

Anne as the teacher checking everyone's work! Her students are going to know their suffix rules, I promise you that. You already know the reasons behind these three rules, like the double letter that guards a short vowel sound. That reason matters today, because Show What You Know asks how you'll remember the rule. Saying why a rule works is usually the best memory trick there is.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-4-guide2

I like the way Anne talks about that turn. She couldn't stop it, but she decided what to do next, and that takes courage. At the start of the week, this box talked about bitterness and the grudge Anne held against Gilbert. Today's box brings him back one last time and mentions the Carrots day in Chapter 15. As you read, notice how far the two of them have come since then.

## Before you read · g4ela-14-4-read

Five years of waiting without knowing it! Anne sure knows how to make a reader curious. This is the final chapter, and Matthew's death still hangs over Green Gables, so read it gently. Two names to know. Redmond is a college, the next big step Anne had planned after Queen's. Carmody is a nearby town with its own school.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-4-guide3

Miss Stacy would be so pleased to hear Anne passing her advice along. Good teachers' words have a way of sticking. A then sentence and a now sentence, joined by a word like but or instead, makes a change easy to see. Since helping verbs have been our grammar all week, read your answers once just for verbs, and check that Anne's plans for the future sound like the future.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-4-chapter

Matthew finding Anne on that pile of shingles feels like a long time ago now. Each question has a challenge that pushes further. For the first, the challenge asks why Anne says there's no sacrifice, so look for her own reason in what she tells Marilla. For the second, put the slate day in Chapter 15 beside the gate. For the third, your thinking from What Good Readers Do is already a strong start.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-4-workshop

Specific over splendid. I might borrow that from Anne for my own classroom. Sam's after reflection works because he gives proof, a real example from his story about Mateo. When you reflect on your growth as a writer, look back at the rubric row you chose to improve this week. That's an easy place to find your example, because you know exactly what changed. And pick a listener who'll stay with you to the end.
