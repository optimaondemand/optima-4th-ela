# Lesson 13.3 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 33 · The Hotel Concert. Guide this lesson: Gilbert. Each script plays right after Gilbert's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what he says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-13-3

Hi, friends. We're in the middle of week thirteen. So far this week, Anne came home from a summer outdoors two inches taller, and then the pass list finally came, with Anne and Gilbert tied for first. Today, in Chapter 33, Anne gets up in front of a big crowd of strangers at a fancy hotel to recite, and for a moment she can't do it. In reading, you'll practice finding a big idea the book never says out loud. Your questions ask you to compare what different people think is valuable. And in Writer's Workshop, you'll keep editing your story, hunting for wrong words, misspellings, and sentences that run together. Keep an eye out for that last one, because it shows up in your warm-up, too.

## Welcome · g4ela-13-3-welcome

Have you ever had to speak in front of people and felt your mouth go dry or your knees go wobbly? That feeling has a name, stage fright, and it happens to grown-ups too, even people who perform for a living. Your body treats a room full of watching eyes almost like danger, so your heart speeds up and your mind can go blank. It doesn't mean you aren't ready. Anne knows her piece, and she's brave in lots of ways, but today her body gets scared anyway. As you read, pay attention to what helps her get past it. What gets a person started again is often something small.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-3-dol

Look closely at the model sentence. The joining verb there connects Anne to a naming word, victim, and not to a describing word. Linking verbs can do both jobs. They can tell what someone is like, as in Anne was nervous, or tell what someone is, as in Anne was a student. Now, a tip for the bottom sentence. When you're looking for where one sentence should end, look for the spot where a brand new subject shows up and starts its own action or feeling. That's usually your break. Hang on to that trick, because Sam uses it in Writer's Workshop today, and you'll need it for your own story.

## Before the word work · g4ela-13-3-guide1

Today's activities have hint buttons, and there's a smart way to use them. Make a real try first, even if you're not sure. A guess you thought about teaches your brain more than a hint you read right away. If you're still stuck, open the first hint. It nudges you without telling you. Then, after you check your work, read the little explanation of why, even on the ones you got right. Sometimes you get an answer right for the wrong reason, and that explanation is where you find out. Building is also where mistakes show up most, and that's fine. A wrong word you fixed yourself is one you're much more likely to remember on Friday than one you got lucky on.

## Morphology · g4ela-13-3-morphology

Sometimes a root hides because its spelling got squeezed. Take the word expect. It's really ex, which means out, plus spect. The s dropped away because the x already makes that sound. So to expect something is to look out for it, the way all of Avonlea was looking out for the pass list last chapter. Suspect has the root too. A suspect is someone people look at very closely, wondering what they did. One more thing makes roots different from the words you usually build with. A base word like build or look can stand all by itself. A root like spect or struct can't. It always needs a part stuck to it, and that's exactly why today's blanks ask you to add one.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-3-vocabulary

Since the bank has an extra word, use it as a final check. Once you've filled all three blanks, try the leftover word in each sentence. If it sounds just as good somewhere, think again about that blank. Two of the bank words have helpful little words inside. Broadminded is two words joined together. Broad means wide, so a broadminded person has room in their mind for lots of ideas. Tactful comes from tact, which means knowing the right thing to say so nobody gets hurt. Think of a friend who tells you there's spinach in your teeth quietly, instead of in front of everyone. That friend is being tactful.

## Grammar · g4ela-13-3-grammar

Linking verbs love to hide inside contractions. When you write she's nervous, the 's is really is, so there's a linking verb tucked in there. The same goes for I'm, you're, and they're. That's handy to know in Writer's Workshop today, because they're is one of the usage words you're checking. If you can stretch it out to they are, it's right. Watch out for one sneaky case, though. An apostrophe s can also mean something belongs to someone. In Gilbert's hat, nothing is linking. It's just his hat. When you do Show What You Know, think about what kind of description makes a short sentence stronger. A word that shows what's going on inside a person usually adds more than a word about how they look.

## Spelling · g4ela-13-3-spelling

One spelling case isn't on today's list, but it's everywhere. When a one-beat word already ends in a silent e, like brave or wide, you don't add a second e. You only add the r or the st, so you get braver and bravest, wider and widest. Two little words also tend to travel with these forms. A comparing word ending in er usually brings than along, as in, My little brother was braver than he felt. A word ending in est usually has the in front, as in, the widest river on the island. For Show What You Know, test your word before you build. Put it after was, like, The hotel was blank. If it fits and describes, you've got an adjective to work with.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-13-3-guide2

Think about how much Anne used to want things she didn't have. When she first came to Green Gables, she wished she had beautiful hair instead of red hair. For a long time she longed for a dress with puffed sleeves, until Matthew finally gave her one at Christmas. Wanting things like that is normal. Everybody does it, especially when they're young. So when you read about the diamonds and silks in today's chapter, remember the girl who wanted puffed sleeves so badly. Then compare her with the girl on the ride home. The Growing-Up Watch asks what your own character would say they're rich in. Start thinking about that now.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-3-wgrd

Fairy tales are a good place to practice this, because their big ideas sit close to the surface. In The Three Little Pigs, nobody ever says that hard work pays off. But the pig who took the time to build with bricks is the one whose house is still standing at the end. The story shows what it believes by who comes out all right. Novels do the same thing, only more quietly. In Chapter 33, nobody wins a prize or gets punished, so the reward may be smaller, like a moment that goes better than anyone expected. Keep an eye out for small rewards like that. If your first try comes out clumsy, that's normal. Keep trimming until it sounds true about real life.

## Before you read · g4ela-13-3-read

Picture what an evening out looked like in Anne's time. There was no TV and no radio, so people went to concerts to be entertained. Those weren't always music. Often, people recited poems and stories from memory, out loud, with lots of feeling. Anne has loved doing that for years. But this concert is at a fancy hotel where rich summer visitors stay, and the audience is full of strangers in expensive clothes. As you read, keep in mind that we only see Gilbert through Anne's eyes. She decides what his face means. When you reach the first stop, ask yourself how sure you can be that she's reading him right.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-13-3-rwm

Notice the word that starts Anne's second sentence. When she says, Why, we have sixteen years to our credit, she isn't asking a question. Long ago, people used why at the start of a sentence to show surprise, almost like saying, Well, of course! It makes Anne sound like her point is so obvious she can't believe anyone missed it. Then check how her sentence ends: more or less. That's Anne being funny and honest at once, admitting some people have bigger imaginations than others. In your mimic sentence, you could add a little ending like that. It makes your character sound like a real person talking.

## Before you write · g4ela-13-3-guide3

Look carefully at what each question asks for, because questions one and three each have two parts. Question one asks about Anne's dress and beads and also about the other women's things. Question three asks what Anne means and also how Jane sees it differently. A lot of answers only do half the job. When you're comparing two people, words like but, while, and unlike help a lot. For example, Jane thinks this, but Anne thinks that. If you want a challenge, each question has a Scholar extension. Try at least one. It's where your best thinking about this chapter will show up, and it's good practice for writing about any book.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-13-3-chapter

For question one, don't stop at Anne's first feeling about her clothes. Feelings can change during a chapter, so check how she feels at the start of the evening and then again at the end. For question two, think about why Gilbert, of all people, would matter so much. They've been rivals since the day she broke a slate over his head, and they just tied for first on the pass list. Make sure you copy your quote exactly, then explain it in your own words. For question three, notice that the word rich is in quotation marks. That's a clue Anne is using it in a new way.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-3-workshop

The usage mix-ups are easier to catch if you know a few tricks. There has the word here inside it, and both are about places. They're is short for they are, so read it the long way and see if it still makes sense. The same goes for you're, which means you are. Too with two o's has an extra letter, and it means more than enough, or also. For run-ons, try reading your sentence out loud. Notice where your voice wants to stop and drop down. That's often where a period belongs. Today's warm-up sentence had that same problem, so you've already practiced fixing one.
