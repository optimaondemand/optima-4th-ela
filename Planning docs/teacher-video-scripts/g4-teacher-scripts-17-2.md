## Welcome · g4ela-17-2-welcome

Speaking carefully when danger is near. Think about that while you read, because almost every grown-up in this chapter chooses their words on purpose. Here's a small moment I don't want you to miss. Right at the start, Mama and Papa talk about Lise, for the first time in three years. Why do you think a night like this one would finally open that door?


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-17-2-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

Choosing small is a skill grown-up writers use every day. Here's a test for your quote. Cover up everything except the words you picked. Do they still prove your point all by themselves? If they do, you chose well. If you need the whole paragraph to explain it, look again. Usually there's a shorter piece in there that does the job better.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-17-2-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence A · Show the fix]

Here's how to find where one sentence ends and the next begins. Read it out loud and listen for where your voice wants to stop and take a breath. Then check each half. Does it have someone doing something? Papa speaks in code on the phone. That's a complete thought. The soldiers may be listening to him. That's another one. Two complete thoughts need a period between them.


## Before the word work · g4ela-17-2-guide1

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule · Spelling rule]

Hunting for two things at once is like playing two games at the same time, so slow down. Here's a habit that helps. Read the sentence once looking only for the grammar, and then read it again looking only for the spelling. Two careful passes work better than one rushed one.


## Morphology · g4ela-17-2-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

The word about papers that prove who you are is credentials. Think about why papers were such a big deal in 1943. Soldiers could stop you on a street or on a train and ask who you were. Credentials make people believe you are who you say you are. That's why the believing root sits right inside the word. Watch for a word today that sounds like a body part, too. It's still about stretching.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-17-2-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: credible · intend]

Let's put two of the words to work. Is Papa's phone call credible? To anyone listening in, it sounds like an ordinary chat about fishing and cigarettes. And what does Papa intend? He intends for Uncle Henrik to understand what he really means. Try making one sentence of your own about this chapter that uses one of these words.


## Grammar · g4ela-17-2-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

Grete's question is the right one. Is it a little stand-in word, or a name? Here's a quick way to check. Pronouns are short and plain, like he, she, it, we and they. Names start with a capital letter in the middle of a sentence. If you see a capital letter, like Mama or Kirsti, it's a name, so skip it today.


## Spelling · g4ela-17-2-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Here's a way to tell if a word really is built. Take the extra piece off and see if a real word is left. Unpack, take off un, and pack is left. That's a real word, so it's built. But what about uncle? Take off un and you get cle. That isn't a word at all, so uncle only looks built.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-17-2-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 4]

Grete's mother buttering the bread and talking about the weather is such a real picture of quiet courage. Here's what to watch. Mama and Papa disagree about who should take the girls. Mama wins the argument, and her reason is smart. Find the part where she explains why going alone is safer. That reason tells you how carefully the grown-ups are thinking.


## Before you read · g4ela-17-2-read

When you reach the phone call, here's one more clue. Papa says Mama is bringing Uncle Henrik a carton of cigarettes. Annemarie knows something about cigarettes in Copenhagen that makes this strange. Read that part twice. Then, when the train stops at Klampenborg, slide the Then and Now on the Read tab. It shows the Deer Park Annemarie wishes they could visit, with a royal hunting lodge on the hill. Look for the deer.


## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-17-2-rwm

Grete's right that this sentence tucks Papa's words inside it. Let's look at it together.

[QUOTE ON SCREEN: press 6, read the line, press 6 again]

Here's the sentence from Chapter 6: "She listened while Papa spoke on the telephone to Uncle Henrik, telling him that Mama and the children were coming for a visit."

Notice the word telling. It lets the writer slip in what Papa said without stopping for quotation marks. And the sentence starts with she listened, so we stay with Annemarie the whole time. For your Mimic, think of a time you heard someone on the phone. Start with what you were doing, then use telling to share what they said.


## Before you write · g4ela-17-2-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

For the question about the code, try this. Pretend you are a soldier listening on the line. What would you hear? Now pretend you are Uncle Henrik. What would you understand? The gap between those two answers is the code. Use the phrase from the book in your answer, and put it inside quotation marks.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-17-2-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

The last question is a big one, so give it room. Ellen doesn't make a fuss about leaving her parents, but it costs her a lot. When you write, name what she has to leave behind. Then say what it shows about her courage. Remember, courage isn't only doing something scary. Sometimes it's going along quietly with something very hard.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-17-2-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Three confident sentences. I like that rule. Here's a way to build your hook. Drop the reader right into a moment, like the bedroom door opening. You might write, The door flies open, and a flashlight fills the room. Then your second sentence says who it is and what's happening. Your last sentence is your claim. If someone could argue with it, you've got a real claim.
