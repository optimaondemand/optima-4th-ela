## Welcome · g4ela-17-2-welcome

A strange phone call and a train going north. Grete knows how to make me want to turn the page. As you read, notice that almost every grown-up in this chapter chooses their words on purpose. There's also a small moment right at the start that's easy to miss. Mama and Papa talk about Lise, for the first time in three years. Why would a frightening night like the one they just had finally open that door? You don't need an answer yet. Just notice how the family talks to each other once the soldiers are gone.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-17-2-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

Choosing small. That's a skill grown-up writers use every single day. To test a quote you picked, cover up everything except the words you chose. Do they still prove your point all by themselves? If they do, you chose well. If you need a whole paragraph to explain them, look again. Usually there's a shorter piece in there that does the job better. One more tip. A grown-up's exact words are often stronger proof than a description of what they did, because you can hear the courage in how they say it. Listen for that today.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-17-2-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Two sentences run together, like cars with no space between them. I'll untangle a different one first, and you can follow along. Say it out loud, and listen for the spot where your voice wants to stop and take a breath. Then check each half. Does it name someone, and does it say what they're doing? If both halves can stand on their own, each one gets a capital letter at the start and a period at the end. And any name, like a person or a town, stands tall with a capital wherever it shows up. Now try your sentence the same way.


## Before the word work · g4ela-17-2-guide1

Two things at once. That's like patting your head and rubbing your tummy, so slow down. A habit that helps a lot is two passes. Read the paragraph once looking only for the grammar, and tap. Then read it again looking only for the spelling, and tap. Two careful passes work better than one rushed one. And if you're not sure about a word, say it in a short sentence of your own. Hearing it in a new place often makes it clear. When you're done, go back and check that you didn't skip the last line.


## Morphology · g4ela-17-2-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Papers that prove who you are. That one made me think about how different life was in 1943. Soldiers could stop you on a street or on a train and ask for your papers, and the papers had to make them believe you. Think about that when the word about papers shows up. Watch for another word today that sounds like a part of your body. It might surprise you which root it uses. When a word looks strange, read its meaning twice, then ask: is it about believing, or about stretching? Every right answer pushes the light a little farther.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-17-2-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: credible · intend]

Seeing if the words stuck is the best way to find out what you know. Before you match anything, cover the meanings with your hand and say what you think each word means. Then uncover them and check yourself. Try it with credible. Think about Papa on the phone, sounding so ordinary. Would someone listening in believe every word? Now try intend. What does Papa plan for Uncle Henrik to understand? Using a word to talk about the story is a great way to make it yours. Then finish the match and see how many you knew by heart.


## Grammar · g4ela-17-2-grammar

Is it a stand-in word, or a name? That question will save you from a lot of mistakes today. Stand-in words are short and plain, like I, you and we. Names usually start with a capital letter, even in the middle of a sentence, like Mama, Papa and Kirsti. If you see a capital letter in the middle, it's probably a name, so skip it. Once you find a stand-in word, look right next to it for the action word that goes with it. Then tap both. Tapping only one of the pair doesn't count as finding it.


## Spelling · g4ela-17-2-spelling

A short trip on the train. I'm glad we're hunting there. Some words look built but aren't, so check each one. Take unpack. Remove the front piece, and pack is still a real word, so unpack is built. Now try uncle. Remove those same first letters, and the three letters left over don't make a word at all. So uncle only looks built. Run that same test on each word before you tap it. If a real word is hiding underneath, tap it. If nothing real is left, leave it alone, even if it starts or ends the way built words often do.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-17-2-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 4]

Buttering the bread and talking about the weather. What a real picture of quiet courage. Watch for a moment when Mama and Papa disagree about who should take the girls north. Mama wins, and her reason is very smart. Find the part where she explains why going alone would be safer. Her reason tells you how hard the grown-ups are thinking about who might be watching them. Ask yourself whether Mama sounds afraid when she says it. Then decide whether her calm is a kind of courage, the way Grete's mother's calm was. Write your answer in a single line in your journal.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-17-2-rwm

Tucking someone's words inside your own sentence. Let's look at exactly how Lois Lowry does it.

[QUOTE ON SCREEN: press 6, read the line, press 6 again]

The sentence comes from Chapter 6: "She listened while Papa spoke on the telephone to Uncle Henrik, telling him that Mama and the children were coming for a visit."

Notice the word telling. It lets the writer slip in what Papa said without stopping for quotation marks. And the sentence starts with she listened, so we stay with Annemarie the whole time. For your Mimic, think of a time you heard someone on the phone. Start with what you were doing, then use telling to share what they said.


## Before you write · g4ela-17-2-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

The family's plan, the strange way Papa talks, and what Ellen leaves behind. Grete lined those up perfectly. For the question about the code, try this. Imagine you are a soldier listening in. What would you hear? Now imagine you are Uncle Henrik. What would you understand? The gap between those two answers is the code. Use the exact phrase from the book in your answer, and put it inside quotation marks. Then explain, in your own words, why Papa couldn't just say what he meant. Keep it to two or three sentences, and make every one count.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-17-2-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

What it costs a ten-year-old to say goodbye. That's a heavy question, so give it room. Ellen doesn't make a fuss about leaving her parents, but it costs her a lot. When you write, name what she has to leave behind. Then say what it shows about her courage, and about her family's courage, too. Remember, courage isn't only doing something scary. Sometimes it's going along without a word with something very hard, without knowing how it will turn out. Find one detail from the chapter that shows how Ellen feels, and use it. Then reread your answer once before moving on.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-17-2-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Three confident sentences beat a long one that wanders. I agree completely. To build your hook, drop the reader right into a moment from the book, like a knock in the middle of the night. Your second sentence tells who it is and what's happening, in a few words. Your last sentence is your claim. Read your claim and ask, could someone disagree with this? If they could, you have a real claim. If nobody could argue, it's just a fact, so add what you think it shows about courage. Keep the whole paragraph short and strong.
