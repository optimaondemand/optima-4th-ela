# Lesson 18.3 teacher video scripts

Number the Stars, Day 3 · Ch. 11, Will We See You Again Soon, Peter? Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 40 seconds each. A gold 🎬 On screen line is a suggestion for that moment: click a button in it and that card goes up on your video. It is never read aloud.

## Lesson video · g4ela-18-3

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Hi, friends. This chapter is quiet, and it's full of goodbyes. The church beside me is in Gilleleje, a fishing town on the coast north of Copenhagen. In October 1943, Jewish families hid in places like its loft while they waited for boats to Sweden. On the Read tab, the Museum Case holds a pair of Sabbath candlesticks, the kind Mrs. Rosen lit to welcome the Sabbath. Look at them before you read. In Writer's Workshop, each paragraph gets a strong last sentence.


## Welcome · g4ela-18-3-welcome

An open casket with no one inside. What a strange relief that is. Grete's question about cost is a good one to carry. A brave plan can work and still take something away from people. As you read, watch the Rosens' faces and hands, not just what they do. Lowry shows you a lot through small things, like a jacket that doesn't fit.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-18-3-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

A reader who calls everything a symbol. I've been that reader! Try it with your own backpack. A pencil is just a pencil. But a keychain a friend gave you on her last day before she moved away? That one carries a feeling. In this chapter, most of the coats are just coats. Save your tally marks for the things somebody guards, misses or remembers.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-18-3-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Two thoughts crowded into one line. That happens in my own notes all the time. I'll fix one first: leo hasnt packed his bag the bus leaves soon. Leo gets a capital. Hasn't gets an apostrophe where the missing letter was. Then I stop after bag, because the first thought is finished, and The starts fresh with a capital. Now try yours, and find the spot where one thought ends.


## Before the word work · g4ela-18-3-guide1

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule · Spelling rule]

Moving from finding to choosing. That's the fun part. One quick test for each. In grammar, read your choice out loud and ask whether it sounds like you chatting with a friend. In spelling, put one hand under your chin and say the word slowly. Each time your chin drops, that's a new beat.


## Morphology · g4ela-18-3-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Darker under the trees. I like that the path gets harder right in the middle, just like the chapter. Three spellings sound almost alike in this game, so your ears can trick you. Before you choose, say the whole word slowly and picture where you've seen it written, maybe on a sign or in a book. Your eyes often remember what your ears forget.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-18-3-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: proceed · concede · access · symbol]

Owning a word, not just recognizing it. That's a high bar, and a fair one. If a sentence about Chapter 11 won't come, start smaller. Use the word about something at school first, then try again with Peter or the Rosens. The second sentence usually comes easier after you've heard yourself say the word out loud.


## Grammar · g4ela-18-3-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

A person talking or a notice on a door. What a clever way to hear it. Picture the hallway at school. A sign on the wall says, Students are not allowed to run. Your friend grabs your arm and whispers, We aren't allowed to run! Same idea, two voices. Before you fill each blank, ask who is speaking, and pick the form that fits that voice.


## Spelling · g4ela-18-3-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Ears as the best spelling check. I agree with that. Watch for one trap. A word like puzzle has a double letter in the middle, and the split goes right between the two z's, so each beat gets one. If your split leaves the last beat with no consonant in front, try again. That last beat always keeps one.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-18-3-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 3]

A small brass key, kept for years. That one stays with me. Think about what you would grab if you had one minute to leave and could carry only one thing. Most people don't choose the most expensive thing. They choose the thing with a story in it. As you read, notice what the Rosens leave behind, and what they still carry with empty hands.


## Before you read · g4ela-18-3-read

[ON SCREEN: Stop 2]

A tiny passenger who needs something warm. Kirsti might be surprised where one of her things ends up. Listen, too, for what Peter calls Mama in this chapter. It's a small change, and Annemarie notices it right away. It tells you how grown-up Peter has had to become. Stop at each pause and jot one quick note before you keep going.


## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-18-3-rwm

Feeling a sentence slow down. Once you notice it, you can't stop noticing it.

[QUOTE ON SCREEN: press 6, read the line, press 6 again]

This one is from Chapter 11: "But their shoulders were as straight as they had been in the past: in the classroom, on the stage, at the Sabbath table."

Notice the three places after the colon. Each one is a small scene from their life before this night. For your Mimic, pick a friend or someone you love, and choose three places where you've seen them stand tall.


## Before you write · g4ela-18-3-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

Three questions and a packet nobody opens. That's a lot to hold. The candlestick question asks what they stand for, so go past what they are. Look back at the Museum Case and at the moment Annemarie remembers Mrs. Rosen lighting them. Then write one sentence about what they are, and one about what they mean to the family.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-18-3-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Only half an answer. That's a perfect way to put it. Read Peter's whole reply out loud, including the last part. Then think about a time someone said goodbye to you, maybe a friend moving away, and never quite said what they were feeling. Use that thinking to explain what Peter's words tell you, and quote his exact words.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-18-3-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

A knot at the end of the thread. I'm keeping that picture. A quick test for each closing sentence: cover the rest of the paragraph with your hand and read only the last line. Does it remind your reader of your claim on its own? If it could end any essay at all, add one word straight from your claim. Try it on both body paragraphs.
