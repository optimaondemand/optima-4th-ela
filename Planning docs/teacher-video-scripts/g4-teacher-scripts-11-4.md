# Lesson 11.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 25 · Matthew Insists on Puffed Sleeves. Guide this lesson: Matthew. Each script plays right after Matthew's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what he says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-11-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week eleven, and Chapter 25 is one of my favorite chapters in the whole book. All week we've been watching grown-ups who really see Anne. Mrs. Allan saw what Anne meant when the cake went wrong, and Miss Stacy got her name right without being asked. Today it's Matthew's turn, and he's the person you'd least expect. He notices something about Anne that nobody else has, and he decides to do something about it, even though it means talking to a stranger. First you'll finish the week's word work with a review. After you read, you'll write about which adult has truly seen Anne, and then you'll write the very last scene of your story's first draft.

## Welcome · g4ela-11-4-welcome

Think all the way back to Chapter 11, when Marilla made Anne her first dresses at Green Gables. They were plain and sensible, and Anne wished so hard for puffed sleeves. That was a long time ago in the story. Today's chapter picks that little wish back up. Authors do this a lot. They plant something small early on and let you forget it, and then much later it comes back and matters. When that happens, it feels like a reward for paying attention. As you go through today, watch how one quiet wish from fourteen chapters ago becomes the heart of a whole chapter. Then think about whether your own story has a small wish that could come back at the end.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-11-4-dol

Notice who's watching in this sentence. Matthew doesn't say a word, but one word, happily, tells you exactly how he feels. That's a lot of work for one word to do, and it fits Matthew, who shows his feelings much more than he talks about them. When you fix the sentence, you might wonder if the second part could start with "he" instead of his name. Here it could, because Matthew is the only he in the sentence. In your own story, be careful with that. If two boys are in a scene and you write "he," your reader may not know which one you mean. Check your story today for any he or she that could belong to two people, and use the name there instead.

## Before the word work · g4ela-11-4-guide1

All four of today's activities look back over the whole week, so this is a good moment to notice what has stuck and what hasn't. Pay attention to the Show What You Know box under the grammar and the spelling. It asks you to fix one item and name the rule. That part matters even more than the tapping. Anyone can get lucky with a tap, but when you can explain why a word or a sentence is wrong, you really know it. Try saying the rule in your own words, as if you were telling a friend. And keep these rules handy. Very soon your Writer's Workshop moves to Revise, and you'll be checking your own story for the same kinds of mistakes.

## Morphology · g4ela-11-4-morphology

Some words look like they have these prefixes but don't. An anteater is an animal that eats ants, so that word starts with ant, not with the prefix that means against. A desk and a dentist start with d and e, too, but if you take those letters off, what's left doesn't mean anything. So before you decide a word has a prefix, check that the rest of it still carries meaning. I'll give you one more fun connection. Your descendants are your children and grandchildren, the people who come after you in your family. They come down from you, the same way you walk down a hill. Once you know a word part, you'll start spotting it in places you'd never expect.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-11-4-vocabulary

Two of this week's words carry a little history. Kindred comes from kin, an old word for family. When Anne talks about kindred spirits, she means people who feel like family on the inside, even though they aren't related. And think about Anne walking the ridgepole of the Barry roof in Chapter 23. That's about as precarious as it gets. Now watch out for one mix-up. Exquisite doesn't just mean really good. It means beautiful in a delicate, careful way, like fine lace or a single snowflake. You probably wouldn't call a pizza exquisite, even a great one. Whichever choice you make, test your sentence by asking whether the word fits the exact feeling you mean.

## Grammar · g4ela-11-4-grammar

Many past-tense verbs end in e-d, like walked and nursed, but plenty don't. Matthew drove to the store. He didn't drived. Verbs that change their spelling instead of adding e-d are called irregular, and your ear usually knows them better than any rule does. Future tense has its own trick. Will always goes with the plain form of the verb, so you'd say will run, never will ran. One more thing to watch for in your own writing. In a story, you won't always have a word like yesterday to help you. Once you start telling your story in the past, stay there. Sliding back and forth by accident is one of the most common mistakes young writers make, and it's an easy fix when you reread.

## Spelling · g4ela-11-4-spelling

Some words do this week's rule in reverse. Words that end in i-e, like lie and tie, change those two letters to a y before you add -ing. So you write lying and tying. You won't find "lieing" in any dictionary. Knowing that backward case helps you see the whole pattern from the other side. Something else is worth knowing for your own writing. A spell checker on a computer catches a lot of mistakes, but not all of them. If a misspelling happens to make a different real word, it slides right past. If you meant tied and typed tide, the computer won't blink. That's why the careful looking you're doing today still matters, even with a computer right in front of you.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-11-4-guide2

This whole week has been about grown-ups who truly see Anne, and each one has seen something different. Mrs. Allan saw what Anne meant to do, even when the cake went wrong. Miss Stacy got Anne's name right without being told. Today, watch for what Matthew sees. Being seen is different from being noticed. Anne gets noticed all the time, usually when something goes wrong, like the day she fell off the roof. Being seen means someone understands what's going on inside you, what you hope for when nobody's asking. As you read, ask what Matthew understands about Anne that the people around her have missed. Then think about the question in the box. The person who sees your character might be a quiet one, too.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-11-4-wgrd

Try moving this chapter into your own time and place. If Matthew lived today, he could order a dress online and never talk to a single person. Would this chapter even work? The whole brave act depends on Matthew having to walk into a store and speak to someone face to face. Now think about the village itself. In a small place like Avonlea, everybody knows Matthew, so there's no hiding if he makes a fool of himself. But a small village also means he knows exactly who could help him. When you get near the end of the chapter, notice who he turns to. That's the setting making things harder and easier in the very same chapter, and it's worth writing down when you look back at the week.

## Before you read · g4ela-11-4-read

Think back to the very first chapters of the book. Matthew was so shy that the only women he felt comfortable around were Marilla and Mrs. Lynde. When he found a little girl waiting at the train station instead of a boy, he hardly knew what to say to her. Keep that in mind today, because it tells you how big this errand is for him. As you read, notice that the author lets you laugh at Matthew in the store and care about him at the same time. A writer can make a scene funny without making the character a joke. Pay attention to how she does that. It's a hard trick to pull off, and you might want it for your own story someday.

## Before you write · g4ela-11-4-guide3

Writing about Matthew is a little different from writing about Anne. Anne tells you everything she feels, usually in a lot of words. Matthew hardly says anything, so your evidence will mostly come from what he does. That's a great reading habit to build. When a character stays quiet, look at their actions and ask what each one shows about how they feel. Be careful with the funny parts. It's easy to spend your whole answer retelling the store scene because it's so fun to describe, but a retold scene doesn't explain anything on its own. After each detail you choose, add a sentence that says what it shows about Matthew. Try using a strong trait word, too, maybe even one of this week's vocabulary words.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-11-4-chapter

Each of these questions has two parts, and the second part is where the real thinking happens. In question one, describing the dresses is the easy half. The harder half is what Matthew decides, and why a man as shy as he is would decide it. In question two, don't stop at saying Matthew was nervous. Think about what it shows that he kept going. For question three, whichever adult you pick, go back and look at that chapter instead of working from memory. The part most people leave out is how Anne changed. Look for a moment after that adult sees her. Does she act differently, or say something new about herself? That change is your strongest evidence.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-11-4-workshop

Look at the box that says Anne did it too. Anne wanted puffed sleeves back in Chapter 11, and the story didn't forget. Your ending works the same way. It doesn't need to be pretty today, but it should answer the want you gave your kid back in Scene 1. Did they get it, or did they get something different? Either one can make a good ending. Sam's ending is rough, but it still answers his want, because Mateo had wanted to make that team since he was seven. Later, when you revise, you'll make your ending as strong as your middle. When you write your sentences in Now You Try, be honest about whether the trouble really grew from scene to scene.
