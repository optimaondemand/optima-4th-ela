# Lesson 12.3 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 29 · An Epoch in Anne's Life. Guide this lesson: Miss Barry. Each script plays right after Miss Barry's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-12-3

Hi, friends. We're in the middle of week twelve, and it's been quite a week for Anne. First her hair turned green, and then her boat started sinking in the Lily Maid chapter. Today, in Chapter 29, Anne and Diana get a trip to Charlottetown to stay with Diana's Aunt Josephine, the same Miss Barry they once landed on in the middle of the night. The whole week has been about learning from mistakes, and today Anne gets to notice that she's actually growing. In your word work, you'll build every answer yourself. After you read, you'll study one sentence about Anne and some cows. Then in your story, you'll trade a few weak words and moments for stronger, truer ones.

## Welcome · g4ela-12-3-welcome

Think about how long Anne has been hoping for grand things. On her very first day at Green Gables, she asked Marilla to call her Cordelia because it sounded so elegant. At Christmas, she was over the moon about a dress with puffed sleeves from Matthew. Anne has always pictured a fancier, more beautiful life than the one she had. In this chapter, she finally gets a real taste of it, with a whole week in the city. As you read, keep checking whether the real thing turns out the way she imagined. Sometimes getting what you dreamed about teaches you something you didn't expect.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-3-dol

The model sentence is long, but it's still only one sentence. Miss Barry is the one doing everything, and the word "and" lets her do two things in a row. The sentence you're fixing has two people doing two different things, and that's the clue. Each complete thought has its own who and its own action. When you see a new who show up, like a "she" or a "they," check whether a new sentence is starting. And in case you're wondering, "prima donna" is Italian. It means "first lady," and it's what people called the star singer of an opera. Also notice "saw" and "felt." Those are irregular past-tense verbs, and you'll meet more of them in grammar today.

## Before the word work · g4ela-12-3-guide1

Building a word from memory is harder than picking it from a list, and that's exactly why it's good practice. When you write your own story, nobody hands you a word bank. You have to pull the spelling and the right form out of your own head. So if one of these feels hard, that's your brain doing real work. If Check My Work says something isn't right, don't just change letters until it turns green. Each question has a hint, so read it, think about the rule, and then try again. Also look closely at your spelling Show What You Know today. That's the answer that gets gathered into your assignment as your Word Study Connection.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-3-morphology

Last week's prefixes always sat at the front of a word. These roots are different. They can show up at the front, in the middle, or at the end, so you have to look all the way through a word to find them. A cat can retract its claws, which means it pulls them back in. When you make a muscle, it contracts, pulling tight together. In math, a protractor is the tool you use to measure and draw angles, and its name comes from this same root. On the carry side, an artist keeps drawings in a portfolio, a flat case for carrying papers. Once you spot the root, the letters around it are clues about which direction the carrying or pulling goes.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-3-vocabulary

One thing that helps with fill-in-the-blank sentences is noticing what kind of word the blank needs. The end of a word often tells you its job. Words ending in -ly, like "quickly" or "carefully," usually tell how something is done. Words ending in -ity or -ness, like "kindness," usually name a thing or an idea. So look at the words around the blank and ask what job that spot is asking for. Then check the meaning, too, because the right kind of word still has to make sense. When you finish, take the word you didn't use and try it in a sentence about yourself. Using every word is how they stick.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-3-grammar

When you're not sure of a past form, a dictionary can settle it. Look up an irregular verb, and right after the word you'll usually find its past form listed. Regular verbs don't get that, because everyone already knows they just add -ed. Be careful about trusting rhymes, too. Sleep becomes slept and keep becomes kept, so you might guess that beep becomes bept. It doesn't. It's beeped. Two verbs can sound alike and still follow different patterns. For your own two sentences, pick verbs that show what someone actually did in Chapter 29. Try to choose two that change in different ways, so you have something different to name for each one in the box.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-3-spelling

The trickiest part today is knowing when this rule even applies. Take love and add -able. You get lovable, and the e is gone, because love doesn't end in a c or a g, so there's no soft sound for the e to protect. Most other words with a quiet e follow the usual habit and drop it before a vowel. One of my favorite examples of the soft-sound rule is gorgeous, a word Anne would adore. That second g stays soft only because the e is still standing there, right before the o. When you look through Chapter 29 for your Show What You Know, words that end in -nce or -nge are good ones to try, because those always have a soft sound at the end.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-3-guide2

Look at Anne's moment in the Growing-Up Watch box. To see why it matters, think back to Josie Pye. Earlier in the book, Josie dared Anne to walk along the top of the Barrys' kitchen roof, and Anne fell and hurt her ankle. These two have never been friends. So when Anne says she's glad Josie won a prize, that's real growth. And there's a second step in what she says. She notices her own feeling and decides it means she's improving. That's a grown-up kind of thinking. Learning from mistakes isn't only fixing what went wrong. It's also catching yourself doing better and recognizing it.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-3-wgrd

A summary and a retelling aren't the same thing. A retelling marches through everything in order, every horse and every balloon. A summary picks the moment that matters and shows how the rest connects to it. One test you can use is to finish this sentence: because this happened, Anne now... If a moment can't finish that sentence, it's probably a detail, not the turning point. Watch out for one trap. The loudest or most exciting moment in a chapter isn't always the one it turns on. Sometimes the turning point is quiet, like a thought someone has alone. The chapter title is a clue, too. Authors sometimes name the turning point right at the top.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-3-read

You might not know what an exhibition is. An exhibition in Anne's time was a lot like a county or state fair today. Farm families brought their best animals, fruit, sewing, and baking to be judged, and there were races and shows to watch. Charlottetown is the biggest town on Prince Edward Island, so for two girls from little Avonlea, this trip is a very big deal. Also notice how a lot of the trip reaches you. Anne tells much of it to Marilla after she gets home, in her own words. So you're seeing the city through Anne's eyes, with all her excitement. As you read, ask yourself whether someone else might describe the same trip a little differently.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-12-3-rwm

Look at the word "placidly." It means calmly, without any fuss. The cows are as calm as can be, and inside Anne's head is a poem about a battle. That pull between the calm outside and the busy inside is what makes this sentence fun to read. Anne is doing a plain chore, but her mind is somewhere far more exciting. When you write your Mimic sentence, try to choose details that pull against each other in the same way. Maybe a character is folding laundry very slowly while their mind is racing about a big game tomorrow. That contrast tells a reader a lot about who a person is.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-3-guide3

Today's Writer's Workshop has a line that works for your chapter answers, too: small and true beats big and vague. If a question asks for two things Anne and Diana do, saying they had fun in the city is big and vague. A specific thing, with a place or a detail a reader could picture, is much stronger. If you take the extra challenge on the first question, you'll also need to say which of your two things Anne seems to treasure more, and how you can tell. Before you submit, look at the checklist at the bottom. It includes your three Read Like a Writer answers from the Reading tab and your spelling answer from Word Study, so make sure those are finished, too.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-3-chapter

For question one, the word "specific" matters. "They had fun in town" isn't specific. A specific answer names something a reader could find in the chapter. And the extra challenge asks which one Anne treasures more, so look at how much she says about each and how she says it. Question two has two parts, so make sure you explain why the concert moves her and also how it changes the way she thinks about being good. The hint under it points you to the exact line. For question three, a decision means comparing two things, so a strong answer names both the city and Green Gables before it tells what Anne chose.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-3-workshop

This is the third revision tool this week. You've added, you've removed and moved, and today you swap. One good place to look for a strong verb is your own reading. Remember the sentence you studied on the Reading tab? The author didn't write that the cows walked down the lane. She wrote that they "swung," and you can almost see them moving. A strong verb doesn't have to be fancy or long, either. "Slammed" is a short, ordinary word that does a lot. Watch out for one trap. Don't swap every verb in your story, and don't grab the biggest word you can find. Make one swap where the moment really matters, then read the sentence out loud to hear the difference.
