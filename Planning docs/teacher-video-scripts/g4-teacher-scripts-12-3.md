# Lesson 12.3 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 29 · An Epoch in Anne's Life. Guide this lesson: Miss Barry. Each script plays right after Miss Barry's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-12-3

Hi, friends. This is Day 3 of week twelve, and you already know our big idea is learning from mistakes. In Chapter 29, Anne and Diana go to Charlottetown to stay with Miss Barry, the same aunt whose bed they once jumped on in the dark. Your word work uses the skills from earlier in the week, but today you build every answer yourself. Then you'll read, answer three questions, and make three swaps in your story.

## Welcome · g4ela-12-3-welcome

Two extra girls is a lot for Miss Barry, but I suspect she loved every minute of it. Anne has wanted a grander, fancier life ever since she asked Marilla to call her Cordelia. Now she finally gets a real taste of one in the city. As you read, keep checking whether the real thing matches what she pictured for so long.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-3-dol

Miss Barry doesn't mind being the star of a sentence one bit. You fixed a run-on last lesson, so this one should feel familiar. The clue is a new who showing up partway through, like a "she," with an action of its own. That's usually where one thought ends and the next begins. And "prima donna" is Italian for the star singer of an opera.

## Before the word work · g4ela-12-3-guide1

A slate with nobody to help sounds hard. I'm glad you have Check My Work instead. You already noticed and sorted, then hunted for examples, and now the words come out of your own head. If Check My Work says something's off, read the hint and think about the rule before you try again. Your spelling Show What You Know gets gathered into your assignment, so take your time.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-3-morphology

Two choices is a friendly number when you're stuck, so Miss Barry has a point. You've met port and tract twice already this week, so these roots should feel like old friends. If you get stuck, use the check from the start of the week. Think of a word you know well, like transport or attract, and see if its meaning fits the definition next to the blank.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-3-vocabulary

Miss Barry has never been shy about calling silly things silly, and her ear test is a good one. You've seen these four words all week. Today the job flips. Instead of matching a word to its meaning, you're choosing the word a sentence needs. Every sentence is about something from this week's chapters, so the story itself is a clue.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-3-grammar

Miss Barry wants good sentences, and I'd guess she has strong opinions about what counts as good. You've noticed these verbs and hunted for them, and today you make them yourself. Some change just a vowel, and some turn into a whole new word. When you write about Chapter 29, try to pick verbs that change in different ways, so you have two different changes to name.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-3-spelling

I'm sure Miss Barry could spot a careless spelling from across the room. You already know why that quiet e sticks around. It's guarding the soft sound of the c or g. For your Show What You Know, words in Chapter 29 that end in -nce or -nge are good ones to try. Explain what the e does to the sound, like you practiced last time.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-3-guide2

I love that Miss Barry owns up to changing her mind. That isn't easy for anyone. You've watched Anne catch herself all week. Back in the first lesson, she said something unkind about Josie Pye in the very breath she promised not to. Now read Anne's moment in the Growing-Up Watch box. She's glad Josie won a prize, and she sees that gladness as proof she's improving. Small changes like that still count.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-3-wgrd

Miss Barry won't tell us which moment she'd pick, so you'll have to decide for yourself. Earlier this week you summarized with a frame: somebody wanted, but, so, then. Today adds the turning point, the moment your whole summary hangs on. Keep in mind that a turning point can be quiet, like a thought someone has alone in bed. And take another look at the chapter title. The box tells you what an epoch is.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-3-read

I'd have laughed too, Miss Barry. Anne's opinions are always worth hearing. The Exhibition in this chapter was a lot like a county or state fair today, with prize animals, baking, and horse races. Much of the trip reaches you through Anne telling Marilla about it afterward, so you're seeing the city through her excited eyes.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-12-3-rwm

Miss Barry not keeping cows made me smile. I can't picture her in a pasture at all. Last lesson, one verb showed you how fast Gilbert moved. Look for that same kind of word here. "Swung" shows how the cows move, and "placidly" means calmly. What makes the sentence fun is the gap between that calm lane and the poem racing through Anne's head. Try to build that same gap into your Mimic sentence.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-3-guide3

I believe Miss Barry about the decorations, since she's read plenty of Avonlea stories. Last lesson you practiced spotting questions that ask two things, so keep that habit going. Before you submit, look at the checklist at the bottom. It includes your three Read Like a Writer answers and your spelling response, so make sure both of those are finished.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-3-chapter

Ice cream as a cure for big feelings is very Miss Barry, and honestly not a bad idea. Same habit as last lesson: count the parts in each question. For question one, "specific" means something a reader could find on the page, so "they had fun" won't do. For question three, a decision means comparing, so a strong answer names both places before it tells what Anne concluded.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-3-workshop

Miss Barry still sounds a little pleased about that kiss, and I don't blame her. This is your third revision tool this week. You added, then you cut and moved, and today you swap. For your verb, look back at the Read Like a Writer sentence. Montgomery wrote that the cows "swung," and you can see them move. Sam's "slammed" shows that a strong verb can be short and ordinary. Make one swap where the moment really matters.
