# Lesson 12.3 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 29 · An Epoch in Anne's Life. Guide this lesson: Miss Barry. Each script plays right after Miss Barry's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-12-3

Hi, friends. This is Day 3 of week twelve, and you already know our big idea is learning from mistakes. In Chapter 29, Anne and Diana go to Charlottetown to stay with Miss Barry, the same aunt whose bed they once jumped on in the dark. Your word work uses the skills from earlier in the week, but today you build every answer yourself. Then you'll read, answer three questions, and make three swaps in your story.

## Welcome · g4ela-12-3-welcome

Anne has wanted a grander, fancier life ever since she asked Marilla to call her Cordelia. This week she finally gets a real taste of one, with a whole visit in the city. As you read, keep checking whether the real thing matches what she pictured for so long. Sometimes getting the thing you wished for teaches you something you never expected.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-3-dol

You fixed a run-on last lesson, so this one should feel familiar. The clue is a new who showing up partway through, like a "she," with an action of its own. That's usually where one thought ends and the next begins. While you're in there, notice "saw" and "felt." They're irregular past-tense verbs, the kind you've been practicing all week. And "prima donna" is Italian for the star singer of an opera.

## Before the word work · g4ela-12-3-guide1

The week has been building toward today. At the start of the week you sorted and noticed, and last lesson you hunted for examples. Now the words come out of your own head, the same way they do when you write your story. If Check My Work says something's off, read the hint and think about the rule before you try again. Your spelling Show What You Know gets gathered into your assignment, so take your time.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-3-morphology

You've met port and tract twice already this week, so these roots should feel like old friends. The only new part today is that the front of each word is given and you supply the root. If you get stuck, use the check from the start of the week. Think of a word you know well, like transport or attract, and see if its meaning fits the definition next to the blank.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-3-vocabulary

You've seen these four words all week, and one of them even turned up in the Growing-Up Watch box last lesson. Today the job flips. Instead of matching a word to its meaning, you're choosing the word a sentence needs. Every sentence here is about something from this week's chapters, so the story itself is a clue. When you finish, try the extra word in a sentence about yourself.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-3-grammar

You've noticed these verbs and hunted for them, and today you make them yourself. Earlier this week you saw that some change just a vowel and some turn into a whole new word. Keep both in mind for Show What You Know. When you write your two sentences about Chapter 29, try to pick verbs that change in different ways, so you have two different changes to name.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-3-spelling

You already know why that quiet e sticks around. It's guarding the soft sound of the c or g, and you've sorted and spotted words like these all week. The new part today is doing both checks on every word before you type. For your Show What You Know, words in Chapter 29 that end in -nce or -nge are good ones to try. Explain what the e does to the sound, like you practiced last time.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-3-guide2

You've watched Anne catch herself all week. Back in the first lesson, she said something unkind about Josie Pye in the very breath she promised not to. Now read Anne's moment in the Growing-Up Watch box. She's glad Josie won a prize, and she notices that gladness as proof she's improving. That's the same old mistake, finally turned around. Think about what a turnaround like that could look like for your own character.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-3-wgrd

Earlier this week you summarized with a frame: somebody wanted, but, so, then. Today adds one new idea, the turning point, the moment your whole summary hangs on. Once you find it, the frame still works. Keep in mind that a turning point can be quiet, like a thought someone has alone in bed. And take another look at the chapter title. The box tells you what an epoch is.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-3-read

You met Miss Barry back in Chapter 19, the night of the spare-room bed. Now she's the one sending the invitation. The Exhibition in this chapter was a lot like a county or state fair today, with prize animals, baking, and horse races. Also notice that much of the trip reaches you through Anne telling Marilla about it afterward, so you're seeing the city through her excited eyes.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-12-3-rwm

Last lesson, one verb told you how fast Gilbert moved when he sprang into the boat. Look for that same kind of word here. "Swung" shows how the cows move, and "placidly" means calmly, without any fuss. What makes the sentence fun is the gap between that calm lane and the poem racing through Anne's head. Try to build that same gap into your Mimic sentence, a slow chore and a busy mind.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-3-guide3

Today's Writer's Workshop says small and true beats big and vague, and that works for your chapter answers, too. Last lesson you practiced spotting questions that ask two things, so keep that habit going. Before you submit, look at the checklist at the bottom. It includes your three Read Like a Writer answers and your spelling response, so make sure both of those are finished.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-3-chapter

Same habit as last lesson: count the parts in each question before you write. For question one, "specific" means something a reader could find on the page, so "they had fun" won't do. Question two asks two things, and its hint points you to the exact line. For question three, a decision means comparing, so a strong answer names both places before it tells what Anne concluded.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-3-workshop

This is your third revision tool this week. You added in the first lesson, you cut and moved in the second, and today you swap. For your verb, look back at the Read Like a Writer sentence. Montgomery wrote that the cows "swung," and you can see them move. Sam's "slammed" shows that a strong verb can be short and ordinary. Make one swap where the moment really matters, and leave the rest alone.
