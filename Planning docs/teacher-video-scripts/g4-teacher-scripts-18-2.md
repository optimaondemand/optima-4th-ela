## Welcome · g4ela-18-2-welcome

A car pulling up outside a quiet house. Grete, you always know exactly where to stop. In Denmark in those years, a family often gathered at home with the casket before a funeral. Relatives and friends came to sit together, quiet and respectful, with lights burning low. So a room full of mourners would not look strange to most people. Keep that in mind when the officer demands to know why so many people are there. The family is using a real, ordinary custom as a disguise. As you read, notice who in the room seems to know the whole plan, and who is still guessing, the way Annemarie is.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-18-2-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

A tally for every light. I can already picture a page full of tiny marks. Let me show you how a repeat can change, using a story outside our book. Imagine a tale about a sailor where the sea keeps coming back: calm at the start, wild in the middle, calm again at the end. The sea is the same every time, but each return tells you something new about the sailor. The tally tells you what matters. The change tells you what it means. In Chapter 10, three things are worth tracking: the casket, the flames and the stars. When you finish, find your longest tally and ask how that thing was different the last time it showed up.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-18-2-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Two pairs squeezed together and a name that owns something. That's a busy sentence for its size. I'll fix a different one first, so you can watch. My sentence is: the sailors map wasnt dry and the storm hadnt passed. It needs a capital T to start. The map belongs to the sailor, so sailor gets an apostrophe and an s. Wasn't is was plus not, so the apostrophe sits right where the o used to be. Hadn't works the same way. Two complete thoughts are joined by and, so a comma goes in front of and. Then a period. Now try sentence B, and for every apostrophe you add, say which job it is doing.


## Before the word work · g4ela-18-2-guide1

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule · Spelling rule]

One small mark with two different jobs. English loves a trick like that. For grammar, try stretching the word back out. If wasn't stretches into was not, the mark is standing in for missing letters. If stretching it makes nonsense, like turning the ship's sail into the ship is sail, then the mark is showing that something belongs. For spelling, try clapping a long weather word, like drizzle or temperature. Feel how the very last beat comes out as one solid chunk that never splits apart. Clap each of your long words the same way before you decide whether it counts.


## Morphology · g4ela-18-2-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Deeper into the woods, and still no lights. I'd want someone to hold my hand on this stretch. Since all three spellings mean to go, the meaning will not help you choose between them. Your ears can. Say each finished word out loud and listen to its last beat. Does it sound like seed, or like sess? If you hear sess, there's only one spelling it can be. If you hear seed, you have two spellings to pick from, and the one that ends with an e is much more common. Only a tiny handful of words use the other one. Think of them like rocks a sailor learns by heart and steers around.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-18-2-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: symbol]

Trying it without the cards first. That's the best way to find out what really stuck. Symbol is a word worth slowing down for, because once you notice symbols, you see them everywhere. Picture a lighthouse. It is a real tower with a lamp at the top. But to a sailor lost in a storm, it can also stand for safety, or for finding the way home. The lighthouse is real, and it means more than itself. That's what makes it a symbol. For the other three words, try saying each one in a sentence about the sea before you tap its meaning. If the sentence sounds right, your match probably is too.


## Grammar · g4ela-18-2-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

Ask what each mark is doing there. That's the right advice for a paragraph this crowded. Watch for one sneaky kind. When a whole group owns something, like the sailors' boats, the apostrophe slides to the end of the word, after the s, because the s already tells you there are many. It still means belonging, not missing letters, so leave it alone. Read the paragraph once just for meaning. Then read it again slowly, pointing at each apostrophe as you go. Only tap a word when you can say the two words it was made from.


## Spelling · g4ela-18-2-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Endings glued so tight they always travel together. I'm going to picture them holding hands now. The tricky part is that these endings don't sound the way they are spelled. One sounds like chur, one like shun, and one like a soft ul at the very end. Think of motion, nature and puddle. Each one finishes with a chunk you cannot pull apart. One warning: not every word with a shun sound belongs. Ocean ends with that sound too, but the letters are different. So check the letters, not just the sound, before you tap a single word.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-18-2-guide2

## Before you read · g4ela-18-2-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Some of those words are the title of the book. I got a shiver when I first noticed that. A psalm is a very old poem, the kind people have sung and prayed for thousands of years. Peter reads from the family Bible that sits on the mantel, an old book kept close at hand, like the one in today's picture. You don't need to understand every line of the psalm. Annemarie does not, and Lois Lowry tells us so. Listen instead for how the words change the feeling in the room, and for the one line about the sky. And when the chapter ends, pause before you turn the page. Lowry stops at the exact moment before we find out more.


## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-18-2-rwm

Where your voice pauses in the middle of a sentence. Let's look at exactly how Lois Lowry does it.

[QUOTE ON SCREEN: press 6, read the line, press 6 again]

The sentence comes from Chapter 10: "Outside, she knew, the sky was speckled with stars."

Notice that Annemarie is indoors, behind dark curtains. She cannot see a single star. Those two small words, she knew, put us inside her head, picturing a sky she can only imagine. And look at the word speckled. It makes the stars sound tiny and scattered, like spots on an egg. For your Mimic, picture something your character knows is out there but cannot see, like waves crashing on a beach in the dark, or snow falling on a roof.


## Before you write · g4ela-18-2-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q1]

One single word that carries a whole reason. Grete makes that sound like a puzzle, and it is a fun one. Start with the first one, about Mama. In the barn, Uncle Henrik told Annemarie it's easier to be brave when you don't know everything. In this chapter, Annemarie finally understands what he meant. But Mama knows everything, and she is brave anyway. When you write, name exactly what Mama says and what she does with her body. Use a few of her own words inside quotation marks. Then add one sentence about what could have gone wrong if her voice had shaken even once.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-18-2-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Too many to count and too big to hold. Grete had me staring at the ceiling like it was the night sky. Now look at the casket prompt. It asks what the casket really holds, and Lowry ends the chapter before she shows us. So use your clues: the lid that's closed so tightly, the story Mama tells about the doctor, and Peter going straight to it once the room is safe. Write what you think, and name the clues that led you there. A careful guess built on evidence counts. Then think about how people feel when they see a casket, and why that feeling might keep soldiers from looking too closely.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-18-2-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Holding one word up to the light. What a good image, Grete. Let me try it with a sentence about the weather. Say a story tells you, The wind howled all night. Why howled, and not blew? Blew is just air moving. Howled sounds like an animal crying out, so the storm feels alive and a bit frightening. That's the move. After your quote, write a sentence that starts with The word, then name your word. Say what it would mean if the author had picked a plainer word, and then say what this word adds. Two sentences like that turn a quote into a real reason.
