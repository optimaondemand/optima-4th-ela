# Lesson 20.1 teacher video scripts

Day 1 · Ch. 1, Little Girl, Little Girl! Freedom Train. Guide this lesson: Marcus (college history student, former walking-tour guide on the Eastern Shore). Each script plays right after Marcus's clip in the same spot, so it adds to what he says instead of repeating it. About 40 to 60 seconds each. A gold 🎬 On screen line is a suggestion for that moment; it is never read aloud.

## Lesson video · g4ela-20-1

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Hi, friends, welcome to week twenty, and to a brand-new book. Freedom Train is a biography, which means it's a true story about a real person: Harriet Tubman. You'll meet a new guide this week, too. His name is Marcus, and he grew up near the same rivers Harriet knew as a girl. Before the lesson starts, you'll see a quilt open up into a night sky. Keep that picture in your mind, because quilts and stars both matter in this book. On the Word Study tab, you'll sew patches into a quilt with two new word parts. And in Writer's Workshop, we start research writing, which is a different job from the opinion essay you just finished.


## Welcome · g4ela-20-1-welcome

One ordinary day. That's a great way into a biography. Before you read, think about your own ordinary day: when you wake up, what you eat, what you do first. Hold that in your mind. As you read, put Harriet's day right beside yours. The differences will tell you a lot. But so will the one thing you both do, which is look out a window and wonder. When you write your central idea later, those small everyday facts are exactly the evidence you'll need.


## Freedom Watch · g4ela-20-1-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 1]

Looking as a kind of freedom. I love that idea. Here's a small challenge before you read. Find a window near you and look out of it for thirty seconds without talking. Then write down three things you noticed. Harriet does something like that in Chapter 1, but she only gets a stolen minute here and there. Watch which direction she keeps looking, and what she has heard about the place that way. In your journal, write one sentence about how a person who can't leave might still be free inside.


## Before you read · g4ela-20-1-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Before you open the book, two things to know. First, this chapter uses the words Master and Mistress. That's what enslaved people were forced to call the people who held them. Sterling uses those words because that is how it was, not because it was right. Second, the end of the chapter is hard. Harriet has to run and hide, and she is hungry and scared. If you need to pause, pause. Then read on to see who finds her, because that moment is gentle, and it matters.
