# Lesson 13.1 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 31 · Where the Brook and River Meet. Guide this lesson: Marilla. Each script plays right after Marilla's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-13-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week thirteen. Last week ended with Anne joining Miss Stacy's class to get ready for the Queen's entrance exam. In Chapter 31, a lot of time passes, starting with a whole summer, and Anne comes out of it looking and sounding different. Today's reading move is about theme, the big idea a story teaches about life. Your word work fits the chapter nicely. Linking verbs are how we say what someone seems like, and comparing words like taller and quieter are how we talk about change. Then in Writer's Workshop, you begin editing your own story, starting with capitals and end marks. As you go, watch for one thread that runs through the whole page: what it looks like when a person changes on purpose.

## Welcome · g4ela-13-1-welcome

Have you ever had a relative visit after a long time and say, "Look how tall you've gotten!" You probably didn't feel any taller. When you see yourself in the mirror every day, the change is too slow to notice. It takes someone who's been away, or an old photo, to show you how far you've come. Keep that in mind today. Anne was eleven when she first rode to Green Gables beside Matthew, talking the whole way. She's older now, and this chapter lets you hold the girl from the beginning of the book next to the girl in front of you. As you read, think about which of Anne's changes she can see herself and which ones other people have to point out.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-1-wgrd

A lot of readers mix up a topic and a theme, so let's sort that out first. A topic is one or two words, like friendship or growing up. A theme is a whole sentence about that topic, something the story believes is true about life. That's why the box asks you to finish a full sentence. Old fables make theme easy, because they often end with a line called the moral that spells it out for you. Most novels don't do that, so you have to find the idea in what happens. And when a character does say a big idea about life, check it against the story. Characters can be wrong. If the events back up what they say, then you've probably found a real theme.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-1-dol

The model sentence is funny when you know Anne. Way back near the start of the book, she told Matthew she liked big words, because big ideas needed big words to say them. Now she's quoting Miss Stacy saying the opposite. That one sentence shows you how much she's changed. When you fix the bottom sentence, watch out for one word that tempts people to add a capital. Seasons like summer and autumn are not names, so they stay lowercase unless they start a sentence. Months and days of the week do get capitals, though. So September gets a capital letter, but fall does not. You'll want this for your own story, too, if your character's big moment happens in a certain season.

## Before the word work · g4ela-13-1-guide1

Notice that today's grammar and spelling are partners. Linking verbs join a person to a describing word, and comparing words are describing words that measure something against something else. Put them together and you get sentences like "Anne seemed calmer than before." That's exactly the kind of sentence you need when you write about how a character has changed, in your chapter answers and in your own story. These are Day 1 notice activities, so the page wants you to find the pattern before it tells you. Try not to peek at the "Now check your thinking" part until you've finished sorting and named the groups in your own words. If a sort surprises you later, figure out why before moving on.

## Morphology · g4ela-13-1-morphology

These two roots show up in a lot of words you already know. A spectator is a person who watches a game, and old-fashioned glasses used to be called spectacles, because they help you see. Something spectacular is worth looking at. The building root is just as busy. A structure is anything that's been built, like a bridge or a barn, and to destroy is the opposite of building, so destruction carries the same root. Directions that tell you how to put something together are called instructions. One more word is worth learning. Perspective means the way you look at something, your point of view. Anne's perspective on her own life is changing in this chapter, so you might use that one in your sentence.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-1-vocabulary

Two of today's words are built from smaller words you already know. Broadminded is broad, which means wide, plus minded. A broadminded person has a wide-open mind. Its opposite is narrow-minded. Tactful is tact plus -ful, and tact means knowing what to say so nobody's feelings get hurt. Take away the -ful and add -less, and you get tactless, which is how you'd describe someone who blurts out the wrong thing. Ambition has an interesting history. It comes from a Latin word about going around, because in ancient Rome, people who wanted an important job walked around town asking for votes. And composure is a cousin of composer, someone who puts music together. A person with composure has kept themselves put together.

## Grammar · g4ela-13-1-grammar

Knowing about linking verbs fixes a mistake that lots of people make, even grown-ups. Because a linking verb joins the subject to a describing word, the word after it describes the person, so it shouldn't end in -ly. You'd say, "I feel bad," not "I feel badly." The same goes for "The bread smells good." And speaking of smells, the verbs for your five senses are the sneakiest ones. "The dog smelled the bread" is action, but "the bread smelled fresh" is linking. Grew can switch too. A farmer grows potatoes, which is action, but the sky grew dark is linking. So if a verb you're not sure about is one of the sense words, slow down and look at what comes after it.

## Spelling · g4ela-13-1-spelling

The page gives you three cases, but a few two-syllable words don't end in y, and they're the tricky ones. Some take an ending, like quiet and quieter. Others sound clumsy with one, so we say more careful, not carefuller. If you're truly stuck on a two-syllable word, more and most is usually the safer choice. Never use both at once, though. "More taller" has two comparing parts doing one job. Then there are a few words that follow no rule at all. Good becomes better and best, and bad becomes worse and worst. You'll actually find better in today's model sentence. And one quick tip: use -er when you compare two things, and save -est for three or more.

## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-13-1-copia

Think about why those books were up in the attic in the first place. The doctor said no books all summer, so bringing them down is a big moment for Anne. When you swap the verb, choose one that shows how she feels about it. Lugged makes the books sound heavy and dull. Hugged them down the stairs would sound very different. When you stretch the sentence, check it the way you'll check your story in Writer's Workshop today. A long sentence is fine as long as it's still one sentence. If you've written two complete thoughts with nothing joining them, you've made a run-on. Either add a joining word or split it with a period and a capital.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-13-1-guide2

Seeing how you have grown is hard, because growing happens a tiny bit at a time, like your hair growing. One trick is to compare yourself with something old. Find a story you wrote in second grade, or a drawing, and you'll see right away how much you've grown as a writer. Anne gets a chance like that in this chapter. Think back to the girl who couldn't stop talking about her imagination and her red hair, and compare her with the Anne you're about to meet. The box asks about your own character, too. Some characters announce their changes and some keep them private, and either one can work. Your choice tells readers a lot about who that character is.

## Before you read · g4ela-13-1-read

A little background will help with the beginning of this chapter. In Anne's time, doctors didn't have most of the medicines we have today. When a child seemed run down, a doctor would often order fresh air and sunshine. So keeping Anne outside all summer was a real prescription, and Marilla would have treated it that way. As you read, notice who tells you about Anne's change. Some of it comes from the narrator describing her, and some comes from Anne herself, in her own words, while she talks with Marilla. When a character explains her own feelings, readers get a window into her thinking. Mark the lines where Anne does that, because you'll want them later.

## Before you write · g4ela-13-1-guide3

These three questions don't ask you for an exact quotation, but they do want evidence. You can give evidence in your own words. Tell what happened in the chapter clearly enough that a reader could find that part in the book. That's called paraphrasing, and it counts. Each question also has two parts, so before you start, find the word "and" in the question and make sure you answer what comes on both sides of it. When you finish, use today's editing skill on your own answers. Check that every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with an end mark. It's good practice for your story, and it makes your thinking much easier for your teacher to read.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-13-1-chapter

For the first question, you'll find what you need in the opening pages of the chapter. Look for the doctor's words and then for Marilla's plan afterward, and make sure your answer covers both. For the second question, today's spelling and grammar are a big help. A change is a comparison between before and now, so comparing words and linking verbs fit right in. Try a sentence that uses "used to" and "now." For the third question, the end of the chapter matters most. Read what Marilla is thinking, and look for the difference between what she's lost and what she's gained. If you take the challenge, it's fine to decide that Anne has changed and also stayed herself. Just explain how both can be true.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-1-workshop

Run-on sentences are the hardest thing to catch today, so try reading your story aloud, quietly. When your voice drops and you take a breath, a sentence has usually ended, and that's where a period belongs. That's how Sam caught "today was tryouts" running into the next idea. Don't go too far the other way, though. A sentence with "and" or "because" joining two ideas is fine, so you don't need to chop up every long one. Check the dialogue in your story, too. When a character starts speaking, the first word inside the quotation marks gets a capital, even in the middle of a line. Then write your three fixes, so you can see the kinds of slips you make most.
