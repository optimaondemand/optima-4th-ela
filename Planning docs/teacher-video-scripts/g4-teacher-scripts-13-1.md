# Lesson 13.1 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 31 · Where the Brook and River Meet. Guide this lesson: Marilla. Each script plays right after Marilla's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-13-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week thirteen. Last week ended with Anne joining Miss Stacy's class to get ready for the Queen's entrance exam. In Chapter 31, a lot of time passes, starting with a whole summer, and Anne comes out of it looking and sounding different. Today's reading move is about theme, the big idea a story teaches about life. Your word work fits the chapter nicely. Linking verbs are how we say what someone seems like, and comparing words like taller and quieter are how we talk about change. Then in Writer's Workshop, you begin editing your own story, starting with capitals and end marks. As you go, watch for what it looks like when a person changes on purpose.


## Welcome · g4ela-13-1-welcome

Marilla missing Anne's chatter is a surprise, isn't it? After all that shushing, she misses the noise. Have you ever had a relative visit after a long time and say, "Look how tall you've gotten!" You probably didn't feel any taller. When you see yourself every day, the change is too slow to notice. It takes someone who's been away, or an old photo, to show you. Anne was eleven when she first rode to Green Gables beside Matthew, talking the whole way. This chapter lets you hold that girl next to the one in front of you. As you read, think about which changes Anne sees herself and which ones other people have to point out.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-13-1-wgrd

That brook turning into a river is a lovely picture, and it fits today's skill well. A lot of readers mix up a topic and a theme. A topic is one or two words, like friendship or growing up. A theme is a whole sentence about that topic, something the story believes is true about life. That's why the box asks you to finish a full sentence. Old fables often end with a moral that spells it out, but most novels don't, so you find the idea in what happens. And when a character states a big idea about life, check it against the story. Characters can be wrong. If the events back it up, you've probably found a real theme.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-13-1-dol

It's nice to hear Marilla and Miss Stacy agree. Short, plain words suit Marilla perfectly. The model sentence is funny when you know Anne. Near the start of the book, she told Matthew she liked big words, because big ideas needed big words to say them. Now she's quoting Miss Stacy saying the opposite. That one sentence shows you how much she's changed. When you fix the bottom sentence, watch for a word that tempts people to add a capital. Seasons like summer and autumn are not names, so they stay lowercase unless they start a sentence. Months do get capitals, so September gets one, but fall does not.


## Before the word work · g4ela-13-1-guide1

Sorting the mending into three piles sounds exactly like Marilla, tidy right down to the unsure pile. Notice that today's grammar and spelling are partners. Linking verbs join a person to a describing word, and comparing words are describing words that measure one thing against another. Put them together and you get sentences like "Anne seemed calmer than before." That's the kind of sentence you need when you write about how a character has changed. These are notice activities, so the page wants you to find the pattern before it tells you. Try not to peek at the "Now check your thinking" part until you've finished sorting and named the groups in your own words.


## Morphology · g4ela-13-1-morphology

Comparing a root to a good habit tucked away in someone is such a Marilla thing to say. I might borrow it. These two roots show up in a lot of words you already know. A spectator is a person who watches a game, and old-fashioned glasses were called spectacles, because they help you see. The building root is just as busy. A structure is anything that's been built, like a bridge or a barn, and to destroy is the opposite of building. One more word is worth learning. Perspective means the way you look at something, your point of view. Anne's perspective on her own life is changing in this chapter, so you might use that one in your sentence.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-13-1-vocabulary

Marilla can't resist a little poke at Mrs. Lynde, can she? Those two have known each other a long time. Two of today's words are built from smaller words you already know. Broadminded is broad, which means wide, plus minded, so a broadminded person has a wide-open mind. Tactful is tact plus -ful, and tact means knowing what to say so nobody's feelings get hurt. Swap the -ful for -less and you get tactless, which describes someone who blurts out the wrong thing. Ambition comes from a Latin word about going around, because in ancient Rome, people who wanted an important job walked around town asking for votes.


## Grammar · g4ela-13-1-grammar

I won't ask which neighbors Marilla has in mind, but I have a guess. Knowing about linking verbs fixes a mistake lots of people make, even grown-ups. The word after a linking verb describes the person, so it shouldn't end in -ly. You'd say, "I feel bad," not "I feel badly." The verbs for your five senses are the sneakiest ones. "The dog smelled the bread" is action, but "the bread smelled fresh" is linking. Grew can switch too. A farmer grows potatoes, which is action, but the sky grew dark is linking. So if a verb is one of the sense words, slow down and look at what comes after it.


## Spelling · g4ela-13-1-spelling

Clapping out beats is a good trick, and Marilla promising to keep it a secret is the sweetest part. The page gives you three cases, but a few two-syllable words don't end in y, and they're the tricky ones. Some take an ending, like quiet and quieter. Others sound clumsy with one, so we say more careful, not carefuller. Never use both at once, though. "More taller" has two comparing parts doing one job. A few words follow no rule at all. Good becomes better and best, and bad becomes worse and worst. You'll find better in today's model sentence. Use -er when you compare two things, and save -est for three or more.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-13-1-copia

Marilla would always pick the shortest sentence that still does the job, and I think she's usually right. Think about why those books were up in the attic in the first place. The doctor said no books all summer, so bringing them down is a big moment for Anne. When you swap the verb, choose one that shows how she feels about it. Lugged makes the books sound heavy and dull, and hugged them down the stairs sounds very different. When you stretch the sentence, make sure it's still one sentence. Two complete thoughts with nothing joining them make a run-on, so either add a joining word or split it with a period and a capital.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-13-1-guide2

Marilla is keeping that night to herself, and I'll respect that. You'll find out soon enough. Seeing how you've grown is hard, because growing happens a tiny bit at a time, like your hair getting longer. One trick is to compare yourself with something old. Find a drawing you made in second grade, and you'll see right away how much you've changed. Anne gets a chance like that in this chapter. Think back to the girl who couldn't stop talking about her imagination and her red hair, and compare her with the Anne you're about to meet. Some people announce their changes and some keep them private, and both tell you something about the person.


## Before you read · g4ela-13-1-read

Asking you not to think too hard about the second stop is the surest way to make everyone think about it, and Marilla knows that. A little background will help with the start of this chapter. In Anne's time, doctors didn't have most of the medicines we have today. When a child seemed run down, a doctor would often order fresh air and sunshine. So keeping Anne outside all summer was a real prescription, and Marilla treated it that way. As you read, notice who tells you about Anne's change. Some comes from the narrator, and some comes from Anne herself, in her own words. Mark the lines where Anne explains her feelings, because you'll want them later.


## Before you write · g4ela-13-1-guide3

Marilla wants answers that stay on track, and I'll admit I'm with her on that one. These three questions don't ask for an exact quotation, but they do want evidence. You can give evidence in your own words. Tell what happened in the chapter clearly enough that a reader could find that part in the book. That's called paraphrasing, and it counts. Each question also has two parts, so before you start, find the word "and" in the question and make sure you answer what comes on both sides of it. When you finish, check that every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with an end mark.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-13-1-chapter

Marilla admitting she enjoyed being miserable is so honest it's almost funny. I think plenty of us know that feeling. For the first question, look in the opening pages for the doctor's words and then Marilla's plan, and make sure your answer covers both. For the second, today's word work helps. A change is a comparison between before and now, so comparing words and linking verbs fit right in. Try a sentence that uses "used to" and "now." For the third, the end of the chapter matters most. Read what Marilla is thinking. If you take the challenge, it's fine to decide that Anne has changed and also stayed herself. Just explain how both can be true.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-13-1-workshop

I love Marilla's lamp over the swept floor. It's the best picture of editing I've heard in a while. Run-on sentences are the hardest thing to catch today, so try reading your story aloud, quietly. When your voice drops and you take a breath, a sentence has usually ended, and that's where a period belongs. That's how Sam caught "today was tryouts" running into the next idea. Don't go too far the other way, though. A sentence with "and" or "because" joining two ideas is fine. Check the dialogue in your story, too. The first word inside the quotation marks gets a capital, even in the middle of a line. Then write your three fixes, so you can see the slips you make most.
