# Lesson 15.2 teacher video scripts

Bridge to Number the Stars, Day 2 · Denmark in 1940. Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-15-2

Hi, friends. Welcome to the second lesson of our bridge week. In the last lesson, you learned how World War II began in 1939, and how Germany took over one country after another until the Allies joined together to stop it. Today we slow down and look closely at just one of those countries, Denmark. That's where our new book takes place, so this is the history the story will expect you to carry in with you. You'll read about what changed in Denmark after the German army arrived, and what stayed almost the same. Then you'll meet the Danish king and a quiet kind of courage. In Writer's Workshop, you'll take an opinion and shape it into one clear claim.


## Welcome · g4ela-15-2-welcome

I like that idea of zooming in, like pulling a map closer until one small country fills the whole screen. So let's find it. Denmark sits in the north of Europe, right on top of Germany, and the two countries share a border. Most of Denmark is a peninsula, which means land with water on three sides, and the rest is islands. It's a small country, smaller than the state of Florida. You might already know one thing that comes from Denmark. The LEGO company started there. As you read, keep a picture in your head of that small country sitting right next to a huge, powerful neighbor. That picture will help a lot of today's facts make sense.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-15-2-wgrd

Grete is right that a number can hold a whole picture inside it. A date can feel like a fact for a test, but it was a real day that real people lived through. Let me show you how this works with something closer to home. If the power goes out for one hour, it's a little adventure with flashlights. If it goes out for a whole month, the picture changes completely. Same kind of fact, very different lives. One mix-up to avoid: circling the numbers isn't the goal. Circling is only the first step. A circled number with no thinking next to it is just decoration. And sometimes the smallest number is the most surprising one, so give it extra attention.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-15-2-dol

I love Grete's way of saying that names start tall. It's a nice way to picture a capital letter standing up straight. Look at the name in the model, King Christian X. That X isn't a letter in his name. It's the Roman numeral for ten. When kings and queens share a name, they get numbers so people can tell them apart, and we read it aloud as Christian the Tenth. The word king gets a capital here because it's part of his title, right before his name. When you just say the king, with no name after it, it stays small. For the run-together part, read your sentence aloud and listen for where your voice wants to drop and stop.


## Before the word work · g4ela-15-2-guide1

Grete's warning about answers that look almost right is a good one. Those near misses are what make finding harder than sorting. In the last lesson, you sorted every word into a group, so each one had a place to go. This time, some words are meant to be left alone, and that takes a little self-control. A trick that helps is to ask the same question about every single word before you tap. Don't tap a word because it looks familiar. Tap it because it passed your test. Also read each set of directions twice. One activity asks for the words that break the pattern, not the ones that follow it, and that's easy to flip by accident.


## Morphology · g4ela-15-2-morphology

Grete's tip about letting the meaning lead is smart, because a word can sound strange and still make perfect sense once you know its parts. Think about trading cards. When you transfer a card to a friend, it goes across, from your hands to theirs. When a caterpillar transforms into a butterfly, it crosses over into a whole new shape. The part pro has a second meaning on this page, too. It can mean in favor of. If you've ever made a list of pros and cons, the pros are the reasons in favor of something. So when a word starts with pro, ask yourself whether it's moving forward or cheering something on. Every plank you lay today brings you one step closer to the new book.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-15-2-vocabulary

I like that Grete turned this into a little guessing game. Making a guess first really does help a word stick. Look for smaller words hiding inside bigger ones. Inside one of today's cards you'll find govern, the same word that hides in governor. The ending ment turns an action into a thing, the way move becomes movement and pay becomes payment. Watch out for a sneaky one, though. Arrest has the word rest inside it, but it isn't about taking a nap. A hidden word can steer you wrong, so always check it against the sentence. For the Quick Match, you met those four words in the last lesson, so see how many you remember before you start tapping.


## Grammar · g4ela-15-2-grammar

Grete's swap test is a lifesaver, and it works for grown-ups too. Lots of people mix up these words when two people share a sentence. You might hear someone say, Me and my sister went to the park. Try the test by taking away your sister. Me went to the park sounds silly, so it should be, My sister and I went to the park. The opposite slip happens too. Grandma made cookies for my brother and I. Take away your brother, and you'd never say for I. It should be for my brother and me. One more clue: after small words like to, for, with, and between, you need the form that receives the action. That's why we say between you and me, and come with us.


## Spelling · g4ela-15-2-spelling

Soft and mumbly is exactly the right description. Those quick parts of a word are where spelling gets slippery. Think of captain and curtain. Say them normally, and the second half sounds like tin, not like rain. That's why people sometimes write captin. You can't hear the right letters, so you have to remember them with your eyes. One trick is a spelling voice. Say the word in a slow, silly way that rhymes the end with rain, so your brain stores the letters you can't hear. Use that voice only for practice, though. When you hunt for today's exceptions, say each word slowly, then say it once more inside a normal sentence, because that's where the mumbling hides.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-15-2-guide2

Showing up again and again is such a good way to say it. Most courage doesn't come with a parade. Look at the picture on this tab. It shows King Christian riding through a crowded street in Copenhagen on his seventieth birthday, in September of 1940, just a few months after the German army arrived. Look at all the people who came out to see him. A king riding past might not sound brave, but every ride said something without any words at all. You can do something like that, too. Sitting with the new kid at lunch every day, even when someone teases you for it, is the same kind of courage. It isn't loud, but people notice, and it helps them feel braver too.


## Before you read · g4ela-15-2-read

Grete's idea of a surface and an underneath is a great way into this passage. Picture a pond that looks perfectly still on top while the ducks paddle hard under the water. A couple of words first. When the passage says the king stayed on his throne, it means he was still the king. A throne is the special chair a king sits on, so the word stands for his whole job. The passage comes from the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, a museum in Washington, D.C., that teaches this history carefully. Be gentle with yourself as you read. One sentence is about people being arrested just for speaking up, and it's fine to feel uneasy about that. Notice the feeling, and keep reading.


## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-15-2-rwm

I love the picture of two small words turning a corner. That's exactly what they do in this sentence. Watch out for a close cousin, though. The word so by itself and the words so that do different jobs. In I was tired, so I went to bed, the word so tells you what happened next. In I went to bed early so that I would have energy for my game, so that tells you why someone did something on purpose. It points to a goal. The writer's sentence uses the goal kind. When you write your Mimic sentence, check that the second half explains why the group wanted something, not just what happened after. Your group could be a team, a class, a family, or a town.


## Before you write · g4ela-15-2-guide3

Keeping the passage close is good advice, since the proof for every answer is sitting right there. This passage has two groups of people in it, the Danes and the Germans. When you write about both, the word they can get confusing fast. If you write, The Germans watched the Danes, and they were careful, your reader can't tell which group was careful. When that happens, use the group's name again instead of they. It might feel repetitive, but clear is better than short. The same goes for he when you write about the king and a German leader in the same answer. Before you move on, reread each answer and stop at every they. Ask yourself who it points to.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-15-2-chapter

Grete's last point matters. When someone chooses something hard on purpose, it tells you a lot about them. For question one, notice that it asks two things, what happened and how the king responded. Answer both, and use what the passage says for the second part, not a guess about his feelings. For question two, the hint gives you a strong clue with the word not. Sometimes history is about what didn't happen. Write each way as its own complete sentence so your reader can count them. For question three, the passage tells you what the Resistance was, but it never says exactly why people joined. That part is yours to think through, so explain it in your own words and point to the passage for support.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-15-2-workshop

No maybe and no kind of. I'm going to borrow that, because wobbly words sneak into lots of writing. Two mix-ups to watch for. First, a claim can't be a fact. Dogs have four legs is true, but nobody would argue with it, so it can't be a claim. You learned that difference in the last lesson, and it matters even more now. Second, a claim isn't a question. Which is better, dogs or cats? doesn't take a side at all. Also watch for other wobbly words, like pretty, sort of, and probably. Compare Kids should probably get more recess with Kids should get more recess. The second one sounds like you mean it. Pick a topic you'd really argue about with a friend.
