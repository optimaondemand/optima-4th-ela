## Welcome · g4ela-14-3-welcome

Before you start, think about who Matthew was to Anne. He was the first person at Green Gables who wanted her to stay. On that first drive home from Bright River, he hardly said a word. Anne talked the whole way, and he liked her anyway. Later, he was the one who noticed that she longed for puffed sleeves, and he gave her that brown dress at Christmas. Matthew didn't make speeches. He showed his love in small, quiet ways. Hold on to those moments as you read today. They'll help you understand why this chapter is so hard for Anne. They might help you understand why it feels hard for you, too.

## Before the word work · g4ela-14-3-guide1

These are the last roots of our unit, and they're good ones to finish with. Script and scribe mean to write. You already know more of these words than you think. When someone adds a P.S. at the bottom of a letter, those letters stand for postscript, something written after. Ject means to throw, like the eject button that throws a disc out of a player. For each blank, read the little clue beside it and ask yourself which action it describes. Then, in grammar, look back at your warm-up sentence. Anne hoped that the tears would come. That word would is a helping verb. It points to something that hadn't happened yet. Watch for small helpers like that today.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-3-guide2

The Growing-Up Watch box has a line I'd like you to read twice. Being grown up is not feeling less. It is feeling all of it and still doing what has to be done. Some readers think that if a character doesn't cry, she must not care very much. Keep an eye on that idea today, because Anne shows it isn't true. Think back to Chapter 9, when Mrs. Lynde called Anne skinny and homely, and Anne stamped her foot and shouted at her. That Anne let every feeling out right away. The Anne in this chapter feels even more, and she holds it for a while. That change is what the What Good Readers Do box is asking you to notice. Read slowly.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-14-3-rwm

The phrase wept her heart out is an old way of saying someone cried as hard as she possibly could. Now look at the two verbs in this model, wept and crept. Neither one ends in e-d. Weep becomes wept, and creep becomes crept, so the suffix rules from your spelling work don't fit them. Sleep and slept follow the same pattern. Verbs like these kept an older way of changing, and you learn them by using them. Notice, too, how short these sentences are. When something enormous happens, Montgomery doesn't pile up words. Two small sentences let you feel how quiet that dark room was. When you write your Mimic, try keeping your sentences short like hers.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-3-guide3

Each of these questions asks you two things, so check that your answer covers both parts. The question about Diana's offer, for example, asks why and also what Anne is trying to do. Use the hint under each question to find the right part of the chapter, then look for the words that show it. The last question might feel a little strange. Anne feels bad for laughing so soon. If you've ever laughed during a sad week, you know that feeling. In the workshop, you'll write a dedication like Sam's, for his mom, who told him to try anyway. A dedication can go to someone who's right there with you, or to someone you miss. Either one is a good choice.
