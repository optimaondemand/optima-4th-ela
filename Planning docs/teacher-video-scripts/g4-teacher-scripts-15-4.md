# Lesson 15.4 teacher video scripts

Bridge to Number the Stars, Day 4 · The October 1943 rescue. Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-15-4

Hi, friends. Our bridge is almost finished. Three lessons of history are behind you, and today's reading is the one all of them were leading to. You'll read about October 1943, when ordinary Danish people helped thousands of Jewish families escape across the sea. Your word work is review, so trust what you practiced. In Writer's Workshop, you'll choose your strongest claim. Next week, the story finally begins.


## Welcome · g4ela-15-4-welcome

Fear and courage in the same story. Grete's right, and they usually do show up together. Think about how long the Danes had been waiting by then. The German army arrived in April 1940, and this rescue came in October 1943. That's more than three years of living under someone else's rules. Annemarie, the girl in the novel, is ten when it opens, so the soldiers arrived when she was about six. Keep that long wait in mind as you read.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-15-4-wgrd

Four passages, each explaining a little of the next. I like picturing them as four puzzle pieces finally clicking together. Try the first step in the box quickly. Write just one short line for each day, not a summary. Then look for a link, like a fact from the first lesson that today's passage suddenly makes clearer. The last step is my favorite. Your big question is something only the novel can answer, so save it for next week.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-15-4-dol

Fishermen and the sea is a fitting model for today. I can almost smell the salt water. A word made from a country's name keeps its capital, even when it describes something else. Think of a Danish pastry, Swedish meatballs, or a German shepherd. All three come from country names, so they stand tall. Earlier this week you capitalized countries and a king's title. This is the same idea, just one step further.


## Before the word work · g4ela-15-4-guide1

Careful readers check before they change. I might put that on a sign in my classroom. Earlier this week, you hunted for words that broke a pattern. Today the hunt is for mistakes, and some items are already right. Try reading each activity all the way through once without tapping anything. Then go back for a second pass. Mistakes are much easier to spot when you aren't rushing to find them.


## Morphology · g4ela-15-4-morphology

The last planks, and every lamp lit. I'll admit I'm a little proud of this bridge. You know both parts well now, across and forward, and you also remember that the forward part can mean in favor of. On today's fill-ins, read the meaning first and ask which direction it points. Then say the whole word aloud. If it sounds like a word you've really heard, you've probably laid the plank in the right spot.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-15-4-vocabulary

Picturing the moment is such a good habit. Some of these sentences really do play like a movie in your head. Look at the example word survive on this tab. The part that means to live shows up in other words, too. When a doctor helps someone who fainted wake back up, we say they revive the person, or bring them back to life. For the Quick Match, you've seen those four words all week, so trust your memory.


## Grammar · g4ela-15-4-grammar

Reading out loud really does help. My ear catches mistakes my eyes skip right over. Earlier this week you used the cover-up trick, taking away the other person to hear the pronoun alone. That still works here. Cover up the extra person, say the sentence with just the pronoun, and trust what you hear. Then ask the question from the first lesson: is this word doing the action, or receiving it?


## Spelling · g4ela-15-4-spelling

Soft and quiet vowels in the middle of a word are sneaky, and Grete's warning is a good one. Earlier this week you practiced a spelling voice, saying a word slowly and a little silly so you could hear every letter. Use it again here, then trust your eyes. When you find a word you think is wrong, picture it written on a sign. Does it look the way you've seen it before? If you aren't sure, leave it alone.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-15-4-guide2

Choosing not to forget is a quiet kind of courage, and I think it's one of the most important kinds. Remembering can start very close to home. Maybe a grandparent once helped a neighbor, or an aunt stood up for someone at work. Asking them to tell you that story is a way of remembering, too. The Think question in this box asks about someone in your life. Pick a real person you'd be proud to write about someday.


## Before you read · g4ela-15-4-read

Not one hero, but many. That's what makes this story so special to me. The photo on this tab shows the water between Denmark and Sweden. At its narrowest, the Swedish coast is less than three miles away, though many boats had to cross much wider stretches. Picture doing that in a small fishing boat in the cold of October. Annemarie's uncle is a fisherman, so this water matters in the novel. One sentence names where the Nazis planned to send people. Read it gently.


## Before you write · g4ela-15-4-guide3

The week's last writing already! It went by faster than I expected. The third question asks you to look across all four readings. The one line per day you wrote for What Good Readers Do is a perfect place to start. The challenge line for that question asks for facts from at least two different days, so name which reading each fact comes from. That way your reader can follow you across the whole week.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-15-4-chapter

A comparison is only as strong as its facts. I'm going to remember that one. Comparing gets easier with the right small words. Try but, while, or unlike to join your two sides. For example, unlike my brother, who loves spicy food, I can't eat even a little. One side, then the other, in one smooth sentence. Make sure each side has its own real fact from the passage, not a general feeling.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-15-4-workshop

Having the most to say beats feeling the most strongly. Wise tip from Grete. Count your reasons on your fingers, and check that each finger is really a new reason. A later bedtime is fun, and a later bedtime is nice, is one reason said twice. A later bedtime gives me time to finish my chapter is a new one. Pick topics from your own life, like chores, screen time, or the best board game. You'll find more reasons when you know a topic well.
