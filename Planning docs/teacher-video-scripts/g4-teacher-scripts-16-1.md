## Welcome · g4ela-16-1-welcome

I love that Grete tells us right away that she's made up, just like Annemarie. That's a good habit for any historical fiction. Ask what's invented and what's real. The street the girls run down is a real place in Copenhagen. You can find it on the map. The soldiers on the corner are real too, because German soldiers really did stand on corners all over the city. So as you read, keep two lists going in your head. One is the story. The other is the true history underneath it.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-16-1-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

People only act this way when. That little sentence starter is going to do a lot of work for us. Here's an example that isn't from the book. If your friend whispers in the library, that's normal. If your friend whispers at a birthday party, something's up. The whisper is the same, but the place makes it strange. That's what you're hunting for in Chapter 1. Look for an ordinary action in a place where it suddenly doesn't fit. When you find one, write the sentence starter in your journal and finish it in your own words.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-16-1-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Grete's tiny mark is the apostrophe, and today it does something important. When a name owns something, like Annemarie owning her neighbors, the name gets an apostrophe and an s. Keep that in your pocket, because the grammar later in this lesson looks at owner-words like her and their. Those never get an apostrophe. Names do. Owner-words don't. For the run-together part, read the sentence aloud and listen for the spot where a brand new who starts. That's your stop.


## Before the word work · g4ela-16-1-guide1

A rule you find yourself really does stay longer. I still remember the ones I figured out the hard way. Here's how this week's word work moves. In this lesson you sort, next time you hunt, then you build, and the last lesson is review. Each step needs a little more from you. So if sorting feels slow, that's fine. Slow now makes building easier later. When the page asks you to explain your thinking, use a full sentence, even if your sort was perfect.


## Morphology · g4ela-16-1-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Lighting a night sky with word parts? I'm a little bit jealous I didn't have this game in fourth grade. Read the Why stars box on this page, because it has a fact I didn't know. In Denmark, the Big Dipper has its own name, Karlsvognen, which means Charles's Wagon. As for the roots, you already know words built from the one that means to lead. A conductor leads an orchestra. When a word gives you trouble, ask yourself which idea fits, breaking or leading. Then let that pick your root.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-16-1-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: relocate · suspicion]

Words that follow you all week. I like that, because you'll meet these again in the chapters too. Look closely at relocate. On its own, it sounds like an everyday word for moving, like moving to a new house. But read the whole meaning on the card, all the way to the end. The last few words matter in this book. Suspicion is the other one to watch. It's a feeling, not something you can see. So when you picture it, think about a face or a pause, not an object.


## Grammar · g4ela-16-1-grammar

Grete says the apostrophe is the biggest clue, and she's right. Here's the trick for the look-alikes. If a word has an apostrophe, try reading it as two words. It's becomes it is. They're becomes they are. If that makes sense in the sentence, it's not an owner-word at all. One word that might surprise you is his. It can sit before a noun, like his coat, and it can stand alone, like the coat is his. So look at what comes right after it before you decide which group it belongs in.


## Spelling · g4ela-16-1-spelling

Grete's related-word trick is a great one, and I've got a second one for you. It's called your spelling voice. When you practice a word, say it the silly way it's spelled, with every vowel loud. Separate becomes sep-A-rate. It sounds funny, and you'd never say it that way out loud to a friend. But it helps you remember the letter that hides when you talk normally. Use your regular voice to sort, then your spelling voice to remember.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-16-1-copia

Writers practicing like this for hundreds of years makes me feel like we're in good company. For the stronger verb, think about how Annemarie is really moving. Is she racing, or dashing, or sprinting? Each one paints a slightly different picture, so pick the one that fits a girl running a race with her friend. For the detail, the page gives you a hint with the schoolbooks. Try your own. You could use something she hears, something she sees, or how her legs feel. Then read all three versions aloud and choose your favorite.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-16-1-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 1]

Choosing your words when your heart is pounding. I've definitely had that feeling, usually right before I had to tell someone bad news. You've probably had it too, maybe when a grown-up asked who broke something. The Courage Watch box asks for your own example, so think of a time you stayed calm on the outside while you were nervous on the inside. That counts as brave. You'll see Annemarie do it in this chapter, and she's only ten.


## Before you read · g4ela-16-1-read

Little Kirsti is going to be fun to watch. Every family has someone who says exactly what they're thinking. Before you start, open Annemarie's Path at the top of this tab. It shows the street where the race happens, and you can see how close the girls are to home when they get stopped. As you read, remember what you learned last week. Denmark had been taken over, and the soldiers had been there for years. The girls grew up seeing them. Keep your journal open for the pause questions, and answer each one before you read on.


## Before you write · g4ela-16-1-guide3

Keeping the chapter close is the right move, and I'd add one thing. When you go back to find a detail, look for something you could point to on the page. A word, an action, or something a character says. Last week your proof came from a passage about history. Now it comes from a story, so your evidence might be how someone acts, not just a fact or a date. Write the detail first, then a sentence about what it shows.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-16-1-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

Being noticed. That word is going to come back again and again in this book. One tip for all three questions. Answer with the chapter, not with everything you learned last week. Your history knowledge helps you understand, but the question wants to see what this chapter shows. On question two, Annemarie's answer isn't a lie, and it isn't the whole story either. Think about what she chooses to say and what she leaves out.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-16-1-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

Not just that she's brave, but exactly what she does. That's my favorite kind of writing advice. Here's a quick test for your moment. Could a friend find it in the chapter from your description? If you wrote, Annemarie is brave on the street, your friend might not know where to look. If you name the exact thing she does or says, they'd find it right away. And remember, courage or loyalty can belong to any character, not just Annemarie. Pick the moment you really care about.
