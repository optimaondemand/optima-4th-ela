# Lesson 11.3 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 24 · Miss Stacy and Her Pupils Get Up a Concert. Guide this lesson: Miss Stacy. Each script plays right after Miss Stacy's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-11-3

Hi, friends. We're in the middle of week eleven, and this week has been all about being seen. In Chapter 21, Mrs. Allan looked past a ruined cake and saw what Anne meant to do. In Chapter 22, Anne was called Miss Anne Shirley for the first time. Today, in Chapter 24, a new teacher arrives, and she gets something right about Anne that nobody asked her to. Before you read, you'll build words and sentences yourself, which is a step up from spotting them. After the chapter, you'll study one sentence the author wrote about Miss Stacy and borrow its shape. Then you'll write Scene 4, the part of your story where your kid finally does something.

## Welcome · g4ela-11-3-welcome

To feel how big Miss Stacy is for Anne, think back to her first teacher in Avonlea, Mr. Phillips. When Anne cracked her slate over Gilbert's head, Mr. Phillips made her stand in front of the whole class with a sentence about her temper written on the board above her. Anne was so hurt that she refused to go back to school at all. So Anne hasn't always loved school. A teacher can make a room feel safe or make it feel like a stage where you're waiting to be embarrassed. As you read today, notice how quickly Anne decides about Miss Stacy, and what Miss Stacy does that Mr. Phillips never did.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-11-3-dol

A tricky case is hiding in this sentence. Days of the week always get a capital letter, and so do months. Seasons don't. So you'd write "on Friday" and "in October," but "in the fall" stays small. The word "Miss" works like a name, too. When it comes right before Stacy, it gets a capital. But if you just write "the teacher," no capital, because that isn't her name. To find where one thought ends, try reading each part by itself. If a part makes sense standing alone, it can be its own sentence. You've got a clue in the verbs, too. One part is about later and one is about before, and that switch in time is often where a new sentence begins.

## Before the word work · g4ela-11-3-guide1

Think about how this week's word work has been climbing. Earlier in the week you noticed and sorted. Today you build, and tomorrow you'll check work that's partly right and partly wrong. Each step asks more of you. Today's activities have hint buttons, and there's a smart way to use them. Try first, even if you're not sure. If you're stuck, open one hint and let it nudge you, then try again before you look for more. When you get one right, still read the explanation. It tells you why the answer works, and that's the part that helps you next time. Also check your typing. The right idea with one letter off still counts as wrong.

## Morphology · g4ela-11-3-morphology

People still use these prefixes to build brand-new words. When computer programmers hunt down mistakes in their code and take them out, they call it debugging, getting the bugs away. When a king loses his crown, people say he's been dethroned, brought down from his throne. So a prefix keeps its meaning even in a word nobody had heard of a hundred years ago. Anti- has a sneaky habit, too. In a few words it drops its i. An antonym is a word that means the opposite of another word, the way hot is the opposite of cold. Miss Stacy said today's word work is mostly building. That's exactly what people did to make every one of these words, one piece at a time.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-11-3-vocabulary

A word can fit a sentence in two different ways. It can fit the grammar, meaning it sounds like the right kind of word in that spot. Or it can fit the meaning. You need both. For example, in "After the game, we drank cold ___," almost any thing-word sounds okay in that spot, but "water" makes sense and "homework" doesn't. So look for the clue the writer tucked into each sentence, like a phrase after a dash or another describing word nearby. One more thing to watch for. Don't save the last blank for whatever word is left over. If you made a mistake earlier, you'll make a second one. Check every blank on its own.

## Grammar · g4ela-11-3-grammar

One mistake shows up with the future tense all the time, so let's catch it early. After the word "will," the verb stays in its plain form. It doesn't get an ending. We say "Anne will jump," never "will jumped" or "will jumps." The word "will" already carries the time, so the verb doesn't need to. For the past ones that change on the inside, it helps to know some of the usual patterns. "Sing" becomes "sang," "ride" becomes "rode," and "think" becomes "thought." Then look at the Show What You Know. When a sentence has a past verb and a future verb together, it shows a change, what someone did then and what they'll do next. That's what growing up looks like in a story.

## Spelling · g4ela-11-3-spelling

Tucked inside one everyday word is a little piece of spelling history. The word business started out as busy plus -ness, which meant being busy, and the y changed to an i, just like this week's rule says. Today we say it like "biz-ness," but the spelling still remembers where it came from. There's one place the rule takes a day off, and that's family names. Diana's family is the Barrys, and the y stays put. If you changed it, you'd be changing the family's name, and you never change someone's name to fit a spelling rule. That's worth knowing when you hunt through Chapter 24 for a y-word for your Show What You Know. Pick an ordinary word, not a name.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-11-3-guide2

Way back in Chapter 3, on her very first evening at Green Gables, Anne told Marilla she'd rather be called Cordelia. When Marilla said no, Anne begged that if she had to be Anne, she should at least be Anne spelled with an e. That was one of the first things Anne ever asked for in this house. Today, Miss Stacy gets it right without ever being told. Think about the week so far. Mrs. Allan saw what Anne meant to do, and being called Miss Anne Shirley made Anne feel grown up. All three are kinds of being seen. Look at the Think question in the Growing-Up Watch box with your own character in mind.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-11-3-wgrd

This idea has been building all week. In Chapter 21, the cake disaster happened at a table with the minister and his new wife as guests, and that's what made it so awful. In Chapter 23, Anne walked the ridgepole because a whole group of girls was watching her. If she'd been alone, would she have climbed up there at all? Now Chapter 24 has a concert, which is the biggest audience yet. Crowds can push people to be brave or to be foolish. You can use this in your own story, too. Sam's Mateo falls in front of everyone, and his dad is watching. When you write today, decide who is standing nearby, because it changes what your kid will do.

## Before you read · g4ela-11-3-read

Some old-fashioned words in this chapter's title are worth a closer look. The title says the pupils "get up a concert." That's an old way of saying they planned and put one together. In Anne's time there were no movies or TV, so a school concert was a big night for the whole community. Families would come out to the hall to watch. Students often did recitations, which means saying a long poem from memory, out loud, in front of everyone. Think about how much practice that takes, and how nervous you might feel. Keep in mind, too, that Anne is coming back to school after weeks at home with a broken ankle. She's been missing out, and she's very ready to be part of something.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-11-3-rwm

Montgomery chose the word "gift" in this sentence, and it should sound familiar. Last week in Writer's Workshop, you named your kid's gift, the thing people like about them. Miss Stacy's gift is something she does with people. Notice how the sentence ends, too. She brings out the best in her pupils "mentally and morally," which means in their thinking and in how they behave. So it covers both their minds and their character. When you write your Mimic sentence, make your describing words match the gift. If the gift is about being kind, words like "tall" and "funny" won't connect to it. You could even try the pattern on your own story kid.

## Before you write · g4ela-11-3-guide3

Look closely at what each question needs. The first two don't ask for an exact quote. You can put what happened in your own words, but you still need to say when or where it happens in the chapter, so your reader could find it. Question three does ask for quotes, and the page already gives you one, Mrs. Lynde calling a lady teacher "a dangerous innovation." An innovation is a new way of doing something. So when you use that quote, explain what Mrs. Lynde was worried about in plain words. Then find a second quote, from Marilla or from Anne, to build out the other side of your answer.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-11-3-chapter

The word "specific" in question one matters a lot. "She's nice" isn't specific. A specific answer names something Miss Stacy actually does, the kind of thing you could see if you were in that classroom. So turn each feeling into an action. For question two, notice it's really two questions in one. Before you finish, read the question again and make sure you've answered both parts. For question three, think about how grown-ups sometimes feel about new things today. Many people feel safer with what they grew up with, and that doesn't make them bad people. Use "because" for each side, so you explain why the grown-ups feel one way and why the students feel another.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-11-3-workshop

Connect today's scene to last week's work. You named your kid's gift and how it becomes a problem. Scene 4 is where your kid takes that gift back. Look at Sam's Mateo. Caring too much made him want to quit, but in Scene 4 that same caring sends him to the front of the line. Your actions should show that turn. Choose strong doing words like "grabbed" or "marched" instead of "felt" or "was." And use today's grammar while you write. Your story is told in the past tense, so keep every verb there. Watch for slips like "he walks" sneaking in. Sam's dialogue line is just a first draft, so yours doesn't have to be perfect either.
