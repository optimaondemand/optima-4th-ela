# Lesson 11.2 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 22 & 23 · Anne Is Invited Out to Tea; Anne Comes to Grief in an Affair of Honor. Guide this lesson: Diana. Each script plays right after Diana's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-11-2

Hi, friends. In the last lesson, Anne's cake went wrong, and Mrs. Allan was kind about it in a way Anne will never forget. Today you'll read two chapters that feel like opposites. In Chapter 22, Anne goes to tea at the manse and has to be on her very best behavior. In Chapter 23, she's at Diana's party, and things get a lot less careful. This week's reading move is about how a place shapes what happens, and these two chapters show it clearly. Your word work today asks you to hunt for words that break a pattern. Then, in your writing, you'll build the worst moment of your own story. Keep an eye on the roof scene, because it's a model for exactly that.

## Welcome · g4ela-11-2-welcome

You can tell Diana loves Anne, because she teased her about pride and still sounded fond of her. In Chapter 23, Diana is the friend standing on the ground, watching. Most stories about a dare focus on the person doing it. But being the friend who watches is hard in its own way. You can see the danger, but you can't make the choice for them. Maybe you've been that friend at a sleepover. As you read, pay attention to what Diana says and does while Anne makes up her mind. Ask yourself what you would have said if you'd been in that garden.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-11-2-wgrd

Diana would pick the garden party in a heartbeat, and honestly, most of us would too. In the last lesson, you saw how a small village makes one mistake feel enormous, because everyone hears about it. Today, add one more piece to what we mean by setting. A setting isn't only a room or a garden. It includes who is there. At tea with Mrs. Allan, Anne is with a grown-up she admires. At the party, she's with a crowd of girls her own age, and some of them, like Josie Pye, aren't exactly her friends. So when you ask what a place allows, also ask whose opinion matters most to Anne in that spot. That question will help you with the roof.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-11-2-dol

Diana is right that Anne's sentence sounds grand. Anne never uses a small word when a big one is handy. Look at one word in the model sentence: "shall." People in Anne's time said "I shall" where we'd usually say "I will." It points to the future, just like "will" does. Then notice the word "perish." It means to die. Anne could have said she'd try her best, but that wouldn't sound like Anne. She talks as if every moment is the biggest moment in history. Keep "shall" in mind for the sentence you're fixing. Words that point ahead in time are good clues that a thought has moved somewhere new.

## Before the word work · g4ela-11-2-guide1

Poor Ruby. Diana is a little hard on her, but tapping every word you see really is an easy habit to fall into. Your grammar and spelling work are closer friends than they look. Every time you put a verb in the past tense, you add an ending, and that's exactly when the spelling rule about y kicks in. Think of the word "cry." Yesterday the baby cried. The y changed to an i because you added an ending. So the spelling you're practicing isn't only for word lists. It shows up whenever anyone tells about something that already happened. Today's chapters are told that way, so you'll meet plenty of these endings once you start reading.

## Morphology · g4ela-11-2-morphology

Diana's clues about the stairs and leaving home are good ones, so let me add a warning to sit beside them. Not every word that starts with the letters d and e has the prefix in it. Think about "desk" or "deep." If you take the d e off, nothing meaningful is left. A real prefix comes off and leaves a meaning behind. Sometimes what's left isn't a word on its own, but it's a root that shows up in other words. You'll see one of those today. Try it on your own with "defrost." What's left when you take off the prefix, and what does the full word mean now?

## Vocabulary · g4ela-11-2-vocabulary

Diana's Josie Pye test made me laugh. Some words simply refuse to fit some people. Look closely at the word "kindred." Hiding inside it is the little word "kin," an old word for family. Kindred people aren't related by blood, but they feel like family. Think about why that idea would mean so much to Anne, a girl who came to Green Gables with no family at all. When a word connects to a character's life like that, you'll remember it a lot longer. One more thing. Once you've matched three, the last one drops into place by itself, and it's easy to skip checking it. Read that word and its meaning together anyway.

## Grammar · g4ela-11-2-grammar

Diana is proud of reading slowly, and for once I'd take her side over Anne's. Two things catch people on a sentence like this. First, not every past tense verb ends in e d. Think of "ran," "went," and "fell." Those are past tense, but they don't wear the usual ending. So don't hunt only for e d. Second, not every word that ends in e d is a verb. "Red" and "bed" end that way, and they aren't actions at all. Time words can help you, too. Words like "yesterday" or "next week" tell you when something happens, even though they aren't verbs themselves. Use them as clues, then find the action word that goes with them.

## Spelling · g4ela-11-2-spelling

Diana wants you looking for reasons, so let me give you one of the reasons behind this rule. Try changing the y to an i in "cry" and adding i n g. You'd get c r i i n g, with two i's in a row. English almost never puts two i's together, so the y stays to keep the word readable. Words like "key" and "monkey" show another reason, because a vowel sits right in front of the y. And watch for words that break even the exceptions. "Day" becomes "daily," and "pay" becomes "paid." Those don't follow any pattern you've learned, so you just have to know them. Use what you notice to explain your answer in the box.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-11-2-guide2

Diana says she's only telling you to watch, but I think she knows more about this summer than she's letting on. The Growing-Up Watch reminds you that Anne has been here before. She broke a slate over Gilbert's head and she shouted at Mrs. Lynde, both times because her pride got hurt. So pride isn't new for Anne. What could be new is whether she notices it. One test that really helps is to ask, "Would I still do this if nobody were watching?" If the answer is yes, it might be brave. If the answer is no, it's probably the dare talking. When Anne reaches the roof, try that test on her.

## Before you read · g4ela-11-2-read

Diana can hardly keep a secret, can she? She nearly told you more than she meant to. A little background will help today. In Anne's time there were no phones, so a letter was a big event, and the way an envelope was addressed said something about who you were. Keep that in mind at the pause. Then look at the title of Chapter 23. It says Anne comes to grief in "an affair of honor." Long ago, that was a fancy name for a duel, a fight two men had when one felt insulted. The author is being playful. She uses a grand, serious phrase for a party game in a garden.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-11-2-rwm

Diana knows exactly how Anne moves when she's happy. That's the kind of thing a best friend notices. Let's make sure every word in this sentence is working for you. A sprite is a small, quick fairy kind of creature, so being wind-blown makes Anne light and fluttery. Now look at "lazy shadows." Shadows can't really be lazy. The author gives them a feeling a person has, and that one word tells you a lot about the evening. Writers use that trick all the time, and it works because it's surprising. When you write your own sentence, pick a time of day you know well and give one thing in that place a feeling of its own.

## Before you write · g4ela-11-2-guide3

Poor Diana. That must have been the most frightening afternoon of her life, and I'm so glad Anne was all right in the end. Anne talks a lot, and two of today's questions ask for a quotation. You don't have to copy a whole speech. Choose the few words that prove your point most clearly, put them in quotation marks, and say who said them. Then, just like last week, follow it with a sentence that explains what it shows. The first question doesn't need a quote, but it does need two details, so make them two different moments from the visit. If you see an extra challenge line after a question, give it a try.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-11-2-chapter

Of course Diana was watching Anne. Anyone in that garden would have been. Marilla's moment is one you get to see even though Diana didn't. For question three, make sure you know what "revelation" means. A revelation is when something you didn't see before suddenly becomes clear, all at once. So the question is really asking what Marilla suddenly understood. The word "pierced" is doing a lot of work, too. Her heart wasn't really stabbed. The author is showing how sharp and sudden the feeling was. For question two, give Anne's reason in her own words, then go one step further and tell what you think is underneath it.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-11-2-workshop

I believe Diana completely. Holding your breath in that garden sounds exactly right. When the moment matters most, a writer slows the clock down. Sam's draft moves fast. He covers the whole fall in one sentence, and next week he'll work on that. You can do it now. Picture a kid about to hang up a drawing at the art show, one she isn't sure about. Instead of "she was nervous," write what her hands are doing and the one thing she keeps looking at. Short sentences help, too. They make the reader read in little gulps. Spend more words on the few seconds before the choice than on anything else in the scene.
