# Lesson 12.1 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 26 · The Story Club Is Formed. Guide this lesson: Diana. Each script plays right after Diana's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-12-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week twelve. Anne is thirteen now, and in Chapter 26 she and her friends start a club where they make up and write their own stories. That makes this a fun chapter for you, because you're in the middle of writing your own story, too. Today's lesson pulls in two directions, and that's on purpose. In the warm-up and the chapter questions, you'll practice summarizing, which means leaving things out and keeping only what matters most. Then in Writer's Workshop, you'll start revising, and today's step is adding. A good writer needs both. You have to know which parts to keep short and which parts deserve more room. Watch for that as you work through the page today.

## Welcome · g4ela-12-1-welcome

Miss Stacy gave the class a writing assignment, and Anne turned it into a club. That's something a lot of real writers do. Grown-up authors often meet in small writing groups, where they share their drafts and talk about what's working. It helps to hear how your story sounds to someone else, and it helps to have friends who are waiting to find out what happens next. As you read, notice what the girls do with their stories once they're written, and how they feel about sharing them. Then think about who might read your story someday. Knowing that a real person will read your words can change the way you write them.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-1-wgrd

A lot of people think a summary means telling the first few things that happened. But a good summary covers the whole chapter, from the start all the way to the end, in very few words. One pattern that helps is to think of it this way: someone wanted something, but there was a problem, so they did something, and then it ended a certain way. Try it on a movie you know well before you try it on Chapter 26. Another thing to watch for is your own opinion sneaking in. A summary tells what happened, not whether you liked it. You'll use this again on the Assignment tab, where one question asks for a summary that's just two sentences long.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-1-dol

Take a closer look at the word "read" in the model sentence. It's spelled exactly the same in the present and the past, but it doesn't sound the same. Today I read, said like "reed," and yesterday I read, said like "red." The rest of the sentence is what tells you which one it is. Now let's think about owning something. A quick test helps. If you can flip it around and say "the horse of Matthew," then it's Matthew's horse, with an apostrophe before the s. But if you just mean more than one person, like the whole Barry family, you write "the Barrys," with no apostrophe at all. Mixing those two up is one of the most common mistakes in writing.

## Before the word work · g4ela-12-1-guide1

Both sorts today use three columns, and both are about words that break a rule. That's not an accident. English has lots of rules, and lots of words that don't follow them, and strong spellers know the exceptions as well as they know the rules. On the sorting activities, you're the one who figures out the pattern first. The page explains it afterward, in the part called "Now check your thinking." Try not to read that part until you've sorted everything and named the groups yourself. You'll remember a pattern you discovered much longer than one somebody told you. And if you get one wrong, that's useful, too. Stop and figure out why it belonged somewhere else.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-1-morphology

These two roots come from Latin, the language of ancient Rome, and once you know them, you'll start seeing them everywhere. A porter is a person whose job is carrying bags at a train station or a hotel. A tractor is a machine built to pull a plow across a field. You even use one of these roots in math class. When you subtract, you pull one number away from another. That gives you a great way to check yourself. If you're not sure about a word, think of another word you know with the same root and see if the meanings match. For your Show What You Know sentence, picture Anne's world. There were no tractors at Green Gables, but horses did plenty of pulling.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-1-vocabulary

Once you've flipped a card and seen the meaning, there's one more check worth doing. Put the meaning right back into the book sentence in place of the word, and read it aloud. If the sentence still makes sense, you've got it. If it sounds strange, something's off, and you should take another look. Word endings can help you narrow things down, too. When a word ends in l-y, like "carefully" or "quietly," it usually tells how something is done. Keep that in mind when you get to the Quick Match. And think about the word "diligent" while you read today. Making up whole stories and writing them out for a club, just for fun, takes a lot of diligence.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-1-grammar

You might wonder why these verbs don't just add e-d like most verbs do. It's because they're some of the oldest and most-used words in English. People said them so often, for so many hundreds of years, that the old ways of changing them stuck around. That's why little kids say things like "I runned" or "I bringed." They aren't being silly. They've figured out the regular rule, and they're using it everywhere. You already met one irregular verb today, in the Daily Oral Language. Your own story is probably told in the past tense, so verbs like these show up in almost every sentence you write. When you reread your draft, listen for any that sound wrong.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-1-spelling

This rule ties right back to week ten. Back then, you learned that c and g usually make their soft sounds before e, i, or y, and their hard sounds before a, o, or u. Keep that in your head while you sort today. Try saying a word as if the silent e had disappeared, and listen to what happens to the c or the g. Then ask yourself what job that little e might be doing. This matters outside of spelling, too. Words like these are some of the most commonly misspelled words in English, even by grown-ups. If you know why the e is there, you'll never have to guess when you're writing one of them in your own story.

## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-12-1-copia

When you move a "when" or "where" part to the front of a sentence, it usually needs a comma after it, right before the main part of the sentence starts. And when you swap the verb, try it out loud, because not every verb fits. "Diana said her Aunt Josephine about our club" doesn't work at all. By the way, that's the same Aunt Josephine whose spare-room bed Anne jumped onto back in Chapter 19. The third way, telling how Diana wrote, is a lot like today's revising step in Writer's Workshop. You're adding a detail so the reader can picture what happened. Practice it here on Diana's sentence, and then you'll be ready to do the same thing in your own story.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-1-guide2

Read Anne's words in this box closely, because there's something funny in them. She says she can't talk about Josie Pye without being unkind, so she never mentions her at all. And then she mentions her, right there in the same breath. "Uncharitable" means unkind, or not generous toward someone. Anne hasn't fixed her mistake yet, but she catches it, and that's a real step. Most of us learn this way. We notice ourselves doing the thing before we can stop doing it. Writers use this to make characters feel real. A character who sees her mistake and slips anyway is much more believable than one who changes all at once. Think about that for your own character.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-1-read

In Anne's time, many of the stories people loved to read were very dramatic, full of daring rescues and tragic deaths, and young writers often copied them. So when the club writes about faraway places and huge events, they're writing the kinds of stories they've read. Something to keep in mind at the second stop is that the author of this book, Lucy Maud Montgomery, grew up on Prince Edward Island. When she wrote Anne of Green Gables, she set it in a small farming town a lot like her own. So you're reading a whole book about the kind of ordinary place the story club never writes about. That doesn't answer the question for you, but it's worth thinking about.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-1-guide3

One habit makes every answer stronger. Start by turning the question into the beginning of your answer. If a question asks why Anne thinks something, your first sentence can start with "Anne thinks a moral is important because." That way, anyone reading your answer knows exactly what you're talking about, even without the question in front of them. Use names, too, instead of only "she" or "they." There are four girls in this club, and your reader needs to know which one you mean. When you finish, read each answer as if you'd never seen the book. Would it make sense to that person? If something's missing, add it before you move on.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-1-chapter

For question two, be careful about which story you're summarizing. The question doesn't want a summary of Chapter 26. It wants a summary of the story Anne wrote, "The Jealous Rival," which is a story inside the story. Use the same summarizing thinking you practiced in the warm-up. For question three, it helps to know exactly what a moral is. It's the lesson a story teaches. You probably know some from fables. In "The Tortoise and the Hare," the moral is that slow and steady wins the race. Once you've explained why Anne cares about morals, take one more step and say what that shows about her as a writer. Both parts of the question need an answer.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-1-workshop

Sam's fall is a good model for choosing where to add. Notice that he didn't add details everywhere. He picked the most important moment in his scene and slowed down right there. Think of an instant replay in a sports game. They don't replay the whole game in slow motion, just the big play. Your story's big play is probably a moment when your character feels something strong, like fear or embarrassment. That's the spot to stretch. It's also where a reader most wants to be right there with your character. When you're done, check your word count. It should go up today. If the number didn't change, you haven't added anything yet, so go back and find your spot.
