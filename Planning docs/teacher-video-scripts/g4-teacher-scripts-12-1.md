## Lesson video · g4ela-12-1

Hi, friends, and welcome to week twelve. Anne is thirteen now, and in Chapter 26 she and her friends start a club where they make up and write their own stories. That makes this a fun chapter for you, because you're in the middle of writing your own story, too. Today's lesson pulls in two directions, and that's on purpose. In the warm-up and the chapter questions, you'll practice summarizing, which means leaving things out and keeping only what matters most. Then in Writer's Workshop, you'll start revising, and today's step is adding. A good writer needs both. You have to know which parts to keep short and which parts deserve more room. Watch for that as you work through the page today.

## Welcome · g4ela-12-1-welcome

Diana's teasing, but she isn't wrong. Anne could stretch one sentence into fifty and enjoy every one of them. Miss Stacy gave the class a writing assignment, and Anne turned it into a club. Real writers do that too. Grown-up authors often meet in small writing groups, where they share drafts and talk about what's working. It helps to have friends who are waiting to find out what happens next. As you read Chapter 26, notice what the girls do with their stories once they're written, and how each of them feels about sharing them with the others.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-1-wgrd

Diana's way of ending stories turns out to be pretty handy for summarizing. Sometimes knowing when to quit is the whole trick. A lot of people think a summary means telling the first few things that happened. A good summary covers the whole chapter, start to end, in very few words. One pattern helps: someone wanted something, but there was a problem, so they did something, and then it ended a certain way. Try it on a movie you know well before you try it on Chapter 26. Also watch for your own opinion sneaking in. A summary tells what happened, not whether you liked it.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-1-dol

Diana sounds a little offended about that missing capital, and honestly, I would be too. Take a closer look at the word "read" in the model sentence. It's spelled the same in the present and the past, but it doesn't sound the same. Today I read, said like "reed," and yesterday I read, said like "red." The rest of the sentence tells you which one it is. For owning something, try a quick test. If you can flip it to "the horse of Matthew," then it's Matthew's horse, with an apostrophe. But if you mean the whole Barry family, you write "the Barrys," with no apostrophe at all.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-1-morphology

Diana's wagon-and-magnet trick is a clever one. I'd never have pictured a magnet, but it fits perfectly. These two roots come from Latin, the language of ancient Rome, and once you know them you'll see them everywhere. A porter carries bags at a train station or a hotel. A tractor is built to pull a plow across a field. You even use one of these roots in math class. When you subtract, you pull one number away from another. If you're unsure about a word, think of another word you know with the same root and see if the meanings match.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-1-grammar

Only Anne would think rule-breaking could be beautiful, but these verbs really do it in style. You might wonder why they don't just add e-d. It's because they're some of the oldest and most-used words in English. People said them so often, for hundreds of years, that the old ways of changing them stuck around. That's why little kids say things like "I runned" or "I bringed." They aren't being silly. They've figured out the regular rule and they're using it everywhere. You already met one irregular verb today in the Daily Oral Language. Notice that the verbs we use most are the ones most likely to break the rule.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-1-spelling

Diana makes it sound easy, and once you see the pattern, it really is. This rule ties right back to week ten. Back then, you learned that c and g usually make their soft sounds before e, i, or y, and their hard sounds before a, o, or u. Keep that in your head while you sort. Try saying a word as if the silent e had disappeared, and listen to what happens to the c or the g. Then ask yourself what job that little e might be doing. Words like these trip up plenty of grown-ups, so knowing why the e is there means you'll never have to guess.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-1-guide2

Diana doubts Anne is cured, and I'd say Diana knows her best friend pretty well. Read Anne's words in this box closely, because there's something funny in them. She says she can't talk about Josie Pye without being unkind, so she never mentions her at all. And then she mentions her, right there in the same breath. "Uncharitable" means unkind, or not generous toward someone. Anne hasn't fixed her mistake yet, but she catches it, and that's a real step. Most of us learn this way. We notice ourselves doing the thing before we can stop doing it.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-1-read

I love that Diana is the one who noticed Avonlea never shows up in the club's stories. She sees more than people give her credit for. In Anne's time, many of the stories people loved were very dramatic, full of daring rescues and tragic deaths, and young writers often copied them. So when the club writes about faraway places and huge events, they're writing what they've read. Keep one more fact in mind at the second stop. The author, Lucy Maud Montgomery, grew up on Prince Edward Island and set this book in a small farming town a lot like her own. That doesn't answer the question for you, but it's worth thinking about.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-1-guide3

Diana has a point about Anne, though I suspect Anne would argue for a longer answer anyway. One habit makes every answer stronger. Turn the question into the start of your answer. If a question asks why Anne thinks something, your first sentence can begin with "Anne thinks a moral is important because." That way, anyone reading knows exactly what you mean, even without the question in front of them. Use names, too, instead of only "she" or "they." There are four girls in this club, and your reader needs to know which one you mean. When you finish, read each answer as if you'd never seen the book, and add anything that's missing.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-1-workshop

Diana likes the quiet moments best, and plenty of good readers agree with her. Sam's fall is a good model for choosing where to add. He didn't add details everywhere. He picked the most important moment in his scene and slowed down right there. Think of an instant replay in a sports game. They don't replay the whole game in slow motion, just the big play. Your story's big play is probably a moment when your character feels something strong, like fear or embarrassment. That's the spot to stretch. When you're done, check your word count. It should go up today. If it didn't change, go back and find your spot.
