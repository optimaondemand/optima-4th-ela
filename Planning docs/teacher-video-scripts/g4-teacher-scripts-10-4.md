# Lesson 10.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 20 · A Good Imagination Gone Wrong. Guide this lesson: Marilla. Each script plays right after Marilla's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 20 to 30 seconds each, since this is a review day.

## Lesson video · g4ela-10-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week ten, and Chapter 20 wraps up five chapters about courage. You already know Anne can be brave about real dangers. Today, her own imagination is what scares her. You'll finish the week's word work with some checking activities, read Chapter 20, and look back across the whole week in your answers. Then in Writer's Workshop, you'll name the one quality that makes your main character special.

## Welcome · g4ela-10-4-welcome

Think back to when Anne first came to Green Gables and told Marilla about Katie Maurice, the friend she pretended lived in the bookcase glass. Imagining helped Anne get through some lonely years, so it's a real part of who she is. Keep that in mind as you read. When the strongest part of a person starts causing trouble, it's worth asking why, and you'll ask that same question about your own character at the end of the lesson.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-10-4-dol

Something new shows up in today's sentence. Earlier this week, you fixed sentences by adding a period and a capital to split two thoughts. Today's model from the novel has someone talking. The words "said Marilla" sit outside the quotation marks because they aren't part of what she says. You'll need this in your own story whenever a character speaks, and one more rule writers follow is that each new speaker gets a new paragraph.

## Before the word work · g4ela-10-4-guide1

You've practiced every one of this week's word skills already, so today is all about checking. That's the same thing you'll do when you reach the Edit step in your Writer's Workshop tracker. Your own mistakes are the hardest to spot, because your brain fills in what you meant to write. So slow down and point to each word with your finger as you go, even the ones that look fine.

## Morphology · g4ela-10-4-morphology

You've known these four "not" prefixes since the start of the week, so this match should feel familiar. One extra thing to notice in the examples box: that's why words like illegal and irregular have double letters. One l comes from the prefix and the other comes from legal. Knowing that will save you from a spelling mistake that plenty of grown-ups still make.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-10-4-vocabulary

By now you've decoded these four words and matched them to their meanings, so today you get to show that you own them. A good test is whether you can use a word for someone outside the book. Who do you know who's inexorable once they've decided something? If you pick the word parts option, notice that incapable and inexorable share a "not" prefix from this week's morphology.

## Grammar · g4ela-10-4-grammar

You sorted these verb forms on the first day and found them in longer sentences on day two, so you know this rule. One reminder: the s on a verb works the opposite way from the s on a noun. One dog, two dogs, but one dog barks, two dogs bark. And two names joined by "and," like Anne and Diana, count as more than one. Every sentence on this page comes from Chapter 20.

## Spelling · g4ela-10-4-spelling

You've worked with this rule all week: c and g usually turn soft before e, i, or y, and stay hard before a, o, or u. You also hunted down rule breakers like girl. Think back to day one, too, when you saw that the rule works letter by letter, so a long word like gorgeous can have a hard sound and a soft sound. Use the rule box on this page as your guide.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-10-4-guide2

All week, the Growing-Up Watch box has followed Anne's courage, from walking up to Mrs. Barry's door in Chapter 16 to running through the snow in Chapter 18. Both times the danger was real. As you read today, notice how the author shows Anne's fear without simply naming it, through what she pictures and how she moves. That's something you can borrow when your own character gets scared.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-10-4-wgrd

All week you've compared how different people see the same event: Anne's tea party, Minnie May's croup, and now the Haunted Wood. Today's page adds one new idea. The story stays close to Anne on purpose, so you'll feel what she feels and forgive her more easily. That doesn't mean she's always right. A strong reader enjoys being close to Anne and still asks what someone else would say.

## Before you read · g4ela-10-4-read

This chapter closes out our week. Picture Prince Edward Island more than a hundred years ago, with no streetlights and no flashlights. The woods between farms were truly dark at night, so a spruce grove after sunset would feel spooky to almost anyone, even without a ghost story. Notice that this chapter can make you laugh and feel sorry for Anne at the same time, and keep both feelings handy for the Pause and Think stop.

## Before you write · g4ela-10-4-guide3

Every question today asks for a quotation from the book. You practiced quotation marks in today's Daily Oral Language, so use them the same way here. Copy the words exactly as they appear in Chapter 20. Then add a sentence after the quote that explains what it shows. That explaining sentence is the part most people skip, and it's what turns an okay answer into a strong one.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-10-4-chapter

For question two, a strong answer admits the other side has a point before it makes its own case. You could start with "Some readers might think..." and then give your reason. For question three, look at the hint under it. It gives you a beginning and an ending to compare, so use words like "at first" and "by the end" to make the change easy to see.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-10-4-workshop

You've built a lot this week: a first sentence, then Scene 1, then Scene 2 with a thought inside your kid's head. Today is lighter. Look at Sam's gift and problem for Mateo; they're one quality seen from two sides. Picture a really honest kid. People trust her, but one day her honesty could hurt a friend's feelings. Find your own kid's version of that.
