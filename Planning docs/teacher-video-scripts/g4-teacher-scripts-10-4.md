# Lesson 10.4 teacher video scripts

Anne of Green Gables, Ch. 20 · A Good Imagination Gone Wrong. Guide this lesson: Marilla. Each script plays right after Marilla's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 30 to 60 seconds each.

## Lesson video · g4ela-10-4

Hi, friends. This is the last lesson of week ten. All week, Anne has meant one thing while the grown-ups around her saw something else: at the tea party, on the night Minnie May was sick, and again today. In Chapter 20, the thing that gets Anne into trouble is her own imagination, the very thing we've loved about her since she stepped off the train at Bright River. Today you'll finish the week's word work, read Chapter 20, and then look back across all five chapters to see how much Anne has changed. In your writing, you'll name the one quality that makes your main character special, because next week that quality is going to cause trouble.

## Welcome · g4ela-10-4-welcome

Before you read, think back to when Anne first came to Green Gables. She told Marilla about the imaginary friends she had when she was lonely, like Katie Maurice, the girl she pretended lived in the bookcase glass. Imagining was how Anne got through some very hard years. So her imagination isn't a silly habit. It's something that once helped her survive. That's what makes today's chapter interesting. As you read, think about why the strongest part of a person can also be the part that trips them up. It happens to real people, too, not just to characters in books.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-10-4-dol

Let's go one step past the punctuation in this sentence. The words "said Marilla" are called the speaker tag. They aren't part of what she says, so they stay outside the quotation marks. When the tag comes after the words, like it does today, the comma goes inside the closing quotation mark. But if you flip it around and put the tag first, so it says, Marilla said, then the comma moves to right after "said," before the opening quotation mark. You'll want this for your own story, especially if it opens with someone talking, the way Sam's does. One more rule writers use: every time a different person starts talking, start a new paragraph.

## Before the word work · g4ela-10-4-guide1

Today's checking activities are practice for something you'll do with your own writing. If you look at the Writer's Workshop tracker, two of the steps coming up are Revise and Edit. Editing is exactly this: finding what's wrong without wrecking what's right. And it's harder with your own writing than with someone else's, because your brain already knows what you meant and fills it in for you. A trick editors use is to slow way down and point to each word as you read it. Try that today, even on the ones that look fine at first glance. It feels slow, but it's how careful readers catch mistakes.

## Morphology · g4ela-10-4-morphology

Have you ever wondered why "illegal" has two l's and "irregular" has two r's? This week's prefixes explain it. One l belongs to the prefix, and the other belongs to the word "legal." The same goes for the two r's in "irregular" and the two m's in "immature." Hundreds of years ago, people said "in-legal," and over time it got smoothed into "illegal" because it's easier to say. Spelling these words with just one l or one r is one of the most common mistakes, even for grown-ups. Now that you know where the second letter comes from, you won't make it. Watch for this week's vocabulary word "inexorable," too. It uses one of these prefixes.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-10-4-vocabulary

You'll know you really own a word, and don't just recognize it, when you can use it to describe someone who isn't in this book. Try it with "inexorable." Who do you know who just won't budge once they've made up their mind? And if you choose the option about word parts, notice that two of this week's words, "incapable" and "inexorable," start with the same "not" prefix you studied in morphology. The word parts work and the vocabulary work this week fit together. If you choose the option about finding a similar word in Chapter 20, look for a moment where someone refuses to change their mind, or finally does. Those moments are where these words live.

## Grammar · g4ela-10-4-grammar

One part of this rule confuses almost everyone at first, so let's clear it up. When you add an s to a noun, it means more than one, like one girl and two girls. But when you add an s to a verb, it goes with just one person: she walks, he hopes. So the s works the opposite way on verbs. That's why sentences about groups of people are where most mistakes happen. One more tricky case: two names joined by "and," like Anne and Diana, count as more than one, so the verb doesn't get an s. "Anne and Diana walk to school." Keep that in mind as you check each sentence.

## Spelling · g4ela-10-4-spelling

This week's rule connects to a bigger spelling pattern. The k sound can be spelled with a c or a k, and English has a pattern for choosing. Before a, o, or u, it's almost always c, like in "cat," "cord," and "cup." The letter k usually shows up before e or i, like in "keep" and "kite," because a c there would make an s sound. The same thinking works for the j sound. A g before e or i often says j, like "gentle," but a word starting with a j sound before a, o, or u needs a j, like "jam" and "jump." And remember that this rule says "usually." Words like "get" and "girl" break it.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-10-4-guide2

While you read, watch how the author writes about fear. She never just writes, "Anne was scared." She shows you what fear does to Anne's body and mind: what she imagines, how she moves, and what happens to her voice and her teeth. Writers call this showing instead of telling. Notice how many details the author uses to make you feel Anne's fear without ever simply naming it. Keep that in mind for your own story. Remember Sam's opening? He made you feel Mateo's nerves without ever using the word "nervous." When your character is scared later on, you'll want to do exactly what this chapter does.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-10-4-wgrd

When a story follows one character this closely, we say it's told from that character's point of view, even though the narrator isn't Anne herself. Authors do this on purpose. If you feel what Anne feels, you'll care about her and forgive her more easily. But that doesn't mean Anne is always right. It means the author wants you close to her. A good reader enjoys being on Anne's side and also stops to ask what someone else would say. This also matters for your own writing. Ask yourself, whose head is your story in? Most of your stories stay close to your main character, and now you know why that works.

## Before you read · g4ela-10-4-read

A little background will help you picture this chapter. Anne lives more than a hundred years ago, on a farm on Prince Edward Island in Canada. There were no streetlights and no flashlights. At night, the woods between farms were truly dark, and the only light might be a lantern or the moon. So walking through a spruce grove after dark would feel spooky to almost anyone, even without a made-up story about ghosts. As you read, notice that the author wants you to feel two things at once. Part of this chapter is funny, and part of it makes you feel sorry for Anne. Keep track of the moments that make you smile and the ones that make you wince.

## Before you write · g4ela-10-4-guide3

Every question today asks you to use a quotation, an exact phrase from the book. And today's Daily Oral Language already showed you how. When you copy Anne's or Marilla's words, put them in quotation marks exactly as they appear in the book. Then don't just drop the quote in and move on. After it, write a sentence that explains what the quote shows. That explaining sentence is the one most people skip, and it's the one that makes your answer strong. A pattern you can use is to say what you think, give the quote, and then explain why it proves your point.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-10-4-chapter

For question two, whether Marilla is fair or cruel, the strongest answers do something extra. They admit the other side has a point. You might write something like, "Some readers might think Marilla is cruel because Anne is so scared, but I think she's being fair because..." That shows you thought about it carefully. For question three, you need to remember Anne at the beginning of the week. If you've forgotten the details, look back at the titles of Chapters 16 through 20. They'll remind you what happened in each one. Then use words like "at first" and "by the end" to show the change clearly.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-10-4-workshop

Let's look at one more example of a gift that becomes a problem, so you can see how this works outside of Sam's story. Picture a kid who is really honest. That's a gift. People trust her. But one day her best friend asks if she likes her new haircut, and she doesn't. Now her honesty could hurt someone she cares about. That's the kind of moment we're looking for. In Anne's case, it's her imagination. Your character's gift should be something readers will like about them, and the problem should come from the same place. Name the gift today, and next week you'll write the scene where it gets them into trouble.
