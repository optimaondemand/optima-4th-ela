## Welcome · g4ela-12-2-welcome

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-2-wgrd

Marilla knows how big Anne can make the smallest thing, and she isn't wrong about that. When you're not sure where to start a summary, try a frame that a lot of readers use. Somebody wanted something, but something got in the way, so they did something, then it ended a certain way. Somebody, wanted, but, so, then. If you can fill in those five words for a chapter, you have a solid summary, and the fun extras have nowhere to go. Try it once for Chapter 27 and once for Chapter 28. Also watch for your own opinion sneaking in. A summary tells what happened. What you think about Anne's choices belongs in your answers on the Assignment tab.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-2-dol

Poor Marilla. I don't think she enjoyed seeing that sentence in print, even if she can't argue with it. A sentence with two complete thoughts and nothing between them is called a run-on, and you'll find plenty of them in first drafts, even in grown-up writing. Today's fix uses a period, but there's a second way, and the model sentence shows it. Look at how the author joins two thoughts with a comma and the word "but." You could do the same with "and" or "so," as in, "The rain stopped, so we went outside." Both fixes are correct. Writers pick a period when they want each thought to land on its own, and a comma with a joining word when the two thoughts belong together.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-2-morphology

New company is a nice way to put it, Marilla. Those little beginnings will feel friendly soon enough. Once you know these roots, you'll start spotting them everywhere. ## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-2-vocabulary

I'd love to know who Marilla had in mind for those sentences, but I doubt she'll ever tell us. The ending of a word can help you narrow down a match. Words that end in "ly," like "quietly" or "gently," usually tell how someone does something. Words that end in "ity," like "curiosity," name a thing or a feeling. Words that end in "ent," like "patient," usually describe a person. That won't give you every answer, but it can rule out a meaning that doesn't fit. When you've matched three, don't let the last one be automatic. Check it as carefully as the first. One of these words shows up in the Growing-Up Watch box on the Reading tab today, so see if you can find it.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-2-grammar

Marilla wants every last one found, and she's right to be strict here, because these verbs hide well. Have you ever heard a little kid say, "I goed to the park"? Kids say that because they've figured out the regular rule and use it everywhere. The verbs that break the rule tend to be the oldest and most common words in English, the ones people have said so often for so long that they wore into their own shapes. That's why short, everyday verbs are where you should look hardest. They're easy to read right past. One tricky case to watch for is a verb that comes right after the word "to," like "wanted to swim." That one isn't in the past at all, even if the sentence is.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-2-spelling

I'm with Marilla on liking a rule you can count on, and this one is very steady. This week's quiet e shows up in more places than the six words on the page. The letter y can keep c and g soft, too. Ice becomes icy, and sponge becomes spongy, and the e can go, because the y does its job. On the other side, when a suffix starts with a consonant, the e almost always stays, as in peaceful or arrangement. A consonant can't make the c or g soft, so the e has to stay and do the work. When you write your Show What You Know, explain what the e does to the sound, not only which letter it is. Telling why shows you really understand the rule.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-2-guide2

## Before you read · g4ela-12-2-read

Marilla's opinion would be fun to hear, but she's right to let you make up your own mind first. Both chapter titles today have a story behind them. "Vanity and vexation of spirit" is an old phrase from the Bible. Vexation means being troubled or upset, so the title warns you trouble is coming. The second title comes from a long poem by Alfred, Lord Tennyson that Anne's class studied at school. In it, a young woman named Elaine, called the lily maid, dies of a broken heart, and her body is floated down a river in a boat. That's the scene Anne and her friends decide to act out. It sounds gloomy, but to Anne it's the most romantic thing in the world.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-12-2-rwm

Marilla wasn't even there, and she still read that boy perfectly. That tells you how well this sentence works. Look closely at the verb. Gilbert doesn't step into the boat or get into it. He springs. That one word tells you how fast he moves and how he's feeling, without the author ever naming the feeling. Then notice "an angry color." Most writers would say "red cheeks," but Montgomery tells you what the color means. Those are two small word choices, and they do a lot of work. For your Mimic sentence, spend the most time on your verb. "Stomped" and "tiptoed" would give you two very different people. Watch for strong verbs like that all through today's chapters.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-2-guide3

Marilla would make a strict teacher, but a fair one, and that advice is worth keeping. Before you start typing, look for the word "and" in each question. The first two questions each ask two things. The first one wants what her hair turned and what had to be done to fix it. The second wants how Anne saves herself and who rescues her. The most common reason a good answer loses points is that it only answers half. So after you write, go back and check off each part. Use today's reading move here, too. You don't need to retell the whole chapter to answer one question. Pick the part that answers it, say it clearly, and stop.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-2-chapter

Marilla saying no more is probably wise, since I doubt she wants to remember that afternoon. For the first question, notice that the fix doesn't happen all at once. ## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-2-workshop

A story as tidy as Marilla's kitchen is a high bar, but it's a good one to aim for. A useful test for deciding what to cut is to ask whether a detail ever comes back. Sam's sentence about Mateo's dad shows up once and then disappears, so it was really just in the way. If you have a sentence you love that doesn't fit, copy it into your notebook before you delete it. Now look carefully at Sam's moved sentences. Before the move, it said "He lay there." After, it says "Mateo lay there." When you move a sentence, a "he" or an "it" might not make sense anymore. After you move yours, read the lines before and after it to make sure everything still works.
