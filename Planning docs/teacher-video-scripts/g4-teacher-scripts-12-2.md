## Welcome · g4ela-12-2-welcome

Think back to the very first chapters of this book. On the buggy ride from Bright River, Anne told Matthew how much she hated her red hair. When Mrs. Lynde made fun of it, Anne stamped her foot and shouted at her. When Gilbert called her "Carrots," she broke her slate over his head. So her hair has been at the center of some of her biggest moments. That's why today's chapter matters so much to her. For Anne, changing her hair would mean getting rid of something that has hurt her since she was little. As you read, see if you can feel how badly she wants it, even when you can guess it won't go well.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-12-2-wgrd

When you're not sure where to start a summary, try a frame that a lot of readers use. It goes: somebody wanted something, but something got in the way, so they did something, then it ended a certain way. Somebody, wanted, but, so, then. If you can fill in those five words for a chapter, you have the bones of a good summary, and all the fun extras will have nowhere to go. Try it once for Chapter 27 and once for Chapter 28. One more thing to watch out for is your own opinion sneaking in. A summary tells what happened. What you think about Anne's choices belongs in your answers on the Assignment tab, not here.

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-12-2-dol

A sentence with two complete thoughts and nothing between them is called a run-on, and you'll find a lot of them in first drafts, even in grown-up writing. Today's fix uses a period, but there's a second way, and the model sentence shows it. Look at how the author joins two thoughts with a comma and the word "but." You could do the same with "and" or "so," as in, "The rain stopped, so we went outside." Both fixes are correct. Writers pick a period when they want each thought to land on its own. Also, take a second look at "wept." It works like "sleep" and "slept" or "keep" and "kept." Verbs that rhyme sometimes change the same way.

## Morphology · g4ela-12-2-morphology

Once you know these roots, you'll start spotting them everywhere, even outside of school. A tractor is a machine that pulls a plow or a wagon across a field. When you subtract, you pull a number away. A seaport is a place where ships carry goods in and out, and a report carries news back to someone. Think about the peddler in today's chapter, too. A peddler walks from house to house with everything he sells right there with him, a whole little store you can take on the road. As you match, don't stop at the root. Look at what each front part adds, because the same root can mean something quite different depending on what comes before it.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-12-2-vocabulary

The ending of a word can help you narrow down a match. Words that end in "ly," like "quietly" or "gently," usually tell how someone does something. Words that end in "ity," like "curiosity," name a thing or a feeling. Words that end in "ent," like "patient," usually describe a person. That won't give you every answer, but it can rule out a meaning that doesn't fit. Another tip is that when you've matched three, don't let the last one be automatic. Check it as carefully as the first. You'll also meet these words in Anne's own voice. One of them shows up in the Growing-Up Watch box on the Reading tab today. See if you can find it.

## Grammar · g4ela-12-2-grammar

Have you ever heard a little kid say, "I goed to the park"? Kids say that because they've figured out the regular rule and use it everywhere. The verbs that break the rule tend to be the oldest and most common words in English, the ones people have said so often for so long that they wore into their own shapes. That's why short, everyday verbs are where you should look hardest. They're easy to read right past. One tricky case to watch for is a verb that comes right after the word "to," like "wanted to swim." That one isn't in the past at all, even if the sentence is.

## Spelling · g4ela-12-2-spelling

This week's quiet e shows up in more places than the six words on the page. The letter y can keep c and g soft, too. Ice becomes icy, and sponge becomes spongy, and the e can go, because the y does its job. On the other side, when a suffix starts with a consonant, the e almost always stays, as in peaceful or arrangement. A consonant can't make the c or g soft, so the e has to stay and do the work. When you write your Show What You Know, explain what the letter does to the sound, not only which letter it is. Telling why is what shows you really understand the rule, and it's what a reader of your answer wants to see.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-12-2-guide2

Notice that the quote in this box comes from Anne herself. In it, she does something most people find hard. She names exactly what each mistake taught her. It's easy to say "I'll never do that again." It's much harder to say what you learned. But naming a lesson and living by it aren't the same thing. The real test is what she does the next time. Watch for that as you read, and keep checking whether she acts differently. A lot of chapters in this book work this way, where one scrape leads to one lesson. That shape could work in your own story. Give your character a mistake, and let it teach them something you can name in one sentence.

## Before you read · g4ela-12-2-read

Both chapter titles today have a story behind them. "Vanity and vexation of spirit" is an old phrase from the Bible. Vexation means being troubled or upset, so the title tells you this chapter is going to bring Anne trouble. The second title comes from a long poem by Alfred, Lord Tennyson that Anne's class studied at school. In it, a young woman named Elaine, called the lily maid, dies of a broken heart, and her body is floated down a river in a boat. That's the scene Anne and her friends decide to act out. It sounds gloomy, but to Anne it's the most romantic thing in the world. Knowing the poem will help you see why the girls thought this was such a wonderful game.

## Read Like a Writer · g4ela-12-2-rwm

Look closely at the verb in this sentence. Gilbert doesn't step into the boat or get into it. He springs. That one word tells you how fast he moves and how he's feeling, without the author ever naming the feeling. Then notice "an angry color." Most writers would say "red cheeks," but Montgomery tells you what the color means. Those are two small word choices, and they do a lot of work. For your Mimic sentence, spend the most time on your verb. "Stomped" and "tiptoed" would give you two very different people. This will help you later in the week, too, because on Day 3 of Writer's Workshop you'll swap weak words for strong ones in your own story.

## Before you write · g4ela-12-2-guide3

Before you start typing, look for the word "and" in each question. The first two questions each ask two things. The first one wants what her hair turned and what had to be done to fix it. The second wants how Anne saves herself and who rescues her. The most common reason a good answer loses points is that it only answers half. So after you write, go back and check off each part. And use today's reading move here, too. You don't need to retell the whole chapter to answer one question. Pick the part of the chapter that answers it, say it clearly, and stop. A short answer that hits both parts is stronger than a long one that wanders.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-12-2-chapter

For the first question, notice that the fix doesn't happen all at once. There's trying, and then there's what finally works. A strong answer shows that order with words like "first" and "finally." For the second question, keep the two people separate. Anne does something for herself before anyone helps her, so give her credit for that part before you get to the rescue. For the third question, the hard part is explaining the mistake to someone who hasn't read it. Use the Story Journey map to find it. The brooch is in Chapters 13 and 14, the Haunted Wood in Chapter 20, the liniment cake in Chapter 21, and the hair dye is today. Then copy her words exactly.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-12-2-workshop

A good test for deciding what to cut is to ask whether a detail ever comes back. Sam's sentence about Mateo's dad shows up once and then disappears, so it was really just in the way. If you have a sentence you love that doesn't fit, copy it into your notebook before you delete it. It might belong somewhere else. Now look carefully at Sam's moved sentences. Before the move, it said "He lay there." After, it says "Mateo lay there." When you move a sentence, the words around it change, so a "he" or an "it" might not make sense anymore. After you move yours, read the lines right before and after it to make sure everything still makes sense.
