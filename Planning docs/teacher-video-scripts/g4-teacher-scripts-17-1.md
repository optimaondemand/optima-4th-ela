# Lesson 17.1 teacher video scripts

Number the Stars, Day 1 · Ch. 5, Who Is the Dark-Haired One? Guide this lesson: Grete, the narrator. Each script plays right after Grete's audio clip in the same spot, so it adds to what she says instead of repeating it. About 40 to 60 seconds each. A gold 🎬 On screen line is a suggestion for that moment: click a button in it and that card goes up on your video. It is never read aloud.

## Lesson video · g4ela-17-1

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Hi, friends, and welcome to week seventeen. This is one of the most important chapters in the whole book, so give yourself time with it. The picture beside me is a real police notice from 1940. ## Welcome · g4ela-17-1-welcome

Courage with no time to think. What a question to carry through a chapter that starts with two friends whispering in bed. Notice how cozy the opening is. Ellen and Annemarie talk about school plays, and Ellen even plays a Dark Queen. Keep that in your mind, because Ellen is going to need those acting skills sooner than she expects. When an author makes a chapter feel this safe at the start, a careful reader starts to wonder why. Take your time with the first pages. You'll want to remember exactly how calm the night felt before the knock.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-17-1-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

Keep one finger ready. That's my favorite kind of reading, the kind where you're hunting while you go. Proof gets stronger when you add one more question: what could have gone wrong? Saying Annemarie is brave is only a start. Saying she is brave because she acted while soldiers were right outside the door, when they could have walked in at any second, is evidence. The risk is what turns an action into courage. When you mark the spot, jot one or two words in your journal about the danger, too. That little note will save you time when you write.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-17-1-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence B · Show the fix]

Quotation marks and a question all in one sentence. That's a lot to keep track of, so I'll fix a different one first while you watch, and then you try yours. When someone asks a question out loud, find the exact words they said. Only those words go inside the quotation marks, and the question mark belongs to them, so it sits inside too. Put a comma before the quote, and start the spoken words with a capital letter. Now look at your sentence. Find the spoken question, and watch for two words that work together to describe one person.


## Before the word work · g4ela-17-1-guide1

Nobody telling you the rule first? That's how real detectives work. They study the clues before anyone hands them an answer. For grammar, try reading each little sentence out loud twice, once the way it's written and once with a different person-word, like changing she to they. Listen to what happens to the action word. Your ear will catch the change before your eyes do. For spelling, cover the beginning and the end of a long word with your fingers. Whatever is left peeking out in the middle is the word underneath. Then decide whether it's a real word.


## Morphology · g4ela-17-1-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

A beam of light reaching across the water to another country. I couldn't wait for you to see this one. The roots in this game are very old. They come from Latin, the language of ancient Rome, and they're still hiding in words you use every day. Before you tap, read the meaning on the card and ask one question: is this word about trusting something, or about reaching out? Once you decide that, the root almost picks itself. Take your time with each one, and watch the far shore as you go. Every right answer changes the picture a little.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-17-1-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: discreet · tense]

A word about keeping quiet and a word about how fear feels. Those two fit this chapter almost perfectly. Discreet is about moving or speaking so gently that nobody notices you. Think about how softly Annemarie opens the bedroom door, just a crack. Tense is how your body feels when you're afraid. Your shoulders pull up and you hold your breath. As you read, look for moments when a character is being discreet, or feeling tense, even though the book never uses those exact words. Writers often show a feeling without naming it. Your job is to name it for them.


## Grammar · g4ela-17-1-grammar

What decides it? A grammar question that sounds like a mystery is my favorite kind. Try a pair from my own kitchen. She bakes. They bake. Say them both out loud and listen to the ends of the action words. Now make up two more pairs of your own, one with he and one with we. Line up everything you notice in your notebook before you write anything down as a rule. Don't decide too fast, either. Something that seems true in one sentence should still be true in every sentence on the page. If it isn't, keep looking.


## Spelling · g4ela-17-1-spelling

Short words wearing extra pieces. That picture will stick with me. Be careful with words that only look built, because some are tricky. Under starts the same way as lots of built words, but it isn't a short word wearing anything. It's just under. So before you tap any word, ask Grete's question and answer it out loud. If you can't find a real word hiding underneath, leave it alone. Then look at the words you did tap and sort them in your head. Where did the extra pieces go? That sorting is how you'll find the rule.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-17-1-copia

The bones stay the same while the feeling changes. That's the whole secret of this activity. Today's sentence is about the necklace, and it's a big moment, so your verb matters. Pulled is fine, but it's soft. Think about what Annemarie really does to that little chain with soldiers just outside the door. For your detail about where or how, think about what Annemarie can hear right then. Write all three versions in your notebook. Then read them in a whisper and circle the one that makes your heart beat a little faster. There's no wrong choice, as long as you can say why.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-17-1-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 1]

Stepping on that paper without a word. Grete's story gave me goose bumps. Fast courage is so interesting to watch. Annemarie isn't the only one who acts fast in this chapter, though. Papa does something very quick, too, when an officer gets suspicious. As you read, keep a little list of everyone who acts before they have time to think it through. Beside each one, jot what they did. You might be surprised how long your list gets, and how many different kinds of quick courage you find on one dark night. Keep that list handy for the questions after you read.


## Before you read · g4ela-17-1-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Boots, voices, doors. This is a chapter you hear as much as see. One detail to watch for when the soldiers come: Mama turns on a lamp. Electricity was strictly rationed, so even that light feels startling to Annemarie. Then notice what Mama checks right away. It's the blackout curtains, the same kind of window covering this notice ordered. Even in a frightening moment, she still follows the rules that keep the family safe. Take it a page at a time, stop at each pause, and keep going all the way to the very last line. That last line matters more than it looks.


## Before you write · g4ela-17-1-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

Hands, photographs and a palm. Grete's list is a good map of where to look. The question about the photographs is the trickiest one. Reread the part where Papa takes out the album, and pay attention to what Annemarie realizes with, as the book says, an icy feeling. Ask yourself why Papa tears the pictures out instead of handing the officer the whole album. When you write your answer, name what Papa does and what Mama says, because the question asks about both of them. Then add one sentence about how fast it all happens.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-17-1-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

A chain, a photograph, a mark on a hand. Small things carrying big feelings is exactly what the last question is about. Close your eyes and picture the very end of the chapter. Then ask yourself what it means that Annemarie held on so tightly for so long. When you answer, start with what she sees. Then add a sentence that begins, This shows that. That second sentence is where your thinking lives. A strong answer to this question doesn't need to be long. It needs to be thoughtful. Use the exact words from the book if you can, inside quotation marks.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-17-1-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

A stone in your shoe. That's exactly how a dropped-in quote feels to a reader. To plug a quote in smoothly, start the sentence with your own words and slide the quote in at the end. You could write, Annemarie tells Ellen to hold still and warns her, "This will hurt." My words carry the reader right up to the quote, so it doesn't land with a thud. Try one of your own with a short quote from Chapter 5. Read it out loud. If it sounds bumpy, add a few more of your own words in front until it flows.
