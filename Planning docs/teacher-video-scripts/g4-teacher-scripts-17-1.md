## Welcome · g4ela-17-1-welcome

Courage with no time to think. Let's hold on to that question all week. Before you start, notice how the chapter opens. Ellen and Annemarie are giggling about school plays and acting. Ellen even pretends to be a Dark Queen. Keep that in mind, because Ellen is going to need those acting skills sooner than she expects. When a chapter starts out cozy, a careful reader starts to wonder what's coming.


## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-17-1-wgrd

[ON SCREEN: Reading move]

Keep one finger ready. I love that. Here's how to make your proof even stronger. When you find the moment, ask one more question: what could have gone wrong? Saying Annemarie is brave is a start. Saying she is brave because she broke the necklace while the soldiers were right outside the door, and they could have seen it, is evidence. The risk is what turns an action into courage.


## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-17-1-dol

[ON SCREEN: Sentence A · Show the fix]

Grete mentioned a question asked out loud. Here's the trick for where the question mark goes. Ask yourself what the soldier said, and only what he said. That part is the question, so the question mark goes inside the closing quotation mark, right at the end of his words. Then look at dark haired. Those two words work together to describe one person, so a little hyphen hooks them into one describing word.


## Before the word work · g4ela-17-1-guide1

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule · Spelling rule]

Noticing first is how real detectives work. They look at the clues before anybody hands them the answer. Here's a tip for grammar today. Read each sentence out loud with he or she, then again with they. Listen to what happens to the action word. Your ear will catch the change before your eyes do. For spelling, try covering the beginning and the end of a long word with your fingers. What's left peeking out is the base word.


## Morphology · g4ela-17-1-morphology

[ON SCREEN: Word parts]

Welcome to Across the Water. I'm excited about this one. Here's something fun. Two of this week's vocabulary words are hiding in today's game. Credible has the believing root, and intend has the stretching root. When you intend to do something, your mind stretches toward it. So once you know cred and tend, you already know a little bit about two of your new words before you even flip the cards.


## Vocabulary · g4ela-17-1-vocabulary

[ON SCREEN: discreet · tense]

Two of these words fit this chapter almost perfectly. Discreet means careful not to draw attention. Think about how quietly Annemarie opens the bedroom door, just a crack. Tense is how your body feels when you're afraid. Your shoulders go tight and you hold your breath. As you read, watch for moments when a character is being discreet, or feeling tense, even though the book never uses those exact words.


## Grammar · g4ela-17-1-grammar

[ON SCREEN: Grammar rule]

Here's a sentence from my own kitchen. She bakes. They bake. Did you hear it? With one person, like he or she, the action word gets an s at the end. With they, it doesn't. It's funny, because usually adding s means more than one. Here it works the other way around. Once you notice that, you'll start seeing it in every sentence you read.


## Spelling · g4ela-17-1-spelling

[ON SCREEN: Spelling rule]

Grete's question is the key. If I take the pieces off, what word is left? Try it with unhelpful. Take off un. Take off ful. What's left is help, and help is a real word all on its own. Be careful with words that only look built. If you take off the pieces and the word left over isn't a real word, it doesn't count.


## Say It Three Ways · g4ela-17-1-copia

The sentence today is about the necklace, and it's a big moment, so your verb matters. Pulled is fine, but it's soft. Think about what Annemarie really does with that little chain. Does she pull it, or does she yank it, or rip it, or snap it? Each one feels different. For your detail about where or how, think about the sounds outside the bedroom door. Then read all three versions aloud and pick the one that makes your heart beat a little faster.


## Before you open the book · g4ela-17-1-guide2

[ON SCREEN: Stop 1]

Grete's story about the woman on the tram gave me goose bumps. Fast courage is so interesting. Here's what I want you to notice in this chapter. Annemarie isn't the only one who acts fast. Papa does something very quick, too, when the officer gets suspicious. While you read, keep a little list of everyone who acts before they have time to think it through. You might be surprised how long it gets.


## Before you read · g4ela-17-1-read

[ON SCREEN: Chapter picture]

Here's one detail to watch for near the beginning of the night. When the soldiers come, Mama turns on a lamp. Electricity was strictly rationed, so the light feels startling. Then look at what Mama checks right away. It's the blackout curtains, the same kind of covering this notice ordered. Even in a frightening moment, she's still following the rules that keep the family safe. Then keep reading slowly, all the way to the very last line.


## Before you write · g4ela-17-1-guide3

[ON SCREEN: Q2]

The question about the photographs is tricky, so here's a hint. Look closely at what is written at the bottom of each page in the album. Annemarie realizes something with, as the book says, an icy feeling. Ask yourself why Papa tears the pictures out instead of handing the officer the whole album. Once you see it, you'll understand how clever and how fast Papa had to be.


## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-17-1-chapter

[ON SCREEN: Q3]

Small things carrying big feelings. That's exactly what the last question is about. The mark in Annemarie's hand is something you can picture. Close your eyes and imagine it. Then ask yourself what it means that she held on so tightly. When you answer, write what she sees first. Then add a sentence that starts with, This shows that. That second sentence is where your thinking lives.


## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-17-1-workshop

[ON SCREEN: Today’s focus]

A stone in your shoe. I'm going to remember that one. Here's a way to plug in a quote smoothly. Start your sentence with your own words, then slide the quote in at the end. For example, you could write, When the soldiers come, Annemarie tells Ellen to hold still and warns her, "This will hurt." See how my words carry the reader right into the quote? Try one of your own. Read it out loud. If it sounds bumpy, add a few more of your own words in front.
