## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-2-wgrd

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-2-dol

Matthew's glad she wrote home, and I would be too. A letter from someone you miss is the best kind of mail. Look closely at the word must in the model sentence. Some helping verbs can never stand alone. You can say I have a dog, but you can't say I must a dog. Words like must, will, can, and should always need a main verb after them. That main verb stays in its plain form, too. We say she must write, never she must writes or she must wrote. That's a common slip when writers hurry. Anne was excited when she said her line, but the punctuation still has to stay calm and correct.

## Morphology · g4ela-14-2-morphology

Matthew calls himself no scholar, but that seed idea of Anne's is one of the best ways I've heard to describe a root. Today's match mixes roots with the little prefixes that steer them. The root ject always means to throw, but the prefix tells you which way. Re- throws it back, pro- throws it forward, and e- throws it out, which is why some machines have an eject button. The page also hides a bonus root in manuscript. Manu means hand, and you can find it in manual and manicure. In today's chapter, Anne reads her essay aloud at the ceremony. Before typewriters, every essay like hers was a manuscript, written out by hand, line by line.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-2-vocabulary

## Grammar · g4ela-14-2-grammar

I'd never thought of helping verbs as neighbors holding up a beam, but Matthew's picture fits. The trickiest helpers are the ones that can also work alone. Have, has, and had can be helpers, but sometimes they're the main verb. In My dog has a bone, has is the main verb, because there's no other verb in the sentence. In My dog has found a bone, has is helping found. So you can't just memorize a list. You have to look at what comes after the word. Watch for a little word that slips in between, like never in I had never seen snow. Never isn't a verb at all. Try finding each main verb first, and then look to its left for its helpers.

## Spelling · g4ela-14-2-spelling

Matthew can bring almost anything back to the farm, and he's got a point about fences. When you type, the computer will underline some spelling mistakes, but it can't catch them all. If you write planed when you meant planned, nothing gets underlined, because planed is a real word. A carpenter planes a board to make it smooth. So your own eyes still matter. One quick check works on any word with an ending. Cover the ending with your finger and check the base word. Then ask whether the base word plus this week's rule gives you exactly that spelling. The Show What You Know box also asks about an exception, so be ready to explain what a word does instead.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-2-guide2

Matthew thinks he only walks along beside her, but that quiet company matters to Anne more than he knows. The box on this tab says something that might surprise you. Maybe it was the last night of a vacation, or a birthday when everyone you love was in one room, and you thought, I want to remember this. Near the end of this chapter, the writer steps out of the story to speak straight to you, the reader. When you reach that part, slow down and notice how it makes you feel.

## Before you read · g4ela-14-2-read

I love picturing Matthew sitting perfectly still through that whole essay. That's how a quiet person shows he's proud. A few words in this chapter might be new. A commencement is a graduation ceremony, the day students finish at a school and celebrate together. At Queen's, some students read essays aloud in front of everyone. You'll also see the results posted on a board. Back then there were no phones or emails, so students crowded around a list on the wall to find out how they did. When you reach the Pause and Think stop, read Matthew's words a second time, slowly. There's nothing to write there, so give yourself time to just think about what he means.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-2-guide3

Matthew likes to tease Anne about her big words, but you can tell he's fond of every one of them. Your three Reading tab answers come along with everything you submit, so take a second look at your Mimic sentence. That pattern uses a semicolon, and a semicolon is worth understanding. It joins two complete sentences that belong together so closely you don't want a full stop between them. Each side has to be able to stand on its own. That connects to today's Daily Oral Language. The sentence you fixed there was two sentences jammed together, and a period was one good fix. A semicolon would have been another. Check that both halves of your Mimic sentence could each be a sentence by themselves.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-2-chapter

You can hear how much Matthew meant what he said, and it makes me want to read that part again. For question one, the hint tells you where Anne planned to go. ## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-2-workshop

I'm with Matthew. A two just shows you where the loose rail is, and that's useful to know. Scoring your own work is hard because you already know what you meant. So for each row, find proof in your story before you give yourself a number. For Word Choice, point to one sentence that's specific and vivid. If you can't find one, that row is probably a two. Look at what Sam did in Scene 2. He swapped a plain phrase for one you can actually picture, and the length barely changed. That's usually what moving from a two to a three looks like. You pick one row, but you name two changes inside it. Anne's work was judged at Queen's this week. Today you get to judge your own.
