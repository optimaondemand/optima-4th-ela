## Welcome · g4ela-14-2-welcome

This week we finish two things at once. Anne finishes her year at Queen's, and you finish the whole book. Endings like that can feel mixed up. Think about the last day of a school year. You're proud of what you did and excited for summer, but you might also miss your teacher or the kids who sat near you. Both feelings can be true, and neither one cancels the other out. Coming home to Green Gables means something very different now than it did when Anne was eleven and nobody was sure she could stay. As you read, notice the small moments where Anne seems to take in how much has changed, in her and in the people around her.

## What Good Readers Do · g4ela-14-2-wgrd

Wishes are a smart thing to watch, because a character doesn't always say them out loud. Sometimes you figure out what Anne wants from what she works hard for, or from what she'd hate to lose. The third step in the box asks what grew the wish, and that's where your thinking can get really good. Wishes don't change all on their own. Think about who believed in Anne along the way, like Miss Stacy, who started the Queen's class. And notice that a new wish doesn't have to erase the old one. Anne can want something bigger and still love Green Gables. Try this with the main character in your own story, too. What did they want in scene one, and what do they want by the end?

## Daily Oral Language · g4ela-14-2-dol

Look closely at the word must in the model sentence. Some helping verbs can never stand alone. You can say I have a dog, but you can't say I must a dog. Words like must, will, can, and should always need a main verb after them. And there's a rule about that main verb, too. It stays in its plain form. We say she must write, never she must writes or she must wrote. That's a common slip when writers hurry. In the sentence you're fixing, look for those helper and main verb pairs, and check that each sentence you end up with has its own person doing something. Anne was excited when she said her line, but the punctuation stays calm and correct.

## Before the word work · g4ela-14-2-guide1

All four of these activities are review, and that fits this week perfectly, because this is publish week. Look at the spelling paragraph further down. The page calls it a student draft, and it's exactly the kind of writing you'll be checking in your own story before anyone else reads it. ## Morphology · g4ela-14-2-morphology

Today's match mixes roots with the little prefixes that steer them, so notice how much work the front piece does. The root ject always means to throw, but the prefix tells you which way. Re- throws it back, pro- throws it forward, in- throws it in, and e- throws it out, which is why some machines have an eject button. The page also hides a bonus root in manuscript. Manu means hand, and you can find it in manual, a job you do with your hands, and in manicure, taking care of your hands. In today's chapter, Anne reads her essay aloud at the ceremony. Before typewriters, every essay like hers was a manuscript, written out by hand, line by line.

## Vocabulary · g4ela-14-2-vocabulary

The word sanctifying shows up again today on the Reading tab, inside a long sentence near the end of the chapter. When you get there, you'll already know it, and that will help you notice how serious that sentence sounds. The other three you can find in everyday life. Have you ever heard of a consolation prize? Think about who usually gets one and why it might have that name. Ambition is a word grown-ups use about people with big plans, and Anne has had plenty of big plans lately. If two words keep getting mixed up for you, try putting each one into a sentence about somebody you actually know. Real people help words stick better than definitions do.

## Grammar · g4ela-14-2-grammar

The trickiest helpers are the ones that can also work alone. Have, has, and had can be helpers, but sometimes they're the main verb. In the sentence My dog has a bone, has is the main verb, because there's no other verb in the sentence. In My dog has found a bone, has is helping found. So you can't just memorize a list of helpers. You have to look at what comes after the word. Another sneaky thing is a little word that slips in between the helper and the main verb, like never in I had never seen snow. Never isn't a verb at all, so it doesn't get tapped. Try finding each main verb first, and then look to its left for the helpers.

## Spelling · g4ela-14-2-spelling

When you type your story this week, the computer will underline some spelling mistakes for you, but it can't catch them all. If you write planed when you meant planned, nothing gets underlined, because planed is a real word. A carpenter planes a board to make it smooth. So your own eyes still matter, especially in publish week. One quick check works on any word with an ending. Cover the ending with your finger and look at what's left. Then ask yourself whether the base word plus the rule would give you exactly this spelling. Try that on each word in the paragraph. The Show What You Know box also asks about an exception, so be ready to explain what a word does instead of following the usual pattern.

## Before you open the book · g4ela-14-2-guide2

The box on this tab says something that might surprise you. Part of growing up is noticing a happy time while it's still happening. Little kids don't usually do that. They just have fun, and that's fine. But you might already know the feeling. Maybe it was the last night of a vacation, or a birthday when everyone you love was in one room, and you thought, I want to remember this. The box also says the writer does something unusual near the end of this chapter. She steps out of the story to speak straight to you, the reader. When you reach that part, don't rush past it. Afterward, come back to the question at the bottom of the box about your own character's good evening.

## Before you read · g4ela-14-2-read

A few words in this chapter might be new. A commencement is a graduation ceremony, the day students finish at a school and celebrate it together. At Queen's, some students read essays aloud in front of everyone. You'll also see the results posted on a board. Back then there were no phones or emails, so students crowded around a list on the wall to find out how they did. Picture how nervous that would make you, looking for your name with a whole crowd around you. When you reach the Pause and Think stop, read Matthew's words a second time, slowly. Then think about why the author chose to end the chapter so close to them. There's nothing to write at that stop, so give yourself time to just think.

## Before you write · g4ela-14-2-guide3

Your three Reading tab answers come along with everything you submit, so take a second look at your Mimic sentence before you move on. That pattern uses a semicolon, and a semicolon is worth understanding. It joins two complete sentences that belong together so closely you don't want a full stop between them. Each side has to be able to stand on its own. That connects to today's Daily Oral Language, too. The sentence you fixed there was two sentences jammed together, and a period was one good way to fix it. A semicolon would have been another. Before you submit, check that both halves of your Mimic sentence could each be a sentence by themselves. If one half can't, it needs a comma or a rewrite instead.

## Chapter Thinking · g4ela-14-2-chapter

For question one, the hint tells you where Anne planned to go. Think about why she wanted to hide, and then what happened before she got there. Your answer will be strongest if it shows how fast her feelings flip in that one moment. For question two, think back to Chapter 2, when Matthew drove to Bright River expecting someone else entirely. Then look at what he tells Anne as they bring the cows home. He says part of it more than once. Ask yourself why a quiet man like Matthew would repeat himself. For question three, answer only with what the line itself tells you. You don't need to guess what comes next. Focus on why an author would give a reader a warning early.

## Writer's Workshop · g4ela-14-2-workshop

Scoring your own work is hard because you already know what you meant. So for each row, try to find proof in your story before you give yourself a number. For Word Choice, point to one sentence that's specific and vivid. If you can't find one, that row is probably a two. Look at what Sam did in Scene 2. He swapped a plain phrase for one you can actually picture, and the length barely changed. That's usually what moving from a two to a three looks like. Then read the directions closely. You pick one row, but you name two changes inside it, so the fix stays focused and still counts. Anne's work was judged at Queen's this week. Today you get to be the judge of your own.
