## Her voice

Real lines from the book:

- “It does seem as if it was the end of everything, doesn’t it?”
- “Oh, we have had jolly times, haven’t we, Anne? It’s dreadful to think they’re all over.”
- “I do wish I was going in with you. Wouldn’t we have a perfectly elegant time?”
- “I’ll be haunting the post office Wednesday.”
- “You old darling, it’s perfectly splendid to see you back again.”
- “Anne, you’ve passed … passed the very first—you and Gilbert both—you’re ties—but your name is first. Oh, I’m so proud!”
- “When I saw the pass list I just rushed over like a wild thing.”
- “If it were me I know I’d go crazy with joy. I am pretty near crazy as it is.”
- “Oh, you’ll pass all right. Don’t worry.”

**How she sounds:** bright, breathless, loyal, a little giggly; she isn’t going to Queen’s, and once or twice that shows. Tics used: “simply”, “perfectly”, “dreadful”, “Oh, Anne”, running everywhere. She promises not to spoil the pass list and very nearly does.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 62 words):

> Oh, it’s Diana, and I’ve run all the way, so you must forgive me for puffing. The Entrance is over and Anne is home, and now everybody is simply waiting for the pass list. Waiting is dreadful. Anne says she’d rather go to sleep and not wake up until it comes. I say we ought to haunt the post office. We are.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello, it’s Diana! This is the chapter where the pass list comes out, and I simply can’t keep still. The question is whose pride matters most to Anne when she sees her name. First there’s word work: find the verbs that only join, and the comparing words that went wrong. Then read it for yourself. I promised I wouldn’t spoil it, and I won’t. Nearly.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> The box says one word isn’t enough, and I think that’s perfectly fair. If somebody asked me what this chapter is about, I’d say success and be done. But that’s only the topic. What does the chapter say success is for? Watch who Anne thinks of first. I know who I’d run to. I ran to Anne, didn’t I?

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> Anne said the top sentence the minute she got home from town, and I agree with every word, except perhaps for Orchard Slope. Find the joining word, and the two that mean the very most. The bottom sentence has two names wanting capitals, and two thoughts squeezed together with nothing between them. Give them each a little room.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four bits of word work, and two of them are tapping games with more than one right answer hiding. Don’t stop at the first one you find. That’s what I always do, and then I miss the rest and have to start over. Take each sentence one word at a time. Miss Stacy says slow and careful wins, and she’s generally right.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> A matching game with yesterday’s two roots and two new little front parts. I always mix up the one that means in with the one that means down or away. So I say a real word out loud first, one I know, and listen to what it means. Try it. It’s ever so much easier than staring.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> The same four words as yesterday, but no cards this time, so it’s all up to you. If you get stuck, picture somebody you know who fits the meaning. Jane Andrews fits one of them perfectly. Nothing rattles her. If nobody you know fits, try another meaning. It’s like choosing a dress, really.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Two sentences, and the joining verbs are hiding in with the doing ones, the way I hide behind Anne when Josie Pye comes along. There’s more than one. I always find the first and go skipping off, and then I have to come back. Ask of each verb, could I watch somebody do it? If not, try putting was in its place and see if the sentence still holds.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> This one’s a bit of a trick. Most of the comparing words are right, and you’re only to tap the ones that are made wrong. Say each one out loud. If it sounds like something a very little child would say, it probably is wrong. Count the beats in the plain word, too. That always tells.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about who you want to tell first when something good happens. I know who Anne told, because I was there, running behind her across the yard. I never once thought she’d do anything else. I’d have done the very same thing, only with more shrieking. Read, and watch where her feet take her.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 32

> Most of this chapter is waiting, and Anne’s letter to me from town, which I’ve read at least eleven times. Notice how frightened she is, and how she still manages to cheer up Moody Spurgeon. Then the pass list. There’s one stop, at the end. Before you get there, keep asking why she wanted it so badly. It wasn’t only Gilbert.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> That sentence is my favorite in the whole chapter. Look how it names two people and then goes back for one of them, as if it couldn’t help it. That little dash is doing such a lot. When you write yours, name two people, then pick one out on purpose. Your reader will know at once who matters most.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now the writing part. I always want to put everything in, every lovely little thing, and Miss Stacy always crosses most of it out. Answer just what the question asks, and show where in the chapter you found it. If you use somebody’s exact words, put them in quotation marks. Then read it over once before you say it’s finished.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about the very words I said when I burst in, so do look at them closely. I was rather out of breath. The second asks about Anne’s other reason for working so hard, the nobler one. The third is about Matthew. He hardly ever says anything, so when he does, it’s worth listening to.

## `diana\g4ela-13-2-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Dialogue today, which is my favorite part of anybody’s story. Read every line out loud, the way I read Anne’s letter. Quotation marks around the words people say, a comma before the part that tells who said it, and a capital to start. If a line sounds like nobody would ever say it, change the words too, not just the marks.
