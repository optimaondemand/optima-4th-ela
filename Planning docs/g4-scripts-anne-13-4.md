## Her voice

Real lines from the book:

- “Oh, Marilla, it’s just lovely. … I don’t believe you ought to be so kind to me—it’s making it harder every day for me to go away.”
- “I declare, my recitation has made you cry, Marilla. Now, I call that a positive triumph.”
- “I’m not a bit changed—not really. I’m only just pruned down and branched out.”
- “I suppose I won’t feel so much like a cat in a strange garret when I get acquainted, though.”
- “I won’t cry. It’s silly—and weak—there’s the third tear splashing down by my nose.”
- “It’s nicer to be miserable!”
- “Oh, it’s delightful to have ambitions. I’m so glad I have such a lot.” (all Ch. 34)
- “Somehow I don’t want to use big words any more. … Miss Stacy says the short ones are much stronger and better.” (Ch. 31)

**How she sounds:** still Anne, still romantic, but sixteen and catching herself: she reaches for a grand phrase, then trims it, because Miss Stacy says short words are stronger. She never quotes her own key line from the chapter (that’s Question 1), and she doesn’t say what happens on the last page.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 68 words):

> It’s Anne. I’m to go to Queen’s in three weeks, and Marilla has brought me the loveliest pale green stuff for an evening dress, which I don’t need at all, and Matthew keeps buying more. I used to say I was in the depths of despair about everything. Now I’m only very excited and a tiny bit frightened, which is much more sensible for a girl of sixteen.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello, it’s Anne, and today’s question is about me, which feels very strange to say out loud. Who am I really, when everything is changing? I’ll try not to make a speech of it. First there’s word work, and you’ll be hunting for mistakes. Then you read about the day I leave Green Gables. Then you decide what stays the same. I’m still deciding myself.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is about me, and I wish it weren’t quite so true. Lonesomer. Isn’t that a lovely, sad word? Find the joining verb, and the word that compares. The bottom sentence needs a stop in the middle and two capitals, one of them mine. I’d be grateful if you gave it back. A girl’s name ought to stand up straight, even when she’s homesick.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> This is the last day of word work this week, and two of the four ask you to catch a mistake. Here’s a secret I learned at the Entrance: some of the sentences are perfectly right, and tapping those costs you. So don’t tap because you’re nervous. Tap because you’re sure. I wish someone had told me that about geometry.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Matching again: the two roots and the two little front parts. I adore roots. A word that means to look can grow into something grand to stare at, and a word that means to build can end up meaning to tear down, depending on what you put in front. Say a word you know aloud before you match. Then ask which part is doing the work.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> There’s a little menu of ways to show what you know, and you only pick one. I would want to do all five, but I’m learning that choosing is a skill too. Pick the one you could do best, not the one that sounds grandest. I’ve learned that the hard way. Then use the words as if they were your own. They are, now.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Six sentences, each telling you what kind of verb something is, and some of them are fibbing, which I used to do about geometry. Only tap the fibs. The test is simple: put was where the verb is and see whether the sentence still makes sense. I used to think every verb was full of action. Most of mine were, I suppose.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Six comparing words, and some are made all wrong, with too many endings piled on, or more stuck in front of a word that already has an ending. I used to pile on extra words exactly like that, to sound grand. Now I try to say a thing once, properly. Tap only the ones that say it twice, or say it wrong.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about knowing who you are. It sounds like something you’d find out all at once, like a fairy in a dell. It isn’t, I’ve discovered. It comes in little bits, and one of them came on Marilla’s lap, in the kitchen, in my new green dress. Read, and watch what I tell her. Then decide whether I was right.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> The box says a big idea has to hold up twice. I like that. It’s like trying on a dress in front of two different mirrors. So when you think you’ve found what this week is really about, take it back to another chapter and see if it still fits. If it doesn’t, it was only one page’s idea. Keep going until it fits both.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 34

> This chapter has a leaving in it, and a lonely little room, and Josie Pye, which is a very odd mixture. There’s one stop, at the end. Before you get there, watch what I hold onto and what I let go. And watch the last page, because something happens there that makes everything feel enormous again. I won’t say what. I’m trying to be less dramatic.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now the writing. I used to answer every question with three paragraphs and a poem. Miss Stacy taught me that the short way is stronger. So answer what’s asked, in plain words, and prove it with something from the book. If you quote me, quote me exactly. I chose those words very carefully, even the little ones.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question wants what I told Marilla, with the picture of the tree in it. The second is about the scholarship, and why I went to bed with my cheeks tingling. The third asks you to look back over the whole week. That one’s the hardest, and the best. Look for what I say out loud, and what you can only see.

## `anne\g4ela-13-4-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> The last edit is your ears. Read your whole story out loud, slowly, the way you’d recite at a concert, and wherever you stumble, something is wrong. Fix it. Then say what you’re proud of. That part felt like boasting to me at first. It isn’t. Knowing what you did well is how you do it again.
