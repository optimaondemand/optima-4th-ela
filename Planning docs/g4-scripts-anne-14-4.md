## Her voice

Real lines from the book:

- “I’m just as ambitious as ever. Only, I’ve changed the object of my ambitions.”
- “I don’t know what lies around the bend, but I’m going to believe that the best does.”
- “We haven’t been—we’ve been good enemies. But we have decided that it will be much more sensible to be good friends in the future.” (all Ch. 38)

**How she sounds:** Sixteen, still bright, trimming her grand phrases. She is at the end of her story, so she can look back at the train station and the gate, but she never answers Question 3 for the student. She asks them to choose their own early and late moments.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 69 words):

> It’s Anne. Marilla went to town to see the eye doctor, and when I came home she was sitting by the kitchen table with her head leaning on her hand. I knew something was wrong before she said a word. This is the last chapter of my story, at least the part you get to read. There’s a bend in it. I didn’t plan the bend. Nobody ever does.

## The clips, in page order

### welcome · `g4ela-14-4-welcome.mp3` · 65 words

Hello, it’s Anne. Today’s question is about two people choosing love over ambition, and what that teaches. One of those people is me, so I may not be perfectly fair. First there’s word work, and some of it is catching mistakes. Then you read the last chapter. Then you look back over the whole road, all the way from the train station to the gate.

### wgrd · `g4ela-14-4-wgrd.mp3` · 63 words

The box asks what changed in me, and what stayed exactly the same. I’ve thought about it a great deal, and I’m not the right person to judge. Nobody sees herself straight, not even in the best looking glass. So find one early moment and one late one, and set them side by side. Then decide for yourself. I’d honestly rather you did.

### dol · `g4ela-14-4-dol.mp3` · 57 words

The top sentence is Mrs. Lynde’s, and when she said it, I sprang straight to my feet. Find the helper and the word it helps. The bottom sentence is two sentences that ran together, which is exactly how I talked when I was eleven. Split it, and give everyone their capitals. Gilbert included. Especially Gilbert, just now.

### guide1 · `g4ela-14-4-guide1.mp3` · 59 words

The last word work of the book, and most of it asks you to catch mistakes. Some sentences are perfectly right, and tapping those costs you. So tap only when you’re sure. I spent years being sure about things I was wrong about. Gilbert, for one. So I’ve learned to look twice before I decide. You do the same.

### morphology · `g4ela-14-4-morphology.mp3` · 65 words

Matching again: the writing root, the throwing root, and two little pieces that go in front. I adore them, because they’re hiding in so many of the words I use. Before you match, say a word you know with that root in it. Then ask which piece is doing which job. It’s rather like working out who in a story is really doing the work.

### vocabulary · `g4ela-14-4-vocabulary.mp3` · 63 words

This one is a menu, and you only choose one thing from it. I would want to do all five, of course. But I’m about to give up something big, and I’ve found that choosing one thing and doing it well feels better than I expected. Pick the one you could do best. Then use the words as if they’d always been yours.

### grammar · `g4ela-14-4-grammar.mp3` · 55 words

A little paragraph about this chapter, and some of the sentences are missing a helper. Tap only those. Read each sentence out loud in your head. Your ear will catch a missing word before your eye does. Mine always has, even when my eyes were busy imagining something else entirely. Leave the good sentences alone.

### spelling · `g4ela-14-4-spelling.mp3` · 59 words

Six words, and some have their endings put on wrong. Tap only those. Look closely at the spot where the ending meets the word. That’s where every mistake in this list lives. I’m going to be a teacher now, you know, so I’ll be hunting for exactly this on a whole room of slates. Practice on these for me.

### guide2 · `g4ela-14-4-guide2.mp3` · 64 words

There’s a box here about the bend in the road. When I left Queen’s, my future looked like a straight road, and I thought I could see a long way down it. Then it turned. I didn’t choose the turn. I only chose how to walk around it. Read, and watch two people give something up. Then decide whether either of us lost anything.

### read · `g4ela-14-4-read.mp3` · 59 words

One stop, at the very end, with what I said to Marilla about the bend. Before you get there, watch the kitchen when Marilla comes home from town. Watch who gives up what, and why. And watch the hill, near the end. Something happens there that I’d been waiting five years for, though I didn’t know I was waiting.

### guide3 · `g4ela-14-4-guide3.mp3` · 65 words

The writing now. The last question asks you to look back across the whole book, and that’s a great deal to hold at once. Don’t try to hold all of it. Pick one early moment and one late one, and set them side by side. Two good details are better than ten hurried ones. Miss Stacy taught me that, and she was right, as usual.

### chapter · `g4ela-14-4-chapter.mp3` · 65 words

The first question is about what I gave up and what I planned instead. The second is about Gilbert and the school, and the hill. The third is the whole book at once. For that one, you might begin at the station, where Matthew found me sitting on a pile of shingles, and end at the gate. Everything in between is yours to choose from.

### workshop · `g4ela-14-4-workshop.mp3` · 61 words

Your story is finished. Today you read it out loud to someone. Go slowly, and pause where it matters. I used to recite so fast that nobody could follow me. Then write a little about how your writing has grown, and how I’ve grown, too. Be specific. Specific is always better than splendid, even though splendid is such a lovely word.
