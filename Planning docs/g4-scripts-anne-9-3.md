## `anne\g4ela-9-3-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello, it’s Anne Shirley, and I’m guiding you again, which is a great honour. Today’s question is how one bad choice grows into a bigger trouble, and you’re to follow the chain link by link. I know something about that, though I’d rather not say what yet. In word study you build the answers yourself instead of sorting them. And the reading move isn’t in the warm-up today. It’s waiting on the Reading tab.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The model has a list in it, and I adore lists. Look at what separates the things in the list, and at the little word before the last one. The sentence you’re fixing has three places and one person’s name. Write it out first, then put in what’s missing, one thing at a time. Read it aloud. If you run out of breath in the wrong spot, something is still missing.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Word study! Today you produce the answers yourself, so there’s nowhere to hide, which I find rather thrilling and slightly alarming. Four word parts to type, three of this week’s words to fit into sentences, four feeling words, and four single letters. In every game, read the whole sentence before you type into its gap. A gap on its own will fool you every time. Check each box before you move on.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Read the little description after each word before anything else. It tells you exactly where the part points: above, or too much, or under, or below. Then choose. If two parts seem to fit, say the whole word aloud both ways and trust your ear, because you’ve heard these words before, even if you didn’t know it. If you miss one, a line will tell you why. Read it, then try again.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Four words in the bank and only three gaps, so one gets left out, poor thing. Read each sentence to the end. Then ask what job the gap is doing. Is it describing a mouth, a feeling, or a temper? That narrows it wonderfully. Try your word in the sentence out loud. If it sounds foolish, it is. Leave the hardest gap for last and the bank will have done half your work.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Every answer is in the bank, and every one names a feeling or an idea, not a thing you could hold. I have a great many. Read the whole sentence and ask what the person is feeling, or what two people have between them. Then find the bank word for it. When you’ve finished, combine the two short sentences in the box. Put the feeling in as a noun. Don’t just glue them with and.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Two decisions each time. First, say the word and find the syllable that gets mumbled. The description tells you which one. Second, decide which letter is doing the mumbling. Your ear can’t tell you that. The sound is the same for all three letters, which I think is very unfair. So picture the word the way you’ve seen it in a book. If you’ve never seen it, guess, and read the line that comes back.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> Two more days till the picnic, and I have thought of very little else, which Marilla says is setting my heart on things. This week’s watch is about finding out what you’ll trade to get what you want. I want that picnic more than anything. As you read, keep two lists in your head: what somebody wants, and what they give up for it. Then decide whether the trade was worth it.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> Careful, now. Next is not the same as because. I get those mixed up when I’m upset. Before you start, get ready to list the order of things as they happen: first, second, third, and nothing else. Then take each pair and say them with because in the middle. If it sounds wrong, it was only next. When you find the real cause, it will be a feeling, not an event. It nearly always is.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 14

> Two stops, and mind the labels. The one printed first belongs at the end of the chapter, and the second belongs partway through, so take the partway one first. It asks whether getting what you want by telling somebody what they already believe really counts. The other you take when you’ve finished. Keep your book open beside you. The questions want the chain of events in order.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> That sentence is about Marilla, and I only saw the outside of it, which was quite bad enough. The author lets you see the inside. Before you write, read it once and put your hand where that feeling would go in your own body. When you write yours, give the feeling a verb that moves, and send it somewhere: a throat, a stomach, a pair of hands. Don’t name the feeling and stop.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Well, now you know the worst of me. Marilla thought I’d lost her brooch, and I hadn’t, and she shut me in my room until I confessed. So I confessed to something I never did, with a bridge in it. Then she found the brooch on her shawl, and I went to the picnic after all, and the ice cream was sublime. Now say what you noticed, and show it from the book.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question asks two things, what I said I did and what Marilla decided I’d done. Keep them apart, because I did keep telling her the truth. The second is about my reasoning, and it made perfect sense at the time. The third wants the chain right to the end, and the last link isn’t the brooch turning up. It’s what Marilla did after. Find it before you answer.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your spelling answer is saved, so here’s the connection. All week you’ve been dealing with the vowel you can’t hear, the lazy one that sounds the same however it’s spelled. Your ear can’t tell you which letter it is. You have to look. Well, my confession sounded exactly like the truth. Marilla couldn’t hear the difference either. She had to go and find the brooch to know. When you can’t hear it, look.

## `anne\g4ela-9-3-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you map your story: a beginning, three things that go wrong in the middle, and an end. Take my week. I tried on a brooch, and then three things made it worse, each one worse than the last, and it ended with Marilla saying she was wrong and me eating ice cream. Count your middle. One thing is a moment, not a story. Make the third the worst, and make the end change somebody.

**How he stays in character.** Eager and dramatic, in love with long words and big feelings, honest about what she did without asking for pity, and a little sheepish that a story got the better of her.

**What he knows, and when.** Before the reading she knows the picnic is two days off, that she has never tasted ice cream and Diana tried to explain it, that Marilla is baking her a basket, that there will be boats on the lake, and that Marilla has told her she sets her heart too much on things. She does not say she tried on the brooch until after the reading, and she never claims to know what Marilla is thinking. The accusation, being shut in her room, the made-up confession with the bridge, the brooch on the black lace shawl, Marilla admitting she was wrong, and the picnic with its sublime ice cream come out only in the RWM and Assignment-tab clips. She was at the picnic, so she can say what the ice cream was like. The RWM sentence is Marilla’s inner feeling, so Anne says she only saw the outside of it and the author showed the inside. She never hints at Ch. 15.
