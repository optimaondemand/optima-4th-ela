## The Copia card (Word Study tab, before the reading)

Seed sentence, exact from early in Ch. 11, one verb marked: *Anne clasped her hands and **looked** at the dresses.*

Three worked examples the card shows:

1. New opener: In the gable room, Anne clasped her hands and looked at the dresses.
2. Stronger verb: Anne clasped her hands and studied the dresses.
3. Added where-or-how: Anne clasped her hands and looked at the dresses, spread out side by side on the bed.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Marilla Cuthbert here, guiding you again. Today’s question is about wanting something and being told no. You’re to watch how the author shows the wanting and the disappointment in the very same breath. Keep hold of one thing you want yourself that somebody has called foolish. It’ll help you understand her. In word study there’s a new word part, the one that means above, or too much.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> Here’s how to go about it. Before you open the book, settle what you’re looking for: one thing the child decides to do, even a small one. When you find it, don’t move on. Back up a line or two and find what happened just before. That’s your cause. Then read a little past it and see who has to deal with what she did. Hold all three in your head together.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> Tap the coloured parts of the model before you touch the second sentence, and read what each one tells you. The one you’re fixing starts with a person, and a person’s name starts with a capital letter. Then it tells you where she is, and after that what she was doing. Look at what the model puts between those two parts. Write yours in your notebook, check it against the model, then tap the button.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Word study, and there’s a new word part this week. It means above, or across, or too much, and I dare say you’ll meet it more often than you’d like. Work through the four boxes in order. In each one, read the whole thing before you start tapping and typing. Most mistakes I’ve seen come from starting before you’ve looked. Check as you go, not at the end.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Read the four words at the top and press the button to see them cracked open. Then, in the game, cover the front of each word with your finger and read what’s left. Read the little description after it, too. That description is your clue: does it point above something, or beneath it? Type the part that fits. If you miss one, read the line that comes up before you try again.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Do the example at the top first, all three steps, even though the answer’s printed. That’s practice. Then for each of the three cards, read the book’s sentence, guess out loud, and only then tap. In the match at the bottom, do the words you’re sure of first and leave the hardest for last, when there’s only one meaning left. If you match wrong, a line comes up. Read it.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Three columns and nobody’s told you what they are. Good. Move the two words you’re surest about first, one to each side, and ask yourself what makes them different. Then find each one’s partner. The pair left over belong together for a reason of their own, so say what that reason is before you drop them. Then name all three groups in the box, and say how you told them apart.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Say each word out loud before you drag it, and say it the lazy way you would in a hurry. One syllable goes mushy. Find that syllable, then look at which vowel letter is sitting in it. That letter tells you the column, not the sound, because the sound is the same in all six. If you get one wrong, a line will tell you why. Read it before you try again.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-copia.mp3` — Word Study · Say It Three Ways (Copia)

> Here’s a plain sentence from before she’d said a word about the dresses. Say it three ways. First, put something at the front, a where or a when. Second, swap the marked verb for one that shows how she looked, since a person can look at a thing a dozen different ways. Third, tell us more about where or how. Keep it true to the picture. Change one thing at a time.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> The child starts Sunday school this week, and she’ll meet other children there, which she’s had little enough of. This week’s watch is friendship. I’ve never had a great deal of use for it myself, but I’ll say this: a friend is somebody you choose, and who chooses you back. As you read, notice whether she has anybody like that yet, and what it costs her that she hasn’t.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 11

> Two stops, and the first comes before she’s even left the house, so look for it early. It asks why a child would care so much about a thing I’ve called ridiculous. Don’t answer for me. Answer for her. The second stop is at the very end, and it’s about honesty. Ask yourself whether saying the true thing is brave or only careless, and have a reason ready either way.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Well. She went to Sunday school alone, since I had a headache coming on, and came home and told me it was horrid. Mr. Bell’s prayer was too long, Miss Rogerson asked a great many questions, and every other girl in the class had puffed sleeves. I ought to have scolded her. The trouble is, some of what she said I’ve thought myself for years. Your turn. Say what you noticed, and show me where.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about the dresses I made. Look at what she said when she first saw them, and at what she didn’t say. For the second, something happened on the road to church that nobody has told me about yet, so you’ll have to find it yourself. It’s small. Read slowly. The third quotes her exactly. Before you answer, count how many girls in that class had what she didn’t.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your morphology answer is saved, so let’s tie it up. This week’s new word part means above, or too much. Well, that child does everything too much, if you ask me. Too much imagination, too much feeling, too much talk. And yet I keep listening. Look back at the sentence you wrote. Does your word say something true about her world? If it doesn’t quite, you’ll know what to reach for next time.

## `marilla\g4ela-9-1-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you name a want and a wall. Make them small and real. That child wanted puffed sleeves, and I was the wall, and I had my reasons. Notice it wasn’t a grand want. It was sleeves. Then look at what the want tells you about the person: she wanted to look like the other girls. Pick your character’s want the same way. Something you could point at, that shows who they are underneath.

**How she stays in character.** Brisk and practical, proud of plain sewing and plain sense, and privately more in agreement with the child than she will admit.

**What she knows, and when.** Before the reading she has made the three dresses and refused the puffed sleeves (she hadn’t any material to waste on them, and thinks them ridiculous-looking anyhow). She knows Anne starts Sunday school this week and that she has had few children to play with. She has not yet had the headache that keeps her home, so before the reading she never says she stayed behind. Anne’s verdict on Sunday school, Mr. Bell’s long prayer, Miss Rogerson’s questions, the nine other girls with puffed sleeves and Marilla’s private agreement come out only in the Assignment-tab clips. She never learns about the flowers on the hat in this chapter (the book says so: Anne discarded the faded wreath in the lane, and Marilla was spared the knowledge for a time), so the chapter clip says plainly that nobody has told her and the student must find it. She never hints at Ch. 12.

**Checked against the novel.** Everything she mentions is in Ch. 11: three new dresses made up plain by Marilla herself, “I hadn’t any material to waste on puffed sleeves,” “I think they are ridiculous-looking things anyhow,” the sick headache that kept Marilla from Sunday school, “I didn’t like it a bit. It was horrid,” Mr. Bell’s long prayer, Miss Rogerson’s questions, “There were nine other girls in it. They all had puffed sleeves,” and Marilla being hampered by the fact that some of what Anne said she had thought herself for years. The copia seed, “Anne clasped her hands and looked at the dresses,” is an exact sentence from the opening scene. No clip reads the page, says a word part on its own, gives away an answer on the page, or names a time of day. Every clip is 40–75 words.
