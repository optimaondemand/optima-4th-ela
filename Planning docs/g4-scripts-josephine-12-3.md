## Her voice

Her lines in Ch. 29 (and Ch. 26, where she reads the story club’s tales and says she “had never read anything so amusing”):

- “So you’ve come to see me at last, you Anne-girl. Mercy, child, how you have grown! … But I dare say you know that without being told.”
- She suggests ice cream across the street to bring Anne back to “common life.”
- She laughs at Anne’s verdict on town life at breakfast.
- “Well, I hope you’ve enjoyed yourselves.” … “And you, Anne-girl?”
- To herself: “I thought Marilla Cuthbert was an old fool … but I guess she didn’t make much of a mistake after all.”

**How she sounds:** crisp, grand, dry, secretly amused; *I dare say*, *at my time of life*, *when I was young*, *the Anne-girl*. She wasn’t in Lover’s Lane or at Green Gables, so the RWM and the after-chapter pause are things she says she must be told.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in her voice (text only, 65 words):

> Josephine Barry, of Beechwood, in Charlottetown. You may remember me. I was jumped upon once, in the middle of the night, by two great girls, and I have not forgotten it. This autumn I have written to my niece and asked for both of them to come to town for the Exhibition. At my time of life one takes one’s amusements where one finds them.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Josephine Barry. I have had two girls from Avonlea in my house this week, which is two more than usual. Your question asks what Anne discovers about herself in town. I asked her very nearly the same thing myself, and she made me wait for the answer. First, some word work, where you build the words yourself. Then the chapter. Then three sentences. Not four.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is about me, and quite right too. I did keep them there all day. Count the capitals in it. A day of the week, a title, a name, and a show grand enough to deserve one. The bottom sentence is about the concert, and it has run two thoughts together, as young people do. Put a stop between them. And give the girl her capital.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Building, today. You type every answer yourself, and there is nothing to choose from. A missing root, a missing word, a doing word put into the past, and a piece added onto the end. When I was young we did this sort of thing on a slate with no help whatever. You have a box that checks your work. Say each one aloud first. Then type it.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Four words, and each has lost its back half. The front is there, and the meaning is there, so you are not entirely abandoned. Read each meaning slowly. Then ask yourself one question. Is something being carried here, or is something being pulled? There are only two roots to choose from. At my time of life, I find most questions come down to two choices.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Three sentences, three blanks, and four words to choose from. One word is left over, which is perfectly in order. Read each sentence to the end before you choose. Then say it aloud with your word in the blank. If it sounds like something a sensible person would say, keep it. If it sounds like nonsense, I dare say it is.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Four sentences, told as though they were happening now. Put each doing word into the past, where it belongs. None of them takes the usual ending, so do not try. Say yesterday in front of it, and listen. Your ear will tell you, if you let it. Then the box at the end asks for two sentences of your own about this chapter. Make them good ones.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Four words, and a piece to add to each. Two rules at once. First, does the word end with a soft sound before its quiet e? Then, what letter does the new piece begin with? Both answers decide whether the e stays. Write each one out in full before you check. Carelessness in spelling is like carelessness in anything. It shows.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There is a box here about learning from one’s mistakes. I have made a few myself, though I seldom admit it. Once I was very angry with two little girls for jumping on my bed. I meant to go home the very next day. Then one of them came to confess, and I unpacked. Watch for the moment Anne is glad about something she would once have sulked over. I was there, and I noticed.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> The box says most chapters lean on one moment. Find it, and the rest falls into place. Anne will tell you a great deal about horses, and balloons, and ice cream at eleven o’clock at night. Not all of it matters equally. As you read, ask which moment the chapter would collapse without. I know which one I would choose. Whether she would choose the same, I cannot say.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 29

> Two stops. The first comes when Anne gives her verdict on town life. I laughed, I am afraid. Think about what her answer tells you about her. The second comes at the very end, after she has left my house. I was not there for it, so you must tell me. Which part do you think she will remember longest?

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> I was not in that lane, of course. I do not keep cows. But that sentence tells me more about the Anne-girl than an hour of her conversation. The cows plod along. She dreams. And her head is full of a battle. Three things, and not one word about the weather. When you write yours, choose your three as carefully, and leave the sunset out.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now you write, and I shall be particular. Say what happened plainly. If you are asked why, give a reason, and point to the line in the book that proves it. Do not decorate. I have read a great many stories by Avonlea girls, and the decoration was never the best part. The best part was always the one true thing in the middle.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question asks for two things the girls did in town. I took them to most of them, so choose well. The second asks why the concert moved Anne so. She told me she did not see how she could ever go back to common life. I suggested ice cream. The third asks what she decided about town and country. Use her words, not mine.

## `josephine\g4ela-12-3-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you swap. A dull doing word for a lively one. A line anybody might say for one only that person would say. And one small action that belongs to your character and nobody else. The Anne-girl kissed my wrinkled cheek on the veranda. Nobody else would have dared. Diana certainly did not. That is the sort of small thing that tells you exactly who someone is.
