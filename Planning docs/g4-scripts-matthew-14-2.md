## His voice

Real lines from the book:

- “Reckon you’re glad we kept her, Marilla?”
- “Well now, I’d rather have you than a dozen boys, Anne. … It was a girl—my girl—my girl that I’m proud of.”
- “It’s only that I’m getting old, Anne, and keep forgetting it.” (all Ch. 36)

**How he sounds:** Shy, slow, “well now”, “I reckon”, farm pictures (plowing, barn raising, fences). He is proud and happy, and **nothing he says hints at what comes next.** He mentions folks fretting only because Marilla does fret in the chapter.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 62 words):

> Well now, it’s Matthew. Anne’s been at Queen’s all winter, and the results go up on the board today. I’m not much for crowds, but Marilla and I are going in for the commencement. I’ve had my good coat brushed. I reckon I’d go even if I had to sit next to a whole row of strangers. That’s just how it is.

## The clips, in page order

### welcome · `g4ela-14-2-welcome.mp3` · 66 words

Well now, it’s Matthew. Today’s question is about two feelings sitting side by side, glad and worried, in the very same day. I dunno much about books, but I know that feeling. First there’s word work, roots and helping words. Then you read about the day the results came out, and Anne coming home. Watch for the happy parts. And notice where folks are fretting, too.

### wgrd · `g4ela-14-2-wgrd.mp3` · 66 words

The box says you can tell how a person’s grown by what they wish for. When Anne first came, she wished she could stay. That was about all she wanted. I remember it pretty well, being the one driving the buggy. Today, listen for what she wishes for next. Then set the two wishes side by side. That’s quite a road to come down, I reckon.

### dol · `g4ela-14-2-dol.mp3` · 66 words

The top sentence is Anne’s, in all the fuss. She wanted to write home right away, and I’m mighty glad she did. Find the little helper word and the word it helps. The bottom one runs two sentences together, the way folks talk when they’re in a hurry. Split it, and give the names their capitals. Anne always did care about a name being done right.

### guide1 · `g4ela-14-2-guide1.mp3` · 62 words

Four bits of word work. One is matching, one is remembering, and two ask you to tap. Tapping is easy to do too fast. I’ve found most things go better slow. Plowing, for one. Read every word before you tap it, and ask yourself if you’re sure. If you’re not, leave it be a minute and come back. No harm in that.

### morphology · `g4ela-14-2-morphology.mp3` · 64 words

Two old roots again, the writing one and the throwing one, and two little pieces that go on the front. Match each piece to what it means. If you get stuck, think of a word you know and say it slow. I’m no scholar. But Anne says the old roots are like seeds. You plant one, and a whole row of words comes up.

### vocabulary · `g4ela-14-2-vocabulary.mp3` · 66 words

Same four words as yesterday, and now you match them up without the sentences to lean on. That’s how you find out what stuck. If one won’t come, try to remember the sentence it lived in. Anne says words have a home sentence. I wouldn’t know about that. I just know one of these words sounds an awful lot like her, once she’s set her mind.

### grammar · `g4ela-14-2-grammar.mp3` · 61 words

One long sentence, and you’re to tap every helper in it. There’s more than one, and some stand right next to each other. Don’t tap the words doing the real work, just the ones helping. It’s a bit like a barn raising. Lots of folks hold the beam, but only one swings the hammer. You want the folks holding the beam.

### spelling · `g4ela-14-2-spelling.mp3` · 62 words

Here’s a little paragraph somebody wrote too fast, and three words in it got their endings put on wrong. Tap only those. The rest are fine, and tapping a fine one won’t help you any. Say each word slow in your head, and look at the spot where the ending joins on. That’s where the trouble usually hides. Same as with fences.

### guide2 · `g4ela-14-2-guide2.mp3` · 63 words

There’s a box here about knowing a good evening while you’re still in it. Anne’s better at that than I am. She notices the sky and the pointed firs and the old Snow Queen, and she says so out loud. I just walk along beside her. Read slow today, the way she would. Take your time over the happy parts. They’re worth it.

### read · `g4ela-14-2-read.mp3` · 61 words

There’s one stop, near the end, when Anne and I are bringing the cows in. She says something about the boy we sent for, and I say something back. You’ll get to it. Before then, there’s the morning the news comes out, and the commencement. I was there for that. I didn’t say a word till she’d finished reading her essay.

### guide3 · `g4ela-14-2-guide3.mp3` · 55 words

Now the writing part. I’m not much of a talker, so here’s the short of it. Read each question till you know what it wants. Answer it plain, and show where in the book you found it. You don’t need fancy words. Anne’s got enough of those for the both of us. Just true ones.

### chapter · `g4ela-14-2-chapter.mp3` · 58 words

The first question is about how Anne heard her news. The second is about something I said to her, coming back from the pasture. I meant every word of it. The third question is about a line the writer put in, not anybody in the story. I’ll leave that one to you and the book. Read it slow.

### workshop · `g4ela-14-2-workshop.mp3` · 58 words

Today you score your own story, row by row, then fix just one thing. Just one. I like that. When a fence needs mending, you don’t tear the whole fence down. You find the one rail that’s loose, and you put it right. Be honest with your scores. Nobody’s going to think less of you for a two.
