## Lesson 7.1 — Ch. 1, *Mrs. Rachel Lynde Is Surprised*

### `welcome\g4ela-7-1.mp3` — **re-record** (opening "Good morning" removed)

> Here is today's question, and it is worth carrying with you: how does a writer make a place feel real — a place that only exists on paper? By the end of today you will be able to tell the difference between the way the narrator describes Avonlea and the way Mrs Rachel Lynde sees it. Those are not the same thing. You will also get to grips with some stubborn plurals — the ones that refuse to simply add an S.

### `turn\g4ela-7-1-1.mp3` — opens Word Study · recorded

> You've just been thinking about the difference between the narrator's voice and a character's voice. Hold on to that. Today's chapter is called *Mrs Rachel Lynde Is Surprised* — and somebody in it is going to be very sure she is right. Before we get there, a little work on words. It's shorter than you think, and you're going to need it.

### `turn\g4ela-7-1-2.mp3` — opens Reading · recorded

> Now open the book. Chapter one is mostly one woman at a window, watching a neighbour drive past in his good suit. Almost nothing happens. Everything gets set up. Read for what Mrs Rachel notices — and then notice what the narrator tells you that she never could. Those are two different voices, and they are both on the page.

### `turn\g4ela-7-1-3.mp3` — opens Assignment · recorded

> So. Matthew drove past in his best suit, and Mrs Rachel could not let it go. Now you write about it. Don't rush to the answer you think somebody wants — say what you actually noticed, and point to the line that made you think it. And at the bottom, your own story is waiting. Today it's about voice, which is exactly what you just watched Montgomery do.

## Lesson 7.2 — Ch. 2, *Matthew Cuthbert Is Surprised*

### `welcome\g4ela-7-2.mp3`

> Today's question is about voice. Everybody you know sounds like themselves — so what is it, exactly, that makes Anne sound like Anne and like nobody else? By the end of the lesson you will be able to point to what makes her voice hers. Keep yesterday's plurals close as well. You will meet them again in the word work, before you open the book.

### `turn\g4ela-7-2-1.mp3` — opens Word Study

> Same skill as yesterday. Narrator, and character. Chapter two has the same shape of title as chapter one — somebody else gets surprised. Watch who. First, though, words. You're still on the word parts that mean *with*, or *together*, and today they matter more than usual, because chapter two is about two people meeting who were not expecting each other at all.

### `turn\g4ela-7-2-2.mp3` — opens Reading · **new**

> Matthew goes to the station expecting a boy and finds a girl who does not stop talking. Listen to what she does on the drive home. A road under the trees, a pond, a view — Anne will not let any of them stay ordinary. Ask yourself why a girl with nothing of her own would want to give everything a name. And keep an eye on quiet Matthew while she does it.

### `turn\g4ela-7-2-3.mp3` — opens Assignment

> Matthew went for a boy and came home with Anne, and said almost nothing the whole way. Now the questions. As you answer, think about how much you knew about Matthew even though he barely spoke. That is voice. And it is what you're building into your own character today — not a description. A way of being in the world.

### `wgrd\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — What Good Readers Do

> Here is a trick worth keeping for the rest of the year. A narrator can stand beside a character and say nothing, and you end up seeing through that character's eyes without noticing. Today you ride with Matthew. When a plain road suddenly sounds beautiful, stop and ask whose idea that was. The narrator's, or Anne's? The answer tells you whose eyes you are borrowing.

### `dol\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Daily Oral Language

> Today's broken sentence is somebody talking, and people talking are the hardest thing to punctuate. Something is missing around the words she says. Something is missing inside a word that has been squeezed short. And a little word at the very front wants a pause after it. Look hard at the model first. Everything you need is sitting in it.

### `morph\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Morphology: Prefixes Review

> You know these two word parts already. Both mean with, or together. Here is why they are worth the practice. Once you can spot them, a long word stops being a wall and becomes two pieces you can take apart. And today's hidden surprise is the one that means bread. A companion, long ago, was simply someone you shared your bread with.

### `vocab\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Vocabulary

> Yesterday you pulled these four words apart. Today you only have to remember them. That is the real test of a word: not whether you understood it once, but whether it comes back when you need it. Match them quickly if you can. If one hesitates, notice which one. That is the word to use in a sentence of your own today.

### `grammar\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Grammar: Irregular Plural Nouns

> Most words make a plural the easy way. You add a letter and move on. The ones in today's sentence refuse. They change their middle, or grow an old ending, or keep a shape from hundreds of years ago. That is why readers trip on them and writers get them wrong. Hunt all three down, and don't stop at the first.

### `spelling\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Spelling: Plurals: -s, -es, y to ies

> Here is the secret behind this week's spelling. You can usually hear the rule before you see it. Say a word out loud with more than one, and listen. If your mouth needs an extra beat at the end, or if a y at the end of the word has to change, that word is doing something more than the ordinary. Let your ear find them.

### `read\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Read: Ch. 2

> One chapter today, and most of it happens in a buggy. Two people who have never met sit side by side for eight miles, and only one of them talks. As you read, keep a rough count in your head of how many things Anne gives a new name to. Then, when the chapter ends, ask yourself what a girl who names everything might be hoping for.

### `chapter\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Chapter Thinking

> These three questions look simple, and they are, on the surface. Underneath them is a harder one. Is Anne as happy as she sounds? She talks and talks, and she names everything she sees. But go back to the very first thing she says to Matthew at the station. When you describe her, don't stop at what she looks like. Think about what she was afraid of.

### `wordconn\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Word Study Connection (Grammar)

> Your grammar answer is already saved, so this is just a moment to connect it. All week you have been looking at words that break the ordinary rule. They were supposed to behave one way, and they turned out another. Today's chapter does the same thing with a person. Matthew was expecting a boy. The rule said a boy. What was waiting at the station was Anne.

### `workshop\g4ela-7-2.mp3` — Writer’s Workshop: Week 7 · VOICE

> Here is one move to try from today's chapter. Anne does not just see a road. She gives it a name that nobody else would have thought of. That is voice, working on a single word. Before you write your two or three sentences, decide one thing your character would call by a name of their own. Then let them say it out loud.

## Lesson 7.3 — Ch. 3 & 4, *Marilla Cuthbert Is Surprised; Morning at Green Gables*

### `welcome\g4ela-7-3.mp3`

> Here is today's question, and it is a hard one. When somebody gets bad news, how does a writer show us how they feel — by telling us outright, or by letting us watch what they say and do? By the end of today you will be able to use the details on the page to explain how Anne takes the worst news of her life. Watch closely. It happens quickly.

### `turn\g4ela-7-3-1.mp3` — opens Word Study

> Three chapters, three surprises. Mrs Rachel, then Matthew, and today Marilla. Montgomery is showing you one arrival through three different pairs of eyes, and asking you to notice that they don't agree. Keep that with you. First the word work — it's the same pattern you've been building all week, and today it should start to feel easy.

### `turn\g4ela-7-3-2.mp3` — opens Reading · **new**

> Two chapters today, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is an evening when Anne hears news she cannot imagine her way out of. The second is a morning at a window. Watch what her imagination does in each one. Does it rescue her, or can it not reach that far? The house stays exactly the same between the two chapters. Only Anne changes.

### `turn\g4ela-7-3-3.mp3` — opens Assignment

> You've just watched Anne go from the worst evening of her life to a morning at a window, with the same house around her both times. That is the whole idea of perspective, in two chapters. Carry it into your answers. And carry it into your own story, where your character is starting to sound like somebody.

## Lesson 7.4 — Ch. 5, *Anne's History*

### `welcome\g4ela-7-4.mp3`

> Last lesson of the week, and the question turns backwards. Where does a person come from — and how much does their past decide who they are now? By the end of today you will be able to explain how Anne's history shaped the girl who arrived at Green Gables. She has been telling you all week without saying it plainly. Today she says it plainly.

### `turn\g4ela-7-4-1.mp3` — opens Word Study

> Last lesson of the week. You've had three chapters of people being surprised by Anne. Today, Anne explains herself. Before that, one more pass at the words you've been working on all week. It's the last time you'll see them before you use them for real.

### `turn\g4ela-7-4-2.mp3` — opens Reading · **new**

> Chapter five is a quieter one. Anne tells Marilla the story of her life so far, and it is not an easy story. Listen to how she tells it. She is not asking you to feel sorry for her. Notice the moments when she stops reporting what happened and starts telling you what she imagined instead. She has been doing that all week. Today you find out where it came from.

### `turn\g4ela-7-4-3.mp3` — opens Assignment

> That's the week. You met Anne through three people who didn't expect her, and then you heard her tell it herself. Four versions of the same girl. Now finish your answers, and give your own character a turn to speak — because by now you know that a person sounds like somebody long before they tell you who they are.
