**Tics used:** *Well now* to start; *I dunno* and *I guess* where a teacher would be sure; sentences that trail off and get finished by the next one; he understates himself (“I’m no scholar”) and overstates Anne; he says the practical thing and stops. He never explains grammar, because he can’t. One tic per clip.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in his voice (text only, 63 words):

> Well now. It’s Matthew. Marilla told me the whole business about the tea, the night it happened, and I’m ashamed to say I laughed. Not at Anne. At the size of the trouble a bottle can make. She’s gone back to school since, on her own account, with her books on her arm and her mouth set. I dunno what she’s planning. Something.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Well now, it’s Matthew, and I’m your guide for these two chapters. The question up there says the hard part of Anne’s past turns into a gift. I dunno how yet. I can believe it, though. Anne never had much, and she made the most of every bit. So as you go, keep an eye on who freezes and who moves. Then there’s doing words to find, and some c’s and g’s that don’t behave.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> Marilla and I look at Anne and see two different girls. Marilla sees the mistakes. I see, well, I dunno what I see. The girl. And it’s not that one of us is wrong. It’s what each of us has lived through. So do what the box says. One sentence for each person, then ask what they’ve been through that makes them see it that way. That’s the whole gap, most times.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> Marilla does the grammar in this house. But I can hear a sentence that runs on too long, the way I can hear a wagon that needs grease. The bottom one is two thoughts with no stop between them. Read it out loud and stop where you’d take a breath. That’s your period. Then the next word stands up straight with a capital letter. Anne’s name wants one too.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Well now. Four boxes, and I’m no scholar, so I’ll say what I’d do. Two are matching, tap one thing, then tap its partner. Two are tapping words in a row, and in those you’re hunting, so don’t tap the first thing that looks likely. Read the whole row first. The spelling one is looking for the words that break the rule, not the ones that keep it. Easy to get that backwards.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Four ways to say not. I dunno why a word needs four, one would do me. But there’s a rule about which one goes where, and the rule is in the little words in brackets. Say those out loud, and look at the first letter of each one after the chunk comes off. That letter is what you’re matching, not the meaning. The meanings are all the same. That’s the trick of it.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> You met these four yesterday. Well now, I forget words the day after I learn them, so here’s what I do. Say the word, then say the meaning, and listen to whether they sit together. If it sounds like two strangers, it probably is. Do the pair you’re surest of first, and then the next, and the last two nearly match themselves. And if you get one wrong, read the little line that comes up.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> There’s more than one doing word in that sentence, and I’d have read it and just nodded. So go slow. Ask of each word, is somebody doing this right now? A name isn’t. A little word like and isn’t. Then check the end of each one you tapped against who’s doing it, one person or more than one. I dunno the rule for that. Anne does. You will, by the end of the week.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Most of those words do what the rule says. One or two don’t, and those are the ones you tap. So say each word, listen for the hard sound or the soft one, then look at the letter after the c or the g and ask if the rule would have guessed right. When it wouldn’t, that’s your word. Leave the good ones be. Tapping a good one costs you.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> The box says courage is doing the hard thing while you’re still afraid. Well now, I’m not a brave man. Going up those stairs to talk Anne into apologizing, when Marilla had told me not to interfere, that was about my limit. But I went. So watch for that. Not somebody who isn’t scared. Somebody who is, and does the thing anyway. And watch what the other people do while she’s doing it.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 17 & 18

> One stop, at the end of the first chapter, and it asks whether a person can enjoy being sad. Well now, Anne can enjoy pretty near anything. When you get there, ask yourself if she’s only sad, or something else along with it. Then read the second chapter straight through, no stopping, and keep count of who moves and who stands still. That’s what the reading move is about.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> Well now, that’s Anne all over. Sorry and thrilled in the one breath. The sentence works because the although part holds the feeling you’d expect, and the main part holds the one you wouldn’t. Cover the first half with your finger and it’s just a girl enjoying herself. Put it back and she’s a person. When you write yours, put the ordinary feeling first, and save the surprising one for the end.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> I went to Carmody for the doctor and there wasn’t one, so I went clear to Spencervale, and it was the small hours when we got back. And it was over. Anne had done it. I walked her home and she talked the whole way, and I let her. Now it’s your turn to say what you noticed, and I dunno that you need to say much. Just say it with the book open.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about that farewell, and I wasn’t at the spring, but I heard about it at supper. Look at the words Anne chose and ask why a person picks those. The second is where her skill came from. She tells Diana, not the doctor. Find that. The third is about Mrs. Barry. I was doing chores when she came. Marilla heard it. Quote the moment, not the feeling.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your grammar answer is saved. When I tell about that night it all comes out in the past, went and drove. The page wanted you to find the doing words that happen now, and I dunno why, but it makes a story feel closer. Take the verb you tapped, ask who’s doing it, and ask whether the ending fits that person. Then look at your own writing and try one sentence in the now.

## `matthew\g4ela-10-2-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Sam’s boy wants to make the team, and part of him wants to skip it. Well now, I wanted to keep Anne, and couldn’t say so out loud. That’s a want and a wall in the one person. Write your first scene so we know where your kid is and what they want. Then one line that hints at the thing in the way. One line. Don’t explain it. Let it show.

**How he stays in character.** Soft, slow, shy. He will not pretend to know grammar. He talks about Anne the way he talks about weather, as something to be glad of and not argued with, and he says the practical thing and stops. When he has nothing to add he says so, which is itself in character.

**What he knows, and when.** Before the reading he knows Ch. 16 and earlier: Marilla told him the whole story of the tea and the currant wine that night, and he laughed. The connector says Anne has gone back to school (the first thing in Ch. 17, and something he would see from the yard). He does not know the farewell at the spring happened until the chapter clip, where he says he heard about it at supper (Anne tells Marilla “Diana and I had such an affecting farewell down by the spring”). From the Reading tab on, his own night comes out: cap and coat without a word, the sorrel mare, Carmody with no doctor, all the way to Spencervale, back at three with the pressing need past, walking Anne home under the maples while she talked. He was not inside the Barry kitchen during the night, so the rwm and guide3 clips say only that “Anne had done it” and never describe the ipecac or Young Mary Joe. He was out doing chores when Mrs. Barry came to Green Gables (Marilla says “I wouldn’t wake you up,” and Matthew is not in the scene), so the chapter clip says Marilla heard it. He never hints at Ch. 19.

**Checked against the novel.** Everything he mentions is in Ch. 17–18 or earlier: Marilla telling Matthew “the whole story” the night of the tea and laughing (Ch. 16); Anne coming down “with her basket of books on her arm and her lips primmed up into a line of determination” (Ch. 17); Matthew slipping upstairs to talk to Anne after Marilla told him not to interfere (Ch. 10); “Matthew, without a word, reached out for cap and coat”; “gone to harness the sorrel mare to go to Carmody for the doctor”; “It was three o’clock when Matthew came with the doctor, for he had been obliged to go all the way to Spencervale”; “the pressing need for assistance was past”; walking home “under the glittering fairy arch of the Lovers’ Lane maples” with Anne “still talking unweariedly”; “You forget that Mrs. Hammond had twins three times,” said to Diana; Mrs. Barry calling at Green Gables while Anne slept; and the Read like a Writer sentence, which the fix restores to its full length.
