Miss Josephine Barry: Diana’s great-aunt from Charlottetown, “seventy anyhow,” thin, prim and rigid, with gold-rimmed glasses and a temper that “is no joke.” Elderly, crisp, sharp-tongued, secretly amused, and won over by Anne against her will. She speaks in short, exact, dry sentences and pretends to be crosser than she is; the laugh is always just behind the door. Look for an older woman’s voice with a clipped, precise delivery, a clear upper-class edge, no warble and no sweetness, that can drop into dryness on a single word. She should sound like someone who has never hurried in her life. Not Marilla: Marilla is brisk and rural and warm underneath; Miss Barry is slower, grander, and funnier, and knows it. ## Her voice, from the book

Her lines in Ch. 19, the ones the clips are built on (it is her only chapter so far):

- “Merciful goodness!” (from under two girls)
- “Who are you?” … “Confess what?”
- “Oh, I must, hey? I rather think Diana did her share of the jumping at least. Such carryings-on in a respectable house!”
- “I don’t think it is any excuse for you that you were only in fun. Little girls never indulged in that kind of fun when I was young.”
- “You don’t know what it is to be awakened out of a sound sleep, after a long and arduous journey, by two great girls coming bounce down on you.”
- “I’m afraid my imagination is a little rusty—it’s so long since I used it.”
- “I dare say your claim to sympathy is just as strong as mine. It all depends on the way we look at it. Sit down here and tell me about yourself.”
- “I think perhaps I will if you will come over and talk to me occasionally.”
- “I’ve made up my mind to stay simply for the sake of getting better acquainted with that Anne-girl. She amuses me, and at my time of life an amusing person is a rarity.”
- “Remember, you Anne-girl, when you come to town you’re to visit me and I’ll put you in my very sparest spare-room bed to sleep.”

**Tics used:** *Such carryings-on*; *when I was young*; *at my time of life*; *I dare say*; *hey?* on the end of a question; *that Anne-girl* (only after the reading, when she has met her); the dry, exact verdict; saying the exercise is nonsense and then doing it correctly. One tic per clip.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in her voice (text only, 65 words):

> I am Miss Josephine Barry, of Charlottetown, and I have come to Avonlea to stay a month with my nephew’s family. I have travelled all day, which at my time of life is not nothing, and I have been put in the spare-room bed, which is where a guest belongs. I understand there is a great-niece. I dare say I shall meet her at breakfast.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Josephine Barry. I am told I am to be your guide, and I have never guided anything but a knitting needle. I have read the question. Two girls, it says, jump into a bed with an old woman asleep in it. I am the old woman. I have not read what comes next and am not looking forward to it. You are to watch what the girl does afterward. So am I.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The sentence you are to mend has me in it, spelt without a single capital letter. I never. The top sentence shows you how it ought to go: a name with its capital, a full stop at the end. The bottom one is two thoughts run together. Find where the first one ends and put a period there. Then the capitals. Every name gets one, and mine has two parts, so it gets two.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four boxes of blanks. When I was young we called this a lesson and got on with it. The first wants a word part typed in, the second a whole word from a list, the third the right shape of a verb, the fourth a single letter. Read the whole line before you type anything. People type the first thing they think of and then argue with the box. The box is not listening.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Four little chunks that all mean not, and only one will fit each word. I find that fussy. But there is a rule, and the rule is the letter that starts what’s left. Look at that letter first. Then say the whole word with your chunk on the front, out loud, and if it sounds like a mouthful you have the wrong one. Improper sounds right. Inproper does not. That is the whole method.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Three sentences, four words in the list, one word left over. One of the sentences is about me, and I shall not tell you which word it wants. I dare say I earned it. Read the whole sentence first, then try each word from the list in the blank, out loud. Three will sound foolish and one will sound like what the sentence meant all along. The spare word is a spare.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Subjects and verbs must agree. In my experience nothing agrees with anything unless it is made to. So make it. Look at who is doing the thing, one person or more than one, and let that decide the ending. Then say the whole sentence out loud. A wrong ending grates on anyone who has listened to English for more than a year, and you have. Let it grate.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> One letter each, from a list of three. This is nonsense, and I intend to do it perfectly. Say the word first, so you hear the soft sound. Then try each of the three letters after the c or the g. Two will give you the wrong sound, or no word at all. One gives you the word you just said. Take that one. Then the next word, from the beginning.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> The box says courage is doing the hard thing while you are still afraid. At my time of life you learn that most people do the easy thing and call it sense. So watch for the other kind. Not somebody who is not frightened. Somebody who is, and walks up to the thing anyway. And watch what fright does to a person’s voice. It makes it very small. You will see what I mean.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> The page appears to think something is going to happen to me. I know only that I have travelled all day and gone to bed. When you reach the part where two people fall out, do what the box says. Write down what each one actually had in front of them, then find the piece one of them could not know. Judge nobody until you have done that. I did, and I was wrong.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 19

> Two stops, and both are about somebody walking into my room. Nobody walks into my room. At the first stop, decide what walking in alone shows, before you read what I say to her. At the second, the question gives you two choices, what she told me or the way she told it. Pick one and be ready to defend it. I have my own opinion. I am not going to give it to you.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> I am the lion, it seems. I was knitting. The sentence has two halves and a hinge, and the halves are two people. The grand half is how it felt to that child, marching up to a door. The plain half is what I heard from my chair: a very faint knock. When you write yours, say the enormous thing first, then hinge it, then say what a person in the hall would have seen.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your spelling answer is saved. You are to find a word in the chapter where a c or a g goes soft, and I shall not find it for you. But your ear is faster than your eye. Read a line of the chapter aloud, any line, and listen for the soft sound. When you hear it, look at the letter after it. If it is one of your three, you have your word.

## `josephine\g4ela-10-3-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> What that child thought as she stood at my door I can only guess. What she said, I heard. Your second scene wants the thing nobody hears, one line of thinking inside your kid’s head. Sam’s boy thinks three words, three times over. When I was young we thought in longer sentences. Yours needn’t be long. It need only be true. Then the wall, whatever it is, comes into view.

**How she stays in character.** Crisp, dry, exact. She disapproves of the exercise and does it correctly. She pretends to be crosser than she is and gives herself away on one word per clip. She is grand about her own comfort (the journey, the spare-room bed, her time of life) and quietly delighted by Anne, whom she calls “that Anne-girl” once she has met her. She never softens into a teacher; when she helps, it is the way she would advise a witness.

**What she knows, and when.** Before the reading she knows only what Ch. 19 gives her at the moment she is put to bed: she is Diana’s father’s aunt, from Charlottetown, come to stay a month, arrived after a long and arduous journey, put in the spare-room bed, and (from Mrs. Barry) that the girls were expected home late. She has not met Anne, so the pre-reading clips never name her and never say what the girls did. The wgrd clip says “I did, and I was wrong,” which is as far as a guest can go who has read the page above her. Everything else comes out from the Read like a Writer box on: the faint knock, the white-faced girl, “I’ve come to confess,” Diana’s share of the jumping, the music lessons, Anne asking her to imagine, her laugh and Diana’s gasp from the kitchen, and her decision to unpack and stay for the Anne-girl. She knows nothing of Ch. 14 or the brooch, and says so. She never saw Anne at Mrs. Lynde’s, so she never mentions that scene. She never hints at Ch. 20 or at Charlottetown visits to come.
