## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> It’s Marilla. Two chapters, and two scrapes, and I was home for only one of them. The question is what Anne learns from her mistakes, and she will tell you herself, at some length. First there’s word work. Find every doing word in the past that breaks the usual rule, and every word that lost its quiet e. Then read. Then tell each chapter short. Shorter than she would.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> The box says the hard part is leaving out the good bits. That child could make a funeral out of a broken teacup, so I know exactly what it means. These two chapters are full of things you’ll want to tell. Most of them you won’t need. Keep why something happened. Let what it looked like go. And if a detail only makes you laugh, it goes first.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is from the book, I’m sorry to say, and every word of it is true. Look at the two doing words that changed inside themselves, and the one that only took the usual ending. The bottom sentence is two thoughts jammed together with no stop between them. Give each its own. And the girl in it wants her capital, whatever she’s done.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four pieces of word work. One is a matching game, and one asks you to remember words without any help. Then two where you tap. Tap only what the directions ask for, not everything that looks interesting. Anne does whatever catches her eye, and I’ve seen where that leads. Read the directions twice. Then do exactly that, and nothing more.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Matching today. Two roots and two little front parts, and what each one means. You met the roots before. The front parts are new company. For each one, think of a whole word you already use that has it inside, and say it out loud. Then take that word apart. What’s left over usually tells you what the part means.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> The same four words as yesterday, and no cards this time. Match each one to its meaning. If you’re not sure, put the word in a sentence about somebody at home. I could think of one for every word on that list, and I’d rather not say who. Don’t guess. Guessing is how a girl ends up believing a stranger at the back door.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> One long sentence with more than one past doing word in it. Tap only the ones that don’t take the usual ending. There’s more than one, so don’t stop at the first. Read it slowly, a word at a time, and ask of each one, did this already happen, and how did it change to show it? I’ve no patience for halfway work. Find all of them.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Six words, and each one once ended in a quiet e. In half of them the e stayed, and in half it was dropped. Tap only the ones where it was dropped. Look at the first letter of what was added. That letter decides whether the e is still needed. It’s a plain rule. I like plain rules. They don’t argue back.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about learning from mistakes. I’ve had a front seat for most of hers. The brooch, the Haunted Wood, the cake with the liniment in it. Every one of them happened on my watch. What I want to know is whether she ever learns anything from them, or only finds a new way to be wrong. Read, and judge for yourself.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 27 & 28

> One stop, at the end of the first chapter. Think about what that scrape teaches her, about strangers, and about wanting to look different. Then read on into the second. I was away that afternoon, over at Carmody, so I only learned of it afterward. Watch who comes along, and watch what she says to him. I have my own opinion. Form yours.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> I wasn’t there to see this, but I can tell you what happened from the sentence alone. A boy jumps into a boat, and his cheeks are red. Three things, and you know he’s angry and he’s leaving. She doesn’t tell you what he thought. She doesn’t need to. When you write yours, pick three things somebody could see, and trust your reader with the rest.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now the writing. I’ll say what I’d say to Anne. Answer the question that’s asked, not the one you wish had been asked. If it wants what happened, say what happened. If it wants a reason, give one, and show where in the book you found it. Plain answers. You can be as romantic as you like in your own story. Not here.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question asks what color her hair really turned and what had to be done about it. I was the one who had to do it, and I’ll say no more. The second asks how she saved herself, and who came along after. The third gives you her own list and asks you to choose one. Use her words. She chose them carefully enough.

## `marilla\g4ela-12-2-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you cut something. One sentence that wanders off from the scene, even if you’re fond of it. Then move one, to where it does the most good. The last sentence of a scene is the strongest spot. I’ve never seen the use of saying a thing twice, so this suits me. A good story, like a good kitchen, has nothing lying about that doesn’t belong.
