## `diana\g4ela-12-1-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello, it’s Diana. Today is about our story club, and how Anne works out what she thinks a good story is. She has a great many ideas about it. Before the reading there’s word work, doing words that don’t follow the usual rule for the past, and a spelling rule about a quiet letter e. Then you’ll tell the chapter in a few sentences. Just a few. Anne would need fifty.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> The box says to tell a chapter in three sentences, not thirty. I’m rather good at that, because I never know what to do with my people, so I simply stop. Anne is the opposite. She keeps every lovely word and every sigh. So as you read, stop now and then and ask what has changed. If nothing has, that part can go.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is Anne’s, and it’s about Matthew, who likes every story she writes. Look at the two doing words that tell what already happened. Neither one takes the usual ending. The bottom sentence is about me, I’m afraid, and somebody forgot my capital. There’s also a story that belongs to someone, and a sentence that never stops. Mend all three.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four bits of word work, and two of them sort into three columns, not two. That’s harder than it sounds. Ruby would put everything in the middle and hope. Don’t do that. Say each word out loud before you move it, because your ear knows more than you think. And the fill-ins want the back half of each word, not the front. Read the meaning first.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Two old roots today, living inside words you already use. One means to carry. The other means to pull, or to draw. The front of each word is already there, and you type the rest. Read the meaning after the blank first. Then ask, is something being carried here, or pulled? Think of a wagon, then a magnet. Anne would think of a chariot and a lodestone. A wagon does just as well.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Four new words, and each card has a real sentence from the book above it. Some of them will sound strange to you. They sounded strange to me too, the first time. Use the sentence anyway, then look for word parts, then guess before you flip. Anne flips first and thinks afterward. I’d think first. Then tap.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> All of these doing words are already in the past, and not one of them takes the usual ending. But they break the rule in three different ways. Say each pair out loud, the now word and then the past one. Listen for how much changes. A little, a lot, or nothing at all. Anne says breaking a rule beautifully is half the fun. Here it’s the whole lesson.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> These words all end in a quiet e, and then something gets added. Sometimes the e stays and sometimes it goes. Look at the first letter of what’s been added, and look at what comes just before the e. Those two together decide it. One column is for words that never had the tricky sound at all. You’ll know them when you say them.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-copia.mp3` — Word Study · Say it three ways (copia)

> That sentence is about me, and it’s quite true. I did write to Aunt Josephine about our club. Now say it three ways. Change the doing word first, but keep it about a letter. Then start the sentence somewhere else, with a when or a where. Then tell how I wrote it. Anne says one sentence said three ways is three times as good. I only half believe her.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about learning from your mistakes. I know a little about that. Anne and I made up the Haunted Wood together, and we frightened ourselves so badly that neither of us would go through it after dark. Anne said she’d be contented with commonplace places after that. As you read, watch whether she is. I’m not sure she is.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 26

> Two stops. The first comes when Anne tells how each girl in the club writes. One of them is me, and I’ll say no more. Think about why four friends would make up such different stories. The second comes at the very end. Ask yourself why none of us ever writes about Avonlea. It’s right outside the window, and nobody thinks of it at all.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now you write, and it’s your answers, not your stories. Anne writes three pages when one line would do. Don’t. When a question asks what happened, say it plainly, then show me where in the book. When it asks why, give a reason and a line to back it up. And keep your summary short. Short is hard. I know, because Anne never manages it.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question asks what the club is called and what each of us has to do. Anne made the rules, so look at what she tells Marilla. The second wants Anne’s story in two sentences. Two! I heard every word of it, and I couldn’t. Keep the people and the ending. The third asks why a moral matters to her. Look at who she says agrees.

## `diana\g4ela-12-1-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you add. Find one place in your story where things happen too fast, where somebody falls or wins or cries and we never feel it. Then slow down and put in what we’d see or hear or feel. Anne’s stories are full of things happening, horses running away and people drowning. The part I love best is always the little bit in between. Put a little bit in.
