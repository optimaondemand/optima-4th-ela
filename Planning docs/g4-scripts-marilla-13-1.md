## Her voice

Real lines from the book:

- “Yes, I believe she could,” said Marilla dryly. “She does plenty of unofficial preaching as it is.”
- “If you are I guess I am too, Anne, for Rachel often has that very effect on me.”
- “There should have been a special commandment against nagging. But there, I shouldn’t talk so.”
- “It’s worth something to have that,” admitted Marilla.
- “Why, Anne, how you’ve grown!”
- “I was thinking about Anne. She’s got to be such a big girl—and she’ll probably be away from us next winter. I’ll miss her terrible.”
- “But there—men can’t understand these things!”
- “You don’t chatter half as much as you used to, Anne, nor use half as many big words. What has come over you?”
- “Why, go to school next year and try again.”

**How she sounds:** dry, brisk, warm underneath; she would rather be caught doing anything than crying, so she mentions the cry once and changes the subject. She took the doctor’s message seriously and says so. Tics used: “I dare say”, “there”, plain rules, a domestic comparison per clip at most.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 64 words):

> It’s Marilla. The Spencervale doctor sent word that Anne was to be kept out of doors all summer, with no books, and I did as I was told. She came back in September brown as a berry and two inches taller. Two inches. I’d made her new dresses longer, and they’re only just long enough. You’ll see what else changed. It isn’t only dresses.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> It’s Marilla. That child grew two inches in one summer, and somewhere along the way she stopped chattering. I can’t say I expected to miss it. The question is how a girl can change and stay the same at once. First there are verbs that do nothing at all but join, and words that compare. Then the chapter. Watch what Anne says out loud, and what she only shows.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> The box talks about the big idea of a story. Anne says hers right out loud in this chapter, and she says it to me. That’s the easy one. The harder one nobody says. Watch what she chooses, and how it turns out, and what the title is a picture of. A brook doesn’t ask permission to become a river. It simply does.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is Miss Stacy’s opinion, by way of Anne, and I happen to agree with it. Short words are stronger. Find the joining word, and the two that compare. The bottom one is two sentences run together, and somebody’s name has lost its capital. I made those dresses myself, so you’ll forgive me for wanting it written properly.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four pieces of word work, and two of them ask you to sort into three piles, not two. That third pile is where the thinking is. Don’t push a word into a pile because it looks like its neighbor. Say it in a sentence first. I sort the mending the same way: what’s finished, what isn’t, and what I’m not sure of yet.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Two old roots today, one that means to look and one that means to build. They don’t stand on their own. They sit inside other words the way the yeast sits inside the bread. You never see it, and you’d notice fast if it weren’t there. When a blank stumps you, ask whether the word is about seeing or about making. That will tell you which root belongs there.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Four words, and two of them describe Miss Stacy. Mrs. Lynde has her doubts about Miss Stacy, which is reason enough to learn them. Read each sentence before you turn the card. Then, when you match, try each word against a person you know. If it fits nobody at all, you’ve likely got the wrong meaning.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Some verbs do something. Some only join a person to a description and do no work at all, rather like certain neighbors I could name. A few go either way, depending on the sentence. For those, put each one in two sentences of your own and see whether it’s doing a job or just standing there. The box at the bottom has a test. Use it.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Comparing words. Some take a short ending, some need more or most in front, and some have a y that must change first. Count the beats in the plain word before you decide. Clap them if you must; I won’t tell anyone. Anne used to pile on the longest words she could find. These days she counts more carefully, and so should you.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-copia.mp3` — Word Study · Say it three ways (copia)

> Here’s one plain sentence about a girl and some books. You’re to say it three ways: add a time, change the doing word, then stretch it out. Anne used to stretch every sentence until it snapped. Miss Stacy taught her better. Try all three, then ask which says the most with the least. That’s the one a sensible person would keep.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about noticing your own change. Anne noticed hers, and said so. I noticed it too, standing beside her one day and finding I had to look up. I’ll not say what I did about it that night. Matthew caught me at it, and that’s more than enough witnesses. Read, and watch who sees the change, and who only feels it.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 31

> Two stops. The first comes when she tells me what she’s been worrying over, on Sunday afternoons of all times. Listen for why growing up feels serious to her, not just that it does. The second comes near the end, and it’s about me. I’d rather you didn’t dwell on it. You will anyway. Keep an eye on how much she talks. Then on how much she doesn’t.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Here’s where you write it down. I’ve no patience for answers that wander. Read the question, decide what it wants, and give it that, with something from the book to show you didn’t make it up. Anne is learning to be her own sharpest critic. Read over what you write once before you call it done. You’ll find something. Everybody does.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about the doctor’s message. I took it seriously, and I’d do it again. The second wants two changes in her, one you could measure with a tape and one you’d only hear. The third is about me, and that night. Be fair to me. I’ll own I had a cry, and Matthew caught me at it. The book doesn’t say I was wrong.

## `marilla\g4ela-13-1-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Editing. This is the part of writing I understand best. It’s like going over a floor with a lamp after you’ve swept it: you find what you missed. Today it’s a capital at the start and a stop at the end of every sentence. Read one line at a time, slowly, with your finger under it. Slow is how you catch things. Fast is how you miss them.
