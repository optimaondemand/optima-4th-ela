## `diana\g4ela-9-2-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello, it’s Diana Barry, and I’m your guide today, for the first time. Your question is about how a friendship starts, and you won’t find it in speeches. You’ll find it in small things people do, mostly with their hands. So watch hands. In word study you’ll be finding the words for things you can’t pick up. And there’s a poem to learn by heart. Say it out loud, not in your head.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> This one’s like a game, and I love games. Pick something small that happens, the smaller the better. Then keep asking, and then what? Follow it in your head. When the chain seems to run out, push once more, because the last link is usually somebody who wasn’t even there when it started. If you can follow it all the way to what somebody is hoping for by the end, you’ve done it.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The model is somebody talking, so look at the marks that hold the talking in, and at what sits inside the last one. The sentence you’re fixing isn’t talking at all. It’s two whole ideas squashed together with nothing between them. Read it aloud and listen for where your voice drops. That’s where the first idea needs a period and the next one needs a capital letter. And two names want capitals too.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Word study! First there are word parts to match, and one of the four isn’t a front part at all, so read the meanings carefully. Then the same four words as yesterday, then a sentence to tap, then a row of words to say out loud. Saying words out loud helps more than you’d think. Do the four boxes in order, and don’t rush the last one because it looks easy.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Tap a word part, then its meaning. Before you tap, look at the three example words in brackets after each meaning and say them. If the part you’re holding fits at the front of all three, that’s your match. One of the four isn’t a front part at all. It goes at the end of a word, and its meaning is a thing, not a direction. Match that one last and it’ll nearly match itself.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> You met these four yesterday. Before you tap anything, say each word and try to picture the sentence it came from. Do the two you remember best first. Then there are only two words and two meanings left, and you can test each pair by saying them together and listening. If you tap a wrong one, a little line comes up to explain. Read it properly. It’s there to help, not to scold.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Tap every one, and there’s more than one. Here’s my test. For each naming word, ask: could I put it in a box and carry it over to Green Gables? If I could, leave it alone. If I couldn’t, because it’s a feeling or an idea, tap it. Be careful. Things you could carry sit right next to the ones you can’t. Read to the very last word.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> You’re after the odd ones out, so don’t tap the words that do the usual thing. Say each word slowly, then quickly. In most of them one syllable goes soft and muddy. Those are the ordinary ones. Leave them be. The ones you want stay clear at both ends however fast you say them. Say each one twice before you decide. Then pick one you tapped and explain it in the box.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> I’m at home with a book, which is where I usually am. Mother thinks I read too much, and Father keeps giving me more. This week’s watch is about letting yourself look forward to things. I’m good at that. I’m not so good at the waiting. As you read, notice what somebody lets herself hope for, and how she behaves while she waits. Waiting badly and waiting well look very different.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 12 & 13

> There’s one stop, after the first chapter, before you go on to the second. It asks what a promise means to somebody who has never had a family to promise her anything. When you get there, think about it from the other side, too. What would a girl who does have a family get out of a promise like that? Then read the second chapter straight through and count the things she looks forward to.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> Well. That’s me in there, and it’s true, I do. I never noticed till I read it. Try this: read the two sentences, then cover the second one with your finger. With just the first, I laughed once. Put the second back, and I’m a person who laughs. When you write yours, make the habit match who your character is, not just any old habit. Mine tells you I’m not much of a worrier.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> So now you know. She asked me straight out if I could like her enough to be her bosom friend, and I said I guessed so. Then we joined hands over the garden path, pretending it was a stream, and swore. I got the giggles, but I meant it. And I’ve been explaining ice cream to her, which is harder than it sounds. Go on. Say what you noticed, and show it from the book.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about something she said to Mother when we met, and I was sitting right there. Why would a person use such big words at a moment like that? For the second, look at the ceremony, not just the promise. Who set it up, and why so grand? The third is about something Marilla said to her at home. I wasn’t there. Read it carefully and decide whether Marilla is right.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your grammar answer is saved. You tapped the words for things you can’t put in a box. Well, I’ve just got one of those. Friendship. You can’t hold it, but you can watch it: two hands joined over a path, a promise said twice, a book lent. Look back at the word you wrote about and ask what somebody would have to do to show it. That’s how a writer makes an invisible thing visible.

## `diana\g4ela-9-2-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you sort walls: a person, a rule, or a fear. Here’s something I only found out afterwards. Anne was frightened of meeting me. She thought I mightn’t like her. That was her wall, and it was inside her the whole time. So when you pick your kid’s main wall, check whether it’s really a person, or a fear wearing a person’s face. Then add a smaller wall of a different kind.

**How she stays in character.** Bright, loyal, a little giggly. She laughs first and thinks second, adores Anne without quite understanding her, and is a little in awe of her own mother.

**What she knows, and when.** Before the reading she has not met Anne. She knows only that a girl has come to live at Green Gables, that there is no other girl near enough to play with and her sister is too little, that she reads a great deal and her father encourages it. She does not know Marilla is on her way with Anne, so the pre-reading clips never say so. The bosom-friend question, the vow over the path, the giggles, Idlewild, the picnic and ice cream come out only in the RWM and Assignment-tab clips. She was in the room when Anne said she was rumpled up in spirit (it was said to Mrs. Barry, not Mrs. Lynde as the question has it), so she can vouch for that. She was not at Green Gables for “You set your heart too much on things,” so she says plainly she wasn’t there. Anne’s fear that Diana wouldn’t like her was said to Marilla, so the workshop clip says Diana only found it out afterwards. She never hints at Ch. 14.

**Checked against the novel.** Everything she mentions is in Ch. 12–13: Orchard Slope over the brook, “I’m awfully glad you’ve come to live at Green Gables,” no other girl near enough to play with and no sisters big enough, Diana on the sofa reading a book she dropped when the callers entered, “She reads entirely too much … her father aids and abets her,” “do you think you can like me a little, enough to be my bosom friend?” and “Why, I guess so,” joining hands over the path imagined as running water, “Diana laughed. Diana always laughed before she spoke,” walking with arms about each other, Diana lending a book, the chocolate sweeties, Idlewild in the birch ring, the Sunday-school picnic with ice cream that Diana tried to explain, and “What if she shouldn’t like me!” (said to Marilla). No clip reads the page, says a word part on its own, gives away an answer on the page, or names a time of day. Every clip is 40–75 words.
