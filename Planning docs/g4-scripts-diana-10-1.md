Her lines in Ch. 16–17 and earlier, the ones the clips are built on:

- “That’s awfully nice raspberry cordial, Anne. I didn’t know raspberry cordial was so nice.” (Ch. 16)
- “I’m—I’m awful sick. I—I—must go right home.” (Ch. 16)
- “It’s ever so much nicer than Mrs. Lynde’s although she brags of hers so much.” (Ch. 16)

- “Oh, Anne, she says I’m never to play with you again. I’ve cried and cried and I told her it wasn’t your fault, but it wasn’t any use.” (Ch. 17)
- “I miss you awfully to tell all my secrets to and I don’t like Gertie Pye one bit.” (Ch. 17, her note)
- “They are awfully fashionable now and only three girls in school know how to make them.” (Ch. 17)
- “Have you got anything to cut it with?” (Ch. 17, returning to practicalities)
- “Why, of course I do. Didn’t you know that?” (Ch. 17)
- “Diana laughed. Diana always laughed before she spoke.” (Ch. 12)

**Tics used:** *awfully* and *awful* for very; *ever so*; *Oh, Anne!*; the laugh before she speaks; school gossip as her frame of reference (Gertie Pye, Ruby Gillis, Mattie Andrews’ red hood); short, practical sentences that land after Anne’s long ones. She likes things she can finish. One tic per clip, not three.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in her voice (text only, 65 words):

> Hello, it’s Diana. I’m going to tea at Green Gables this afternoon, a proper invitation, and Mother says I’m to knock at the front door and not run into the kitchen the way I always do. I’ve got my second-best dress on. I’ve been practising sitting with my toes together. Anne will do all the talking. She always does, and I don’t mind one bit.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> It’s Diana again. The question at the top is about an honest mistake turning into a real problem. I don’t know anything about that. All I know is I’m going to tea, and I’m awfully excited. So as you go, keep two lists in your head: what somebody meant to do, and what actually happened. Then there are doing words to sort, and a c-and-g rule that isn’t as simple as it looks.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-wgrd.mp3` — Warm-Up · What Good Readers Do

> Mother and I can watch the same thing and come away with two different stories. It used to make me cross. Now I think it’s just how people are. So when the box says pick one event, pick a small one, and put each person beside it. Then find the one thing somebody didn’t know. That’s usually the whole gap, right there. It was for Mother and me, anyway.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> That’s my name in the bottom sentence without its capital letter, and Anne’s too. I mind that. The top one shows you how a sentence ends and starts again. The bottom one has two whole thoughts squashed together with nothing between them. Read it out loud and listen for the place your voice drops. That’s where the first thought wants a period and the next wants a capital letter.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four boxes. Word parts to type, then words to guess and match, then two sorts. I like sorts, because you can tell when you’re finished. Do them in order, and read every bit of a box before you tap anything, because two of them have a third column, and the third column is the tricky one. Anne would say the tricky one is the interesting one. Anne isn’t doing your sorting.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> Four little chunks for one job. I’d have said that was silly, four chunks that all mean not. But look at the letter right after each blank. That letter picks the chunk. Cover the blank, say what’s left, and try the chunk that sounds like it belongs on the front of it. The wrong one sounds wrong out loud. Anne says everything sounds better out loud, and for once she’s right.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Three steps, then the flip cards, then a match. Here’s the thing about the flip cards. Guess first, out loud, before you tap. If you tap first there’s nothing to guess, and then the match is just remembering. In the match, do the two you’re surest of first. Then there are only two left and you can try them both ways and listen for which pair sounds right.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Sorting doing words. Gertie Pye squeaks her pencil through every grammar lesson and it makes my blood run cold, so I’d rather do it here. Say each chip with its little word in front, the she or the they. Listen to the end of the doing word. Some change, some don’t, and some have a helper word stuck on the front. That’s your three groups. Name them in your own words.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Two letters, and each one has two sounds. Say every word first, before you look at the columns, and listen for a hard sound or a soft one. Then look at the letter right after the c or the g. That letter decides it. Two of the words have both letters in them, one hard and one soft, so check those twice. Ruby Gillis would just guess. Don’t be Ruby.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-copia.mp3` — Word Study · Copia

> One sentence, said three ways. Anne can say a thing six ways before she gets to the end of the lane, and I used to think that was showing off. It isn’t. It’s trying them on. Read the sentence, find the doing word, and change just that first. Then start it somewhere else. Then add where or how. Keep the one that sounds most like you.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> I’m dressed and I’m nervous, which is a silly thing to be about tea. The box on courage says it’s doing the hard thing while you’re still frightened. I’d have said courage was not being frightened. So while you read, watch for somebody who’s scared and goes anyway. And watch what fear looks like from the outside. Mostly it looks like standing very still.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 16

> There are two stops, and I’ve read them, and I don’t like the sound of either one. One of them has my tea in it. The first stop asks whether somebody did anything wrong. Decide that before you read one more word, and then see if the rest of the chapter changes your mind. The second one asks the difference between a mistake and a wrong thing. Take your time on it.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Well. Now you know. I was awfully sick, I had a headache all the next day, and Mother smelled my breath and that was that. She says Anne did it on purpose. I don’t believe it for one minute. Anne didn’t have any herself, she said she didn’t want any after the apples. But nobody’s asking me. They’re asking you, so show it from the book, not from how you feel.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about what I drank, and why Anne didn’t know. Look at what Anne said when she set it down in front of me. The second is about Mother, and she’s my mother, so I’m not the one to ask. Put what she believes happened next to what happened. The third is about Marilla, and I wasn’t there. The hint gives you two things she did. Use both.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your word-parts answer is saved. You made a word that means not something. Well, the whole afternoon was a not. Anne did not mean it. Mother does not believe her. I wasn’t even home to say goodbye. Look back at the word you chose and ask which person in the chapter it fits best. If it fits somebody, put them in your sentence. If it doesn’t, pick a different word.

## `diana\g4ela-10-1-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Sam wrote about the weather first, and then crossed it out. Anne’s chapter starts with October, a whole page of it, and I love October, but the story starts when Marilla says I can come to tea. So write your two first sentences, two different kinds, and read each one out loud. The one that makes you want to know what happens next is the one you star.

---

**How she stays in character.** Bright, loyal, a little giggly. She thinks Anne is the cleverest person she knows and doesn’t understand half of what Anne says. She measures things by school (Gertie Pye, Ruby Gillis) and by her mother. She reacts to the page before she helps, and her help is short and practical, the way she asks Anne “Have you got anything to cut it with?” in the middle of an eternal farewell.

**What she knows, and when.** Before the reading she knows only that Marilla has said she may come to tea, that Anne ran over to ask her, and that she is to go in her second-best dress and knock at the front door (Ch. 16 opens with these). She has not tasted anything, so the pre-reading clips never mention cordial, wine or being sick, and the Copia clip and the two sorts stay clear of the plot. Her afternoon comes out only from the Assignment tab on: three tumblerfuls, feeling awful sick, going home dizzy, the headache all day Sunday, her mother smelling her breath and saying Anne set her drunk on purpose, and “never, never” letting Diana play with her again. She was in Carmody with her father when Anne came to the door, so she does not describe that scene, and she never saw Marilla kiss Anne’s cheek, so the chapter clip says plainly she wasn’t there. She never hints at Ch. 17 (the ten-minute farewell, the note, going back to school).

**Checked against the novel.** Everything she mentions is in Ch. 16 or earlier: the tea invitation (“you can ask Diana to come over and spend the afternoon with you and have tea here”), Anne running to Orchard Slope to ask her, Diana in her second-best dress knocking “primly at the front door” when she usually runs into the kitchen, the girls “toes in position,” Gertie Pye’s squeaking pencil that made Diana’s blood run cold, Ruby Gillis and the wart pebble, Mrs. Lynde bragging about her cordial, Anne “I don’t believe I’ll have any just now,” three generous tumblerfuls, “I’m—I’m awful sick,” walking home dizzily, the rain all Sunday and the fearful headache, “Her mother smelled her breath,” Mrs. Barry’s “thoroughly bad, wicked little girl” and “never, never going to let Diana play with me again,” Diana “gone over to Carmody with her father,” and the chapter opening with October before Marilla’s invitation. The Copia seed, “Anne revelled in the world of colour about her,” is Montgomery’s second sentence.
