## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hello. Gilbert Blythe here, and it’s my first go at guiding, so go easy on me. Your question is about a moment in the schoolroom where somebody does something sudden, and what was inside her that made her do it. I’ll be honest, I don’t know what’s coming any better than you do. In word study you’re checking a draft somebody else wrote, so you get to be the teacher for once. Enjoy that.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> Somebody’s shouting in the model, and the marks show it. Look at what sits inside the closing quotation mark and what kind of stop it is. The sentence you’re fixing has two people doing two different things, so it wants to be two sentences. Say it aloud with some feeling and you’ll hear where the shout ends and the telling begins. Names get capital letters. Write it out first, then check.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Word study, last day of the week. You match the word parts one more time, pick one thing to do with the words, and then mark two drafts the way a teacher would. Here’s the thing about marking. Tapping a good one costs you, so you can’t just tap everything and hope. Read each one twice and decide. I’ve been on the wrong end of a teacher’s marking. It’s more fun on this side.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> You’ve matched these before, so make it harder on yourself. Cover the meanings with your hand and say what each part means before you look. Then match. The three real front parts all point somewhere: above, under, below. The fourth one isn’t a front part at all. If you’re stuck between two, say the example words in brackets out loud and listen for where the part sits.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Five choices and you only do one, so don’t pick the easy one. Pick the one you’d actually enjoy. If you like arguing, teach a word to a younger kid; you’ll find out fast whether you really know it. If you like the book, hunt the chapter for a word close to one of ours. Whatever you choose, use the word the way the book does. Read your answer back once before you move on.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Somebody wrote a draft about the chapter and labeled the naming words. Some labels are wrong. Don’t trust them just because they sound sure. For each one, ask: could I drop this on my foot? If yes, it’s a thing, and check the label says so. If it’s a feeling, an idea, or a word for a bunch of people, it’s something else. Tap only the wrong ones. A right one tapped costs you.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Six words, some spelled wrong, and your ear won’t catch a single one. Say them and they all sound fine. That’s the trick of the lazy vowel. So don’t listen. Look. Picture each word the way you’ve seen it printed, and compare it letter by letter with what’s on the screen. Tap only the ones that don’t match your picture. Then fix one in the box and say why your ear failed.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> First day back, and I mean to make an impression. This week’s watch is friendship, and I’ll tell you what I know about it: you find out who your friends are after you’ve made a fool of yourself. As you read, watch who stands by whom when things go wrong, and what it costs them. And watch me. I’m not saying I’ll behave. I’m saying watch.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> Today you’re weighing causes, not just finding one. Here’s how. Every time something pushes her toward what she does, make a mark in your head. Big pushes and small ones. When you get to the sudden moment, stop and line them up. Then take away the biggest and ask whether she’d still have done it. If yes, it wasn’t the biggest. Then zoom out over the whole week, the way the box asks.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 15

> One stop, at the end. Read straight through, and pay attention to the schoolroom: who sits where, who’s watching whom. It matters. When you reach the stop, don’t rush it. It asks what somebody did wrong and what it hit. Answer the second part carefully. What a thing hits depends on the person, not the thing. And keep your book handy. The questions will send you back into it.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Right. So I called her Carrots, holding up her braid, and she cracked her slate over my head. I told Mr. Phillips it was my fault, which it was. I said sorry after, honest, and she walked past like I wasn’t there. Then I gave her a candy heart and she ground it underfoot. I don’t know why it landed so hard. You’ve read more of her than I have. Tell me.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is why it hit her so hard, and I’m the wrong person to ask. Look back at what somebody else called her earlier in the book, and what she did then. The second is about a punishment. Think what sitting next to me meant after what I’d done. Not to me. To her. The third goes back over the whole week. Pick one chain, and make each link touch the next.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-wordconn.mp3` — Assignment · Word Study Connection

> Your vocabulary answer is saved. One of this week’s words was vindictive, and Mr. Phillips used it on her in front of the whole school. Well, she wouldn’t take my apology, and she ground my candy heart to powder, so maybe he had a point. Or maybe there’s a difference between wanting revenge and refusing to be laughed at. Look at your answer and decide which word fits her better. Say why.

## `gilbert\g4ela-9-4-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Today you write five scenes, one line each. Look at what the author did with me. The whole slate business is one scene, one room, told all the way through, and it’s the only thing anybody will remember about this chapter. So when you write your five lines, don’t cram. Each scene gets one place and one thing that happens. And make the worst moment a choice somebody makes, not just bad luck.

**How he stays in character.** Easy, teasing, quick to own up. He is used to being liked and is caught off balance by somebody who won’t like him.

**What he knows, and when.** Before the reading he has just come home from his cousins’ in New Brunswick, is back at school, and knows he teases the girls. He has not met Anne, so the pre-reading clips never describe her, her hair or what she will do; the welcome and read clips speak of “somebody” and only say the printed question is about a sudden moment. Carrots, the braid, the slate, “It was my fault, Mr. Phillips. I teased her,” the apology, being walked past, and the candy heart come out only in the Assignment-tab clips. He was not there for Ch. 9 or for anything at Green Gables, so he cannot explain why the word wounded her; he sends the student back to the book for that. He never hints at later chapters.

**Checked against the novel.** Everything he mentions is in Ch. 15: visiting his cousins over in New Brunswick all summer and coming home Saturday night, teasing the girls (pinning Ruby Gillis’s braid to her seat), picking up the end of Anne’s braid and saying “Carrots! Carrots!” in a piercing whisper, Anne bringing her slate down on his head and cracking it, “It was my fault, Mr. Phillips. I teased her,” Mr. Phillips’s “displaying such a temper and such a vindictive spirit,” “I’m awful sorry I made fun of your hair, Anne. Honest I am,” Anne sweeping past without a look, and the pink candy heart ground to powder under her heel. No clip reads the page, says a word part on its own, gives away an answer on the page, or names a time of day. Every clip is 40–75 words.
