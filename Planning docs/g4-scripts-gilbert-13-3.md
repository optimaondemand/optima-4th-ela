## His voice

Real lines from the book:

- “Look here, Anne. Can’t we be good friends?” (Ch. 28)
- “I’m awfully sorry I made fun of your hair that time. I didn’t mean to vex you and I only meant it for a joke.” (Ch. 28)
- “I think your hair is awfully pretty now—honest I do. Let’s be friends.” (Ch. 28)
- “All right. I’ll never ask you to be friends again, Anne Shirley. And I don’t care either!” (Ch. 28)
- Ch. 33, narration: he sat “away at the back of the room, bending forward with a smile on his face”; he “was merely smiling with appreciation of the whole affair in general”; he had driven Josie Pye over.
- Ch. 17, narration: “Now Gilbert was head of his spelling class; now Anne, with a toss of her long red braids, spelled him down.”

**How he sounds:** easy, good-humored, steadier now (he tied her for first on the pass list and she still isn’t speaking to him). He knows only what he saw: he smiled, he sat with Josie Pye, he wasn’t in the buggy going home. He can’t know what Anne thought his smile meant, so he never says. Tics used: “look here”, “awful” for very, “honest”, a bet now and then.

The opening paragraph under the greeting is also in this voice (text only, 56 words):

> Look here, it’s Gilbert Blythe. I don’t suppose Anne would want me introducing her chapter, but she isn’t speaking to me, so she can’t very well object. There’s a concert at the White Sands Hotel, and Anne’s to recite. I drove over. I sat at the back. That’s about all I’m going to tell you, honest.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-welcome.mp3` — Warm-Up (in the greeting)

> Hi, it’s Gilbert. Today’s question is about one small moment at a concert, and what it shows about how Anne has grown. I was in the room for that moment, as it happens. First, some word work: building comparing words and filling in joining verbs. Then read the chapter, and decide for yourself what happened on that stage. I’ve got my own idea.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-dol.mp3` — Warm-Up · Daily Oral Language

> The top sentence is from the book, and from where I sat, it was awful true. Find the joining verb, and what it joins Anne to. The bottom one is two sentences that ran into each other. Split them, and give everybody their capitals, Anne included. She’d want hers. She’s particular about that sort of thing, and about how her name is spelled.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-guide1.mp3` — Word Study tab

> Four pieces of word work, and most of them ask you to build something instead of just picking. That’s harder, but it’s the kind of hard that sticks. If a blank stumps you, say the whole sentence out loud. Your ear knows more than you think. Mine has gotten me out of a few tight spots at the blackboard, anyhow.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-morphology.mp3` — Word Study · Morphology

> These blanks are all over the place: in the middle of a word, at the end, even at the very front. So read the whole line before you type. Most of them ask whether the word is about looking or about building. A crowd at a ball game is doing one of those. A barn raising is doing the other. The odd one out wants the ending for a person who does it.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-vocabulary.mp3` — Word Study · Vocabulary

> Three sentences, three blanks, and four words in the bank, so one gets left over. Read each sentence all the way through before you choose; the end of a sentence usually tells you more than the start. I’ll own up that one of these words fits me pretty well. I won’t say which. Anne would say it was the wrong one.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-grammar.mp3` — Word Study · Grammar

> Four blanks and four joining verbs, and each one gets used once. They’re close cousins, so try every choice in every blank and listen hard. Read the whole sentence, not just the words around the gap. Sometimes the end of it settles the matter, like a but halfway through that changes everything.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-spelling.mp3` — Word Study · Spelling

> Four comparing words to build, and two decisions every time: how many beats the plain word has, and whether there’s a y at the end that needs changing first. Do them in that order. Anne and I have spelled each other down more times than I can count. Whoever checks the second thing usually wins.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-guide2.mp3` — Reading tab

> There’s a box here about deciding what counts as rich. I wasn’t in the buggy going home, so I can’t tell you what was said there. But I saw that hotel, all lit up and dripping with diamonds, and I saw Anne in a plain white dress. Read, and watch what she decides is worth counting. I’d bet it isn’t the diamonds.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-wgrd.mp3` — Reading · What Good Readers Do

> The box says some big ideas are never said out loud. That’s true at concerts, too. Nobody stands up and tells you what a moment meant; you have to work it out. So watch what Anne chooses, and what happens right after. Then write the sentence nobody in the chapter says. Don’t look to me for it. I was only smiling.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-read.mp3` — Reading · Read: Ch. 33

> Two stops. The first comes when Anne is up on that platform and can’t get a word out. Think about what changes for her, and what gets her started. The second stop comes on the drive home, after the concert. Listen to what she says she’s rich in, and ask whether she means it. I think she does. Honest.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-rwm.mp3` — Reading · Read like a Writer

> I like this sentence because Anne doesn’t argue. She just says it, flat out, and then backs it up with three things nobody can take away from her. Look at the one word that tells you how she said it. Take that away and she sounds like she’s guessing. When you write yours, choose your how-word carefully. Then give three reasons. Three’s a strong number.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-guide3.mp3` — Assignment tab

> Now you write. Here’s how I’d go about it. Read the question twice, then find the spot in the chapter where the answer lives before you write a word. When a question asks for an exact moment, quote it. If you’re guessing why someone did something, say what in the book made you guess. Anne calls that being dull. I call it being right.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-chapter.mp3` — Assignment · Chapter Thinking

> The first question is about Anne’s dress and her beads, next to all those city jewels. The second asks what she sees from the stage, and wants you to quote it. I’ll admit I’m curious what you’ll say about that one. The third is about the drive home, where Jane wants diamonds and Anne doesn’t.

## `gilbert\g4ela-13-3-workshop.mp3` — Assignment · Writer’s Workshop

> Read slowly today, slower than feels right. You’re hunting three things: the wrong word that sounds like the right one, a spelling you wouldn’t bet a nickel on, and a sentence that ought to be two. When I’m not sure of a word, I look it up. There’s no shame in that. The shame is leaving it wrong when you could have checked.
