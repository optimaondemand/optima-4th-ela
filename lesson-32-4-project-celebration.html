🔧 Now fix this one — from Anne of Green Gables

Find and fix: capital M on “marilla” (proper noun), capital A on “anne” (proper noun), comma after “said” before the quotation, opening and closing quotation marks, capital I on “i” (pronoun) in two places, apostrophes in “don’t” and “I’d,” and a period at the end.

Corrected: Marilla looked at Anne across the kitchen table and said, “You have been a real blessing to me and I don’t know what I’d do without you.”

Capital M on “Marilla” (proper noun — start of sentence). Capital A on “Anne” (proper noun). Comma after “said” (before a direct quotation). Opening and closing quotation marks around the spoken words. Capital I on “I” (pronoun, always capitalized). Apostrophes in contractions “don’t” and “I’d.” Period inside the closing quotation mark. Compare with the model — both sentences show dialogue introduced by a speaker tag with a comma and quotation marks.

You have built an extraordinary vocabulary this year — across five novels and 30 weeks of word study. Today, choose one way to show what you know. You may use any word from any novel this year.

✍️ Option A — Use in 2 Sentences

Choose 1 vocabulary word. Write two original sentences that show you truly understand its meaning — use it in two different contexts.

📖 Option B — Find It in Reading

Find a vocabulary word (or a word related to it) in any of the novels you read this year. Write the sentence and explain what the word means in that moment.

🔤 Option C — Connect to a Root

Choose one vocabulary word and connect it to a root, prefix, or suffix. Explain how knowing the word parts helps you remember or understand its meaning.

🔄 Option D — Sort by Meaning

Choose 4 vocabulary words from across the year. Sort them into two groups by meaning, category, or feeling. Explain why each word belongs in its group.

💬 Option E — Explain to a Younger Student

Explain 2 vocabulary words as if you are teaching them to a younger student. Use simple words and give an example for each.

📝 Option F — Use 2 Words in a Response

Use 2 vocabulary words in a short written response about something from the reading this year. Both words must fit naturally.

Grammar · Day 4 Explain
Grammar Spiral — Year-End Reflection

📋 Teacher Recording Notes
• Week 32 Day 4 grammar: explain one grammar skill from this year
• Students choose ANY grammar skill: commas, dialogue punctuation, complex sentences, interjections, irregular verbs, etc.
• They explain what it is, why it matters for writing, and give an example
• Model what a good explanation sounds like: clear definition + reason + example
• ~2–3 minutes

You have learned a lot about grammar this year — commas, dialogue punctuation, complex sentences, interjections, verb tenses, and more. Each skill you learned helps your writing become clearer and more powerful.

💭

Why do grammar rules matter? A sentence without commas can confuse the reader. Quotation marks help us know who is speaking. Each rule is a tool. Which one do you understand best?

✏️ Your Task — Grammar Reflection (Journal)

Pick ONE grammar skill you learned this year. In your reading journal, write:

  1. What is the rule? Explain it in your own words.
  2. Why does it matter? What goes wrong in a sentence when this rule is missing?
  3. Give an example. Write one sentence that uses this rule correctly.
💡

Some grammar skills from this year: Commas in a series • Dialogue punctuation • Complex sentences (subordinating conjunctions) • Interjections • Irregular past-tense verbs • Appositives • Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)

📚 What Good Readers Do
Step back and look across all three novels.

Good readers step back and ask: what did I discover when I read these stories together? Each novel — Anne of Green Gables, Number the Stars, Prince Caspian — gave you different characters, different worlds, and different problems. But some things connected.

🧠 Ask yourself:

Look back across all three novels. Think about which characters changed the most, which moments surprised you, and which themes will stay with you. What did you discover about your theme when you saw it across more than one story?

💭 Hold these questions in mind as you look at the novel shelf and write your final synthesis responses.

📖 Part A: Looking Back, Looking Forward
Celebrate what you accomplished and set a goal for next year.
1Write your Final ELA Reflection (5–7 sentences). Look back on the whole year — the novels, the writing, the vocabulary, the grammar, the discussions. What are you most proud of? What surprised you about yourself as a reader and writer?
Think about everything, not just the novels. Did you get better at writing paragraphs? Did a grammar skill finally click? Did you start reading faster or understanding more? Be specific and honest.
Write 5–7 sentences reflecting on your entire ELA year. What are you proud of? What surprised you?
2Which character from any novel this year stays with you? Not your favorite — the one you think about even when you’re not reading. Who are they, and why do they stick with you?
This might be the character you admired most, argued with in your head, or felt closest to. It could also be a character whose choices bothered you. Either counts.
Name the character and novel. Explain why this character stays with you.
3Set a reading goal for next year. This isn’t “read more books.” Make it specific: a genre you want to try, a skill you want to sharpen, a kind of book you’ve been curious about, or a reading habit you want to build.
Good goals are specific and personal. “I want to try a mystery novel because I liked the suspense in Number the Stars” is better than “I want to read more.”
Write your specific reading goal for next year and explain why you chose it.
