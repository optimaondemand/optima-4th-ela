This Week at a Glance

The last three chapters. Harriet scouts behind Rebel lines and guides three Union gunboats up the Combahee River, and almost eight hundred people climb aboard. The war ends, but on the train home three men throw her into the baggage car. In Auburn she supports two freedmen’s schools, opens a home for old people, and speaks for women’s right to vote. On Day 4 your student looks back over the whole book. In Writer’s Workshop, the research report gets revised, edited and finished with a conclusion. This is the last week of the third quarter.

💛 A heads-up for families
In Chapter 18, the Union boats burn plantations and barns and trade fire with Confederate soldiers. In Chapter 19, the Fifty-fourth Massachusetts attacks Fort Wagner, President Lincoln is shot, and Harriet is hurt when three men drag her into the baggage car. In Chapter 20, Harriet’s first husband, John Tubman, is shot in an argument, and the book ends with Harriet’s death in 1913. The book uses the words of its time, such as “master,” “contrabands” and “Negro.” Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
How details support the central idea. Your student says a chapter’s big idea in one sentence, gathers details that prove it, and sets aside the ones that don’t. On Day 4 the same move covers the whole book: one moment from the beginning, one from the middle, one from the end.
Big idea
Victory is real, but the work isn’t finished. The war is won and slavery ends, but Harriet is still thrown off a train seat and still waiting for her pay. She keeps working for freedom for the rest of her life.

“It was dusk, and the North Star was shining in the darkening sky, when her eyes closed for the last time.”The last sentence of Freedom Train, Chapter 20

Words to Know

Use them at home!
fortificationswalls and defenses built to protect a place
furloughtime off from duty to go home for a while
pensiona regular payment to a veteran or a retired worker
temperancethe movement to cut down on or ban alcohol
Word parts
A year-end review: port (carry), rupt (break), struct (build), dict (say), then pens (pay), fid (trust) and lect (choose).transport · erupt · pension · confident · elect
Spelling
The year’s suffix rules. Double the last letter after a short vowel, drop a silent e, change y to i. The end of the base word tells you which rule to use.stop → stopping · bake → baking · cry → cried
Grammar
Prepositional phrases start with a word like in, on, under or through and end with a noun. They tell where, when or how. Too many in one sentence bury the verb.in the baggage car · through the woods · before dawn

A short job at the end of the Reading tab: one sentence on Day 1, a sort on Day 2 (“a real victory, or not finished yet?”), a log on Day 3, and a slider on Day 4 that looks at the whole book. Every moment in it is taken from the book.
🔍 Reading lens
Before reading, your student picks one of three lenses, such as “Pick the event” or “Ask why,” and reads through it. One line at the end of the chapter questions asks what the lens helped them notice.
🧵 Sew the Quilt
The word-parts game closes out the year: on Day 1 the roots from all year (port, rupt, struct, dict), and on Day 3 this week’s (pens, fid, lect). Four patches a day.
🎵 Harriet’s Song on the River
On Day 1, the card shows “Come Along,” the song Harriet sings on the Combahee when frightened people won’t let go of the rowboats. At the end of each verse they throw up their hands and shout “Glory!”, and the oarsmen push off.
🎧 Marcus
Two clips from Marcus in every lesson, a welcome and what to notice before reading, plus a short film on the Word Study tab about the day’s word work.
✍️ Copywork and the last sentence
On Day 1, your student copies Harriet’s own words about the raid: “The women had bags on their shoulders, baskets on their heads, and young ones tagging behind, all loaded.” On Day 3, they study the book’s last sentence and write one built the same way.

This week your student polishes the report. Day 1: read it out loud and fix at least two sentences that sound wrong. Sam, the example writer, hears “Tubman sang song say goodbye to her family” and fixes it to “Tubman sang a song to say goodbye to her family.” Day 2: a four-pass edit (prepositional phrases, vague words, run-ons, and an ending that answers the question). Day 4: a three-part conclusion that restates the main idea, sums up the best evidence and ends with a lasting thought. Each day’s work goes into My Research Report.

By Heart: the Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Days 2 and 4 your student practices the same four lines for the last time in this unit. By Day 4 they should be able to say them with almost no help on the screen.

How to help: Ask your student to say the lines to you without looking. Then ask whether the caged bird ever gets free, and whether Harriet does.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did Harriet do on the Combahee River?Listen for: she scouted first, then guided three gunboats up the river past the torpedoes, and almost eight hundred people came aboard.
  2. Why did Colonel Montgomery ask Harriet for a song?Listen for: frightened people held on to the rowboats so they couldn’t push off. Her song calmed them, they shouted “Glory!”, and the boats got away.
  3. Why was Harriet’s mother upset when she came home on furlough?Listen for: Harriet had been gone too long and had no pay to show for it. Her father, Ben, said he was proud of her.
  4. What happened on the train ride home?Listen for: the conductor and three men threw her into the baggage car, even with her War Department pass. The war was won, but she still wasn’t treated as an equal.
  5. What did Harriet do in Auburn after the war?Listen for: one of these: supported two freedmen’s schools by selling vegetables and chickens, took in guests who were old or sick, bought land for a home for old people, spoke for women’s right to vote.
  6. How does the book end, and how does it connect to Chapter 1?Listen for: the North Star is shining when Harriet dies. In Chapter 1, her father showed her that same star.
