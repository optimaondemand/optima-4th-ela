About the Book

Freedom Train: The Story of Harriet Tubman (1954) is a biography: a true story of a real person’s life. Harriet Tubman was born into slavery in Dorchester County, on Maryland’s Eastern Shore, around 1822. In 1849 she escaped north to Pennsylvania. Then she went back, again and again, as a “conductor” on the Underground Railroad, guiding family members and others to freedom. During the Civil War she worked for the Union army as a nurse, scout and spy, and helped lead a raid that freed more than 700 people. She died in Auburn, New York, in 1913.

Dorothy Sterling wrote the book for young readers, and she tells it like a story, with scenes and conversations. We read it because Harriet’s courage is real, and because a biography teaches a new kind of reading: finding the central idea, keeping events in order, and asking how an author sees her subject.

Sterling wrote 70 years ago, and historians have learned more since. Today, for example, the National Park Service counts about 13 rescue trips and about 70 people Harriet guided herself. Week 25 is about exactly this: comparing different accounts of the same events.

How a Week Works

Every week has four lessons, one fluency reading, and a Check-In. Each lesson follows the same path, so your student always knows what comes next. Across the four days, the skills build:

Day 1Learn it: define and explain
Day 2Find it: identify and sort
Day 3Use it: write and apply
Day 4Prove it: revise and explain
  • 📚What Good Readers Do: one reading habit to practice in the chapter, with an on-page box for reading notes
  • ✏️Daily Oral Language: fix a sentence by copying a correct model
  • 🔤Word study: prefixes, roots, spelling patterns and vocabulary from the chapters
  • 🌟Freedom Watch: each chapter names one kind of freedom, or one step toward it, to look for
  • 🎧Marcus, the guide: a college history student who grew up near the Choptank River on Maryland’s Eastern Shore. He introduces lessons in short audio clips. (He is made up; Harriet’s story is true.)
  • 🌌The quilt opening: each lesson opens on a quilt square that becomes a night sky leading to the North Star
  • 🎵Songs With a Hidden Meaning: real spirituals, with what an overseer heard and what the singers meant
  • 🎙️Fluency: a short passage read three times, then recorded
  • ✍️Writer’s Workshop: one research report built across the unit

Your Student’s Research Report

Writer’s Workshop

Across the unit, your student writes one research report that answers a real question, about Harriet Tubman, the Underground Railroad, or life in her time, using facts from more than one source. Each week adds one piece, with an example writer named Sam showing how it’s done.

Wk 20Choose a question
Wk 21Gather notes
Wk 22Draft
Wk 23Add facts and examples
Wk 24Revise and edit
Wk 25Publish and present

The report ends with a short oral presentation in Week 25. Writing saves in the browser on the computer your student uses, so if your student switches computers, help them keep a copy somewhere else too (a document or a printout).

By Heart: The Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

Dunbar, the son of two people who had been enslaved in Kentucky, wrote about a caged bird beating its wings against the bars. Its song is not happy; it is a prayer to be free. Your student hears the whole poem in Week 20, read by Marcus, and memorizes these four lines. Each time the poem comes up, a few more words fade from the screen.

How to help: Ask to hear it now and then. By Week 25 they should be able to say all four lines to you.

Good to Know Ahead of Time

The whole unit · slavery
The book tells the truth about slavery, written for young readers: families split apart, people bought and sold, hard work with no pay, and punishment. The focus is on Harriet’s courage. It’s fine to say “that’s a hard part of our history” and talk it through.
The words of its time
Sterling wrote in 1954 and uses the words of Harriet’s time and her own: “slave,” “Master” and “Mistress.” Many books today say “enslaved people,” which reminds readers that slavery was something done to people. Either word is fine to use at home, and the difference is worth a conversation.
Week 20 · Chapters 1–2
As a young child working for Miss Sarah, Harriet is whipped. The chapters don’t dwell on it, but some students find it upsetting.
Week 20 · Chapter 3
The grown-ups hear news of Nat Turner’s rebellion in Virginia (1831), a violent uprising against slavery, and of the harsh laws that followed.
Week 20 · Chapter 4
When Harriet won’t help catch a man running away, an overseer throws a heavy weight that hits her in the head. In real life, the injury affected her for the rest of her life.
Weeks 21–24
The danger of escaping: slave catchers, rewards, and long nights in hiding. Later, the Civil War. Battles are part of the story, but described for young readers.

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “What is this chapter mostly about?” and “How do you know?”
  • Find Maryland, Pennsylvania and Canada on a map, and follow Harriet’s routes north.
  • Go outside on a clear night and find the North Star together.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading, the poem, and the research presentation.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Doing the research for them. Choosing sources and taking notes are the lessons.
  • Treating every website as true. Week 25 is about checking sources against each other.
