Fix four mistakes: peter → Peter (proper noun), caspian → Caspian (proper noun), to wounded → too wounded (wrong “to”), miraz → Miraz (proper noun), and add a period at the end.

Corrected: Peter told Caspian that he was too wounded to fight Miraz in single combat.

Peter, Caspian, and Miraz are proper nouns — names are always capitalized. Too means “also” or “excessively” — not “to” (direction) or “two” (number). A period ends the statement.

🔤 Vocabulary: Decode & Discover
Chapter 13 words — use context and word parts to decode.

When you meet an unfamiliar word, use three steps to figure it out: read the context, look at the word parts, then confirm the definition. Watch how it works with challenge.

📌 Three-Step Decode: challenge

Step 1 — Context: Peter said he would send Miraz a challenge to single combat. What clues does the sentence give you? “Send” and “single combat” suggest this is a formal invitation to fight.

Step 2 — Word Parts: challenge comes from Latin calumnia — a false accusation, a claim that must be answered. It became an invitation that demands a response.

Step 3 — Confirm: challenge = an invitation to compete or fight; a call that demands an answer.

✏️ Grammar: Dialogue Punctuation — Define
Learn how commas, quotation marks, and end marks work in dialogue.

This week we’re studying dialogue punctuation — the rules for using commas, quotation marks, and end marks when characters speak. Today we’ll define the rules and look at examples from Chapter 13.

📌 Three Rules for Writing Dialogue

1. Quotation marks go around the exact words a character says.

Example: Peter said, “I’ll send him a challenge to single combat.”

2. Comma before the quote when a speaker tag comes first.

Example: Peter said, “I’ll send him a challenge.”

3. End mark inside the closing quotation mark.

Example: “That’s what I’m fighting him to find out.” said Peter. ← period stays inside.

Read these examples from Chapter 13. Notice where the commas, quotation marks, and end marks appear.

“Very well, then,” said Peter, “I’ll send him a challenge to single combat.”

Quotation marks surround the spoken words. A comma ends the first part of the dialogue before the speaker tag. The second part opens with a new set of quotation marks.

“That’s what I’m fighting him to find out,” said Peter.

The end mark (comma here, because the sentence continues) sits inside the closing quotation mark. The speaker tag follows without a capital letter.

💭

The pattern: Dialogue punctuation is how readers know who is speaking and where the words begin and end. Think of quotation marks as a frame — everything inside is the character’s voice.

Tomorrow you’ll practice identifying dialogue punctuation on your own. For today, just study these examples and notice where each mark appears.
