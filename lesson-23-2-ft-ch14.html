🌱 Morphology: Word Bases: lev + sist
Two roots: 'to raise' and 'to stand'

lev means to raise or lift. sist means to stand. Both roots build lots of words about strength, position, and persistence.

📌 Examples

elevate = e- + lev + -ate (to lift up)  |  lever = a tool for raising heavy things  |  resist = re- + sist (stand against)  |  persist = per- + sist (stand through; keep standing)

🔠 Spelling: Schwa & Accent in Academic Words
Discover the pattern by listening.

Both syllable types are everywhere. Look at these two groups of words:

Group A — longer history terms

ab-o-LI-tion-ist · CON-fed-er-ate · re-BEL-lion

Group B — names from the reading

MARY-land · Com-ba-HEE · STER-ling

🔍 What do you notice?

Say each word slowly. Which syllable sounds strongest?

Which syllables get softer and turn toward an ‘uh’ sound?

Why do longer history words need careful syllable work?

Longer words have stressed syllables and unstressed syllables.

The stressed syllable is the one you say with the most force. Unstressed syllables often soften into schwa, the quick ‘uh’ sound.

When a history word is hard to spell, divide it into syllables and mark the strongest one first.

Today’s focus: Today: add SPECIFIC details — names of people, exact dates, specific numbers. These make your research writing real instead of vague.

👀 Watch How — Sam, our example writer

Sam looks at his body paragraphs. Where he wrote ‘many people’ or ‘during the war,’ he now adds SPECIFICS.

BEFORE
Sam’s vague: Many people were freed during the war.
AFTER
Sam’s specific: On June 2, 1863, more than 700 enslaved people were freed in the Combahee River Raid — the first U.S. military operation led by a woman, Harriet Tubman.

Notice: Sam swapped many for 700+, during the war for June 2, 1863, and added the first U.S. military operation led by a woman. Specifics = trust.

📋 Today’s Tool: Specific Swaps

Three swaps to make your writing real: (1) ‘many’ → a number, (2) ‘during the war’ → a date, (3) ‘people said’ → specific names.
