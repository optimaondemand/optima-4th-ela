✏️ Grammar: Complex Sentences — Define
Learn what makes a sentence complex.

A complex sentence has two parts working together:

📌 The Two Parts

Independent clause — a complete thought that can stand alone as a sentence.

Dependent clause — a group of words that can’t stand alone. It depends on the independent clause to make sense. Dependent clauses start with signal words like because, although, when, even though, if, since.

Read these three example sentences from Chapter 7’s world. In each one, the dependent clause is italicized. Notice what it adds to the meaning.

Although every group wanted something different, Caspian had to make a decision for all of them.

The dependent clause gives the contrast. It sets up the challenge Caspian faces before we learn what happens.

Caspian decided to blow the Horn because no other plan was certain to work.

The dependent clause gives the reason. Without “because no other plan was certain,” we don’t understand why Caspian made this choice.

Trumpkin volunteered to go to Cair Paravel even though he doubted the Horn would work.

This dependent clause shows the contrast between what Trumpkin believes and what he does. It makes his loyalty stand out even more.

💭

Notice the pattern: In each sentence, the dependent clause adds information — a reason, a contrast, or a condition. The independent clause is the main action or idea. Together they make the sentence richer and more precise than either part alone.

Tomorrow you’ll practice identifying dependent clauses in sentences on your own. For today, just study these examples and notice how the pattern works.

🔠 Spelling: Schwa Sound — Discover
Find the pattern before you learn the rule.

Before I tell you the rule, I want you to discover it yourself. Look at these two groups of words. The bolded vowel in each word makes a special sound. Say each word out loud slowly.

Group A

about   afraid   away   alone

Group B

open   problem   person   lemon

🧠 Discovery Questions

Say each word out loud. Does the bolded vowel make its regular sound, or does it make a softer “uh” sound instead? Do you notice this same “uh” in both groups, even though different vowels are bolded?

The Schwa Sound (ə): In English, some vowels in unstressed syllables make a lazy “uh” sound. This sound is called the schwa. Any vowel — a, e, i, o, u — can make the schwa sound.

In Group A: the a in a-bout, a-fraid, a-way, a-lone all say “uh” not the short-a or long-a sound.
In Group B: the e in o-pen and prob-lem says “uh,” and the o in per-son and lem-on says “uh” too.
