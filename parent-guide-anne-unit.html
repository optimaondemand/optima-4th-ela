About the Book

Published in 1908 and set on Prince Edward Island, Canada, Anne of Green Gables follows an orphan girl who is sent by mistake to an elderly brother and sister, Matthew and Marilla Cuthbert, who had asked for a boy to help on the farm. Over eight weeks your student follows Anne from age 11 to 16 as she makes a best friend, makes plenty of mistakes, finds a rival in Gilbert Blythe, and grows into a young woman who knows who she is.

We read it because it is a classic with real literary language, and because Anne’s growing up gives fourth graders a lot to think and talk about: imagination, belonging, friendship, temper, courage, and learning from mistakes.

“Isn’t it splendid to think of all the things there are to find out about?”Anne Shirley, Chapter 2

🗓️

How a Week Works

Every week has four lessons, one fluency reading, and a Check-In. Each lesson follows the same path, so your student always knows what comes next. Across the four days, the skills build:

Day 1Learn it: define and explain
Day 2Find it: identify and sort
Day 3Use it: write and apply
Day 4Prove it: revise and explain
  • 📚What Good Readers Do: one reading habit to watch for in the chapter
  • ✏️Daily Oral Language: fix a sentence by copying a model from the novel
  • 🔤Word study: prefixes and Latin roots, spelling patterns, and vocabulary from the chapters
  • 🧩Grammar: taught with sentences from the book
  • 🪶Read Like a Writer: study how Montgomery built a sentence, then copy the pattern
  • 🎙️Fluency: a short passage read three times, then recorded
  • 👩‍🦰Guide characters: Anne, Marilla, Matthew, Diana and others take turns guiding each lesson
  • ✍️Writer’s Workshop: one realistic-fiction story built across the unit

Your Student’s Story

Writer’s Workshop

Across the unit, your student writes one realistic-fiction story about a kid who could be real, facing something kids really face. It will be about one page long, in five scenes. Each week adds one piece, with an example writer named Sam showing how it’s done.

By Heart: The Unit Poem

Who has seen the wind?
Neither I nor you:
But when the leaves hang trembling,
The wind is passing through.Christina Rossetti

Your student learns these four lines over the eight weeks. Each time the poem comes up, a few more words fade from the screen until they can say it from memory.

How to help: Ask to hear it now and then. By Week 14 they should be able to say the whole thing to you.

How Learning Is Checked

Every lesson
Chapter questions answered with evidence from the book, plus word-study and writing responses, submitted in Canvas.
Every week
A fluency recording, plus a Check-In (about 20–30 minutes) covering grammar, word parts, spelling, reading, and one short writing task.
End of unit
The finished realistic-fiction story, published in Week 14.

Lessons have built-in self-checks and hint buttons, so students can see right away whether they’re on track.

💛

Good to Know Ahead of Time

Week 14 · Chapter 37
Matthew dies near the end of the book. Many readers find this the hardest chapter. It may help to read it together or check in afterward.
Week 10 · Chapter 16
At a tea party, Anne mistakes currant wine for raspberry cordial and Diana goes home drunk. It’s played as a misunderstanding, and it causes a big rift between the families.

The language is from 1908, with long sentences and words like “kindred spirit” and “tragical.” Short read-alouds together can help a lot, especially in the first two weeks.

🤝

How to Help (Without Doing It for Them)

Try this

  • Ask “Where in the book does it say that?” when your student answers a question.
  • Point them to the hint buttons and self-checks before you step in.
  • Ask the questions in each weekly guide. Talking it through first makes the writing easier.
  • Be the audience for the fluency reading and the poem.

Try to avoid

  • Typing or wording answers for them. Teachers need to see your student’s own thinking.
  • Fixing their story draft. Revising and editing are lessons of their own in Weeks 12–13.
  • Reading ahead and sharing what happens next. The surprises are part of the fun.
