✏️ Grammar: Spiral Review — Define
Look back at the grammar skills you learned this year.

Before you finalize your comparison paragraph, review the key editing rules that matter most for polished writing.

📌 Key Editing Rules for Your Final Draft

Proper nouns — Names of specific people, places, and things are always capitalized (Anne, Narnia, Harriet).

Commas in a series — Use commas to separate three or more items (courage, loyalty, and hope).

Quotation marks in dialogue — Quotation marks surround the exact words a character speaks. A comma or end mark belongs inside them.

Interjections — Words that show strong feeling, set off by a comma or exclamation point (Oh! Well, I suppose so.).

Irregular past-tense verbs — Some verbs do not add “-ed” to form the past tense (ran, wrote, brought, told).

Subject-verb agreement — The verb must match the subject in number (Anne runs. The girls run.).

🔠 Spelling: Spiral Review — Editing for Patterns
Review the major spelling patterns you learned this year.

This year you studied several important spelling patterns. Good spellers don’t just memorize words — they notice patterns and apply rules. Here is a review of the major patterns you learned.

📌 Major Spelling Patterns from This Year

Doubling rule: Double the final consonant before adding -ing or -ed to a short-vowel word ending in one consonant. (run → running)

Drop-e rule: Drop the silent e before a vowel suffix. (write → writing)

Change-y rule: Change y to i before adding -es or -ed (unless the y follows a vowel). (carry → carried)

au / aw: Both make the same /ô/ sound. au usually appears in the middle of a word; aw often comes at the end. (caught, draw)

ei / ie: Use ie after most letters; use ei after c or when the sound is /ā/. (believe, receive, eight)

Read your paragraph as if you’re seeing it for the first time — pretend you wrote it six months ago. Where is the theme clearly stated? Where do you need a stronger example? Is the opening strong enough to pull a reader in?

📌 What Makes a Strong Comparison Paragraph?

Opening sentence: Names both novels and hints at the shared theme — not just “Both books have themes.”

Evidence from each novel: A specific moment, character action, or quote — not a vague summary.

Connection: A sentence that explains HOW the two examples connect to the same theme.

Closing sentence: Returns to the big idea and leaves the reader thinking.

Mark at least two places in your paragraph where you can improve. Then use those marks when you write your revised paragraph in the Assignment tab.
