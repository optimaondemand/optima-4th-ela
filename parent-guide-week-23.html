This Week at a Glance

Harriet’s fame spreads across the North. She speaks at antislavery meetings, helps a crowd rescue a captured runaway named Charles Nalle, and plans with John Brown, who calls her “General Tubman.” Then the Civil War begins. Harriet sails south to Beaufort, South Carolina, where thousands of people have escaped to the Union army. She nurses the sick, teaches freedwomen to earn a living, and is there on January 1, 1863, when the Emancipation Proclamation is read aloud. In Writer’s Workshop, your student makes the research report stronger with more facts, exact names and numbers, and a quote.

💛 A heads-up for families
In Chapter 15, a fight breaks out in Troy, New York, when a crowd frees Charles Nalle from the police: people are clubbed, and one man is struck with a hatchet. John Brown leads an armed raid on Harpers Ferry and is hanged. In Chapter 17, Harriet nurses soldiers with wounds, fever and smallpox. The book uses the words of its time, such as “master,” “contrabands” and “Negro.” Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
The author’s perspective. Your student looks at how Dorothy Sterling feels about Harriet: the words she chooses, the moments she gives the most pages to, the difference between a fact and her opinion of it, and what another person telling the same story might leave out.
Big idea
Free, but not yet all the way free. In Beaufort, people who have escaped say “We’s all free,” but a soldier says they are only half free until they have land and fair pay. On January 1, 1863, freedom is promised.

“This is the first day we have ever had a country.”Harriet, Chapter 17

Words to Know

abolitionista person who worked to end slavery
Word parts
lev means “lift.” sist means “stand.”elevate · lever · resist · persist
Spelling
Schwa and stress. In a long word, a vowel in a quiet syllable fades to “uh.” To spell it, say a related word where that syllable is loud.abolition → abolish · definition → define
Grammar
Comparative adverbs compare how two things were done. Short ones take -er; most -ly ones take more. Never both.ran faster · listened more carefully · not “more louder”

New in the Lessons

The third-quarter lesson pages work the same way as last week. This week’s extras:

⭐ Freedom Watch, after reading
A short job at the end of the Reading tab: one sentence on Day 1, a sort on Day 2 (“moving freedom forward, or holding it back?”), a log on Day 3, and a slider on Day 4. Every moment in it is taken from the book.
🔍 Reading lens
Before reading, your student picks one of three lenses, such as “Pick the event” or “Ask why,” and reads through it. One line at the end of the chapter questions asks what the lens helped them notice.
🧵 Sew the Quilt
The word-parts game goes on with new roots, lev (lift) and sist (stand). It is tied to the flag a woman in the Beaufort camp pieces together from scraps of old clothing in Chapter 16. Four patches a day.
🎵 A Song That Changed Its Meaning
On Day 4, the card shows “My Country, ’Tis of Thee,” the song the crowd sings on January 1, 1863. Most of them had never been included in its “land of liberty.” That morning, Harriet says, “This is the first day we have ever had a country.”
🎧 Marcus
Two clips from Marcus in every lesson, a welcome and what to notice before reading, plus a short film on the Word Study tab about the day’s word work.
✎ Copia: Say It Three Ways
On Day 1, your student rewrites “Harriet told her story to the crowd” three ways: a new opening, a stronger verb, and an added detail.

By Heart: the Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Days 2 and 4 your student practices the same four lines, with a little less help on the screen each time. The whole poem, read aloud, is still a tap away.

How to help: Ask your student to say the lines to you without looking. Then ask how the caged bird’s song is like the song the crowd sings on January 1, 1863, and how it is different.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did John Brown call Harriet, and why?Listen for: “General Tubman.” He was so impressed by her courage and leadership that he wrote she was “the most of a man” he ever met.
  2. How did Harriet help free Charles Nalle?Listen for: she slipped past the guards, sent boys to ring the fire bell for a bigger crowd, and held on to Nalle through the fight until he got away to Canada.
  3. What was Harriet’s snake story about?Listen for: treating each bite won’t help while the snake keeps biting. The snake is slavery; Mr. Lincoln has to set the slaves free.
  4. Why did a soldier say they were only “half free”?Listen for: they had escaped, but they still had no land to farm and no soldier’s pay.
  5. What happened on January 1, 1863?Listen for: the Emancipation Proclamation was read aloud, everyone sang “My country, ’tis of thee,” and Harriet said it was the first day they ever had a country.
  6. How does Dorothy Sterling feel about Harriet? How can you tell?Listen for: she admires her, and a word or moment from the book that shows it.
