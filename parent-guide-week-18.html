This Week at a Glance

Annemarie figures out the grown-ups are lying and asks Uncle Henrik for the truth. He tells her, “It is much easier to be brave if you do not know everything.” That night, people gather around the casket, and Nazi soldiers arrive. Peter reads aloud a psalm about the God who numbers the stars. The casket turns out to hold warm clothes and blankets, and the Rosens slip away toward the boat. At dawn, Mama still hasn’t come home.

💛 A heads-up for families
In Chapter 10, soldiers burst into the gathering in the night and threaten the family. It’s one of the most frightening scenes in the book. The chapter also includes Psalm 147, read aloud by Peter, which gives the book its title.
Reading skill
Noticing symbols and repeated ideas. The stars, Ellen’s necklace, the casket: objects that keep coming back often stand for something bigger.
Big idea
The cost of courage. Even when the brave thing works, people still pay for it: goodbyes, fear, and long hours of waiting.

Words to Know

Use them at home!
proceedto move forward; to continue
concedeto give in; to admit defeat
accessa way to reach or enter something
symbolan object or image that stands for an idea
Word parts
ceed, cede, and cess are three spellings of one root meaning “to go” or “to yield.”proceed · concede · access · recess
Spelling
Consonant + -le endings. These words split just before the consonant-le, which makes its own syllable.can·dle · ta·ble · bun·dle · han·dle
Grammar
Contractions. The apostrophe marks the missing letters. Watch for it’s (it is) vs. its (belonging to it).they’re · can’t · it’s

New in the Lessons

Grete, the Museum Case, and Annemarie’s Path are back this week. Look for these too:

🌲 The Way to the Boat
This week’s word-parts game. On the night of Chapters 9 to 12, no lights are allowed at Uncle Henrik’s farm. Each right word shows the next stretch of a dark path, past the apple tree, through the woods and across the meadow, until it reaches Uncle Henrik’s boat at the harbor on Day 4.
⭐ The Psalm
On Day 2, a starry card shows the line from Psalm 147 that Peter reads aloud, the line that gives the book its title. Grete reads it too.
🤝 Real Rescuers
On Day 4, a new card about Børge Laursen and Jacob Andersen, two fishermen who carried Jewish families to Sweden in their little boat, Marie, about ten times in October 1943.
🏛️ Museum Case
Real objects tied to each chapter: an armband from a Jewish Dane who escaped to Sweden and joined Danes training there to help free Denmark, a pair of Sabbath candlesticks like the ones the Rosens left behind, and a tall Danish clock like the one Annemarie keeps checking while she waits for Mama.

By Heart

Robins will wear their feathery fire
Whistling their whims on a low fence-wire;
And not one will know of the war, not one
Will care at last when it is done.Sara Teasdale, “There Will Come Soft Rains” (1920), lines 5–8

The four Teasdale lines keep fading on Days 2 and 4. By now your student may be able to say most of them from memory.

How to help: Ask what Teasdale means by “not one will know of the war.” How does that fit this week’s chapters?

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What did Uncle Henrik mean that it’s easier to be brave if you don’t know everything?Listen for: if you don’t know the secret, you can’t give it away, so not knowing protects you.
  2. What was really in the casket?Listen for: warm clothes and blankets for the people escaping by boat.
  3. Why do you think the book is called Number the Stars?Listen for: the psalm says God numbers the stars, so each one counts; any thoughtful connection to Ellen’s star works too.
  4. Who was brave this week, and how do you know?Listen for: an action from the book used as evidence.
