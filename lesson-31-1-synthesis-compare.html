📚 What Good Readers Do
Compare themes across texts

This week you are not reading a new chapter. You are doing something harder: holding two books up next to each other and comparing their themes.

Comparing works best when you start small. One idea, two books. Not every idea and every book at once — that turns into a list, and a list is not a comparison. Today you choose your one idea and the two novels you will hold up beside it.

💡

How to Do It:

  1. Pick one idea: Choose a single word: courage, loyalty, leadership, or growth. One word only. It is the lens for everything else this week.
  2. Pick two books: Choose the two novels where you remember the most about that idea — not the two you liked best. You need memory, not affection.
  3. Say why those two: Write one line: I picked these two books because in both of them somebody has to … If you cannot finish that line, swap a book.
🧠 As you work today, ask yourself:

Choose one idea — courage, loyalty, leadership, or growth — and choose two of your novels. Then write one line saying why those two books belong together for that idea.

💭 Hold these questions in mind as you work through today’s lesson.

🔤 Vocabulary: Decode & Discover
Four words from across your year’s novels — decode and discover their meanings.

These four words appeared across the novels you read this year. Use three steps to figure out each word: read the context, look at the word parts, then confirm the definition. Watch how it works with solemnly.

📌 Three-Step Decode: solemnly (from Number the Stars)

Step 1 — Context: Annemarie nodded solemnly and promised she would be brave. What clues does the sentence give you? “Nodded” and “promised” suggest this is a very serious moment.

Step 2 — Word Parts: solemn (serious, grave) + -ly (adverb suffix meaning “in that way”). So solemnly means “in a serious, grave way.”

Step 3 — Confirm: solemnly = done or said in a very serious, sincere way.

✏️ Grammar: Spiral Review
Day 1: Notice — two versions of the same idea. Which is stronger, and what did the structure do?
✍️ Grammar Level: Author's Editor
🔎 Same facts, different sentence

Two versions of the same paragraph opening. Everything you learned this year is in play.

🔎 Read both before you choose

Before: The children returned to Narnia. It had been a year. Everything had changed. They were confused.

After: When the children returned to Narnia after a year away, everything had changed, and they were confused.

Four flat statements became one sentence with a spine.

🎯

Directions: Tap the version you think is stronger — then be ready to say what the structure did to the meaning.

Tap the stronger version:

The children returned to Narnia. It had been a year. Everything had changed. They were confused. When the children returned to Narnia after a year away, everything had changed, and they were confused.

Count what changed: a dependent clause (When...) subordinates the arrival; a prepositional phrase (after a year away) absorbs a whole sentence; a conjunction (and) joins the consequence.

Four sentences of equal weight told the reader nothing about what mattered. One sentence with a main clause tells them exactly where to look. That is the year’s work in a single revision.

🪶 Show What You Know
Name three different grammar moves in the stronger version and say what each one did for the reader.
Explain the effect, not the label.
