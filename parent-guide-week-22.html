This Week at a Glance

Harriet is free, but her family is not, so she keeps going back. She leads out her sister, her brothers and many others, and then her old parents, who ride north in a cart she builds from junk-heap wheels. In 1850 a new law lets slave catchers chase runaways even in the North, so Harriet takes her people all the way to Canada. Rewards for catching her grow huge, and the enslaved people begin to call her Moses. In Writer’s Workshop, your student starts drafting the research report.

💛 A heads-up for families
Harriet’s brothers are about to be sold away from their families. Old Ben is put in jail for helping a runaway. Posters offer rewards for Harriet “dead or alive,” and one writer worries she may be burned alive if she is caught. In Chapter 14, Harriet carries a pistol and tells frightened runaways there is no going back. She gives crying babies medicine to keep them asleep. The book uses the words of its time, such as “master” and “slave catchers.” Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
Cause and effect in history. Your student names what made something happen, follows a chain of causes, tests whether an event really caused the next one or only came first, and decides which cause mattered most.
Big idea
Freedom for one is not enough. Harriet could stay safe. Instead she goes back again and again, because her people are still enslaved.

“My people must go free.”Harriet, Chapter 10

Words to Know

Use them at home!
abolitionthe act of ending something, especially slavery
conductora guide on the Underground Railroad, or the person who runs a train
obtainto get or acquire something
expressto make a thought, feeling or idea known to others
Word parts
press means “push.” tain means “hold.”express · impress · contain · obtain
Spelling
The suffixes -ive, -al and -ic turn a word into a describing word. Sometimes a letter drops first, like a silent e.act → active · music → musical · hero → heroic
Grammar
Adverbs tell how, when or where something happens. A strong verb can often do the job of two adverbs, and some adverbs add nothing at all.moved quietly · came back again · waited outside

New in the Lessons

Week 22 is part of the third quarter, so the lesson pages work a little differently from here on. Look for these:

⭐ Freedom Watch, after reading
The Freedom Watch is now a short job at the end of the Reading tab: one sentence on Day 1, a sort on Day 2 (“helping someone get free, or not?”), a log on Day 3, and a slider on Day 4. Every moment in it is taken from the book.
🔍 Reading lens
Before reading, your student picks one of three lenses, such as “Pick the event” or “Ask why,” and reads through it. One line at the end of the chapter questions asks what the lens helped them notice.
🧵 Sew the Quilt: Old Rit’s quilt
The word-parts game goes on with a new quilt: the one Harriet’s mother finished in Chapter 2, which Harriet tucks over her parents’ legs as she drives them north in Chapter 12. Each right press or tain word sews a patch, four a day.
🎵 Songs With a Hidden Meaning
On Day 2, the card shows the two songs Old Rit sings at the jail. The sheriff hears a hymn and a silly dance song. Old Ben hears that Harriet has come for him, and that the escape is tonight.
🎧 Marcus
Two clips from Marcus in every lesson, a welcome and what to notice before reading, plus a short clip on the Word Study tab about the day’s word work.
✎ Copia: Say It Three Ways
On Day 1, your student rewrites “Harriet led her people north” three ways: a new opening, a stronger verb, and a where or a how.

Your Student’s Research Report

Writer’s Workshop
Wk 20 · Choose a questionWk 21 · Gather notesWk 22 · DraftWk 23 · Add facts and examplesWk 24 · Revise and editWk 25 · Publish and present

This week your student starts drafting, one piece a day. Day 1: a one-sentence thesis that answers the research question (who, what and why). Day 2: an introduction with a hook and the thesis. Day 3: the first body paragraph, from the first idea group of notes, with a topic sentence, facts with sources, and a link back to the thesis. Day 4: the second body paragraph, in the same shape. Each day’s new piece goes into the one document, My Research Report.

By Heart: the Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Days 2 and 4 your student practices the same four lines, with a little less help on the screen each time. The whole poem, read aloud, is still a tap away.

How to help: Ask your student to say the lines to you without looking. Then ask how Harriet is like the caged bird this week, and how she is different by the end.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. Harriet was free. Why did she keep going back?Listen for: her family and her people were still enslaved. She told William Still, “My people must go free.”
  2. How did Harriet get her old parents out?Listen for: she raised money in New York, Old Rit sang a secret message at the jail, Jacob Jackson gave his horse, and Harriet built a cart from junk-heap wheels and drove them to the train.
  3. What is the “lion’s paw”?Listen for: the British lion, meaning Canada, where Queen Victoria’s law kept runaways free.
  4. What did the Fugitive Slave Law cause?Listen for: runaways weren’t safe in the North anymore, so the Underground Railroad stretched to Canada.
  5. Why did people call Harriet Moses?Listen for: like Moses in the Bible, she went back into the land of slavery and led her people out, hundreds of them, and never lost one.
  6. What is your thesis sentence?Listen for: one sentence that answers the research question with who, what and why.
