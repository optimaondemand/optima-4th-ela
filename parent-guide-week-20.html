This Week at a Glance

A new unit and a new kind of book. Freedom Train is a biography: a true story of a real person’s life. It begins in 1827 on a plantation in Maryland, where Harriet Tubman was born into slavery. This week she is a child, then a teenager. She works from before sunrise until after dark, learns from the people around her, and keeps looking north. Your student also starts a new writing project: a research report.

💛 A heads-up for families
The book tells the truth about slavery, at a fourth-grade level. This week Harriet is whipped by the woman she works for, the grown-ups hear news of Nat Turner’s rebellion, and at the end of Chapter 4 Harriet is badly hurt when an overseer throws a heavy weight. The book also uses the words of its time: “slave,” “Master” and “Mistress.” Reading together, or checking in after each chapter, helps. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
Finding the central idea in a biography. Instead of holding on to every fact, your student asks what each chapter is mostly about, and proves it with details.
Big idea
Learning to look north. With no school and no books, Harriet still learns: from her father, from songs, and from the stars.

“That’s the North Star, child. The best star there is.”Daddy Ben, Chapter 1

Words to Know

biographya true book about someone’s life, written by another person

postponeto put off until a later time
Word parts
ex- means “out of.” post- means “after.”exit · extend · postpone · postscript
Spelling
Syllable division in names. Find the vowels, split between the consonants, and read each chunk.Mar·y·land · Bal·ti·more · Phil·a·del·phi·a
Grammar
Adjectives describe nouns: what kind, how many, which one.a cold spring · three geese · a brave, determined girl

New in the Lessons

A new book brings a new guide and a new look. Look for these:

🎧 Marcus, the unit guide
Short audio clips from Marcus, a college history student who grew up on Maryland’s Eastern Shore near the Choptank River and used to lead walking tours there. He welcomes each lesson, sets up the day’s Freedom Watch, and says what to notice before reading. Marcus is a made-up guide; Harriet’s story is true.
🌌 The quilt opening
Each Freedom Train lesson opens with a short animation: a quilt, one square of it turning into a night sky, and the stars leading to the North Star. Students can replay it from the top of the page.
🧵 Sew the Quilt
This week’s word-parts game. Each right ex- or post- word sews a patch onto a quilt, four patches a day, and on Day 4 a North Star is stitched in. The game says plainly that historians don’t think quilts were secret escape maps; quilts were part of family life, like the one Harriet’s mother made.
🎵 Songs With a Hidden Meaning
On Days 3 and 4, a card shows real spirituals, such as “Go Down, Moses” and “The Gospel Train,” with what an overseer heard and what the singers meant. Students think first, then reveal the meaning.
📝 My reading notes
A small note-taking box in What Good Readers Do, shaped to each day’s reading skill: three facts and one sentence, a four-column chart, a list of who taught Harriet, and an idea with two proofs. The notes follow your student to the Reading tab and into the journal.

Your Student’s Research Report

Writer’s Workshop

A research report answers a real question with facts. This week your student writes one opinion sentence and one research sentence to feel the difference, brainstorms questions about Harriet Tubman, the Underground Railroad or Maryland in the 1820s, picks one and tests that it isn’t too big or too small, and plans three sources: the book, a website, and the library (or a family member).

By Heart: the New Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Day 2 your student hears the whole poem, read by Marcus. From then on, they learn these four lines, with a little less help on the screen each time.

How to help: Ask what the caged bird wants, and why its song isn’t a happy one. Then ask your student to say the lines to you.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What was one day like for Harriet when she was not yet eight?Listen for: up before the sun, tending fires and the baby, working until after dark.
  2. How did Harriet learn without a school?Listen for: from her father, from songs and stories, and from news passed from cabin to cabin.
  3. What did Daddy Ben teach her about finding north?Listen for: moss on the north side of the trees by day, the North Star at night.
  4. What’s your research question?Listen for: a real question about Harriet or her time, not too big and not too small.
