This Week at a Glance

The biography is finished, so this week your student reads history the way people first read it. On the night of June 1–2, 1863, Harriet Tubman guided Union gunboats up the Combahee River in South Carolina, and more than 700 enslaved people were freed. Your student reads three real accounts of that night: a newspaper report that never names her, a letter she dictated herself, and a book written six years later. Day by day they compare what each writer tells, leaves out, and why. In Writer’s Workshop, the research report is revised, edited, published and presented, one step a day.

💛 A heads-up for families
These are real documents from 1863, so they use the words of their time, such as “slaves” and “contrabands.” In her own letter, Tubman calls the people she freed “livestock” on purpose, to mock how slaveholders talked about human beings; the lessons explain this. The accounts mention burning plantations and fighting. The full list is on the unit guide.
Reading skill
Comparing accounts of the same event. Your student reads each account on its own, finds one fact in every source and sets the versions side by side, asks who was close to the event and who wrote later, and finally picks the account they would lean on and says why.
Big idea
Who tells the story matters. One newspaper called Harriet only “a black woman.” Her own letter, printed weeks later, shows her voice and her humor. Reading more than one account gives a fuller, truer picture.

“We weakened the rebels somewhat on the Combahee River…”Harriet Tubman, in a letter she dictated in 1863

Words to Know

Use them at home!
gallantbrave and noble
commissarya place where an army keeps its food and supplies
rebeldomthe Confederate states; the land of the rebellion
contrabandthe Union army’s word for people who escaped slavery to its lines
Word parts
Prefixes that tell where: trans- (across), inter- (between), sub- (under), super- (above, beyond).transportation · international · submarine · supernatural
Spelling
Words that sound alike. A spellchecker misses them because they are real words. Say the long form out loud to check.affect / effect · its / it’s · their / there / they’re · accept / except
Grammar
Commas in a series separate three or more things in a list, including the comma before and, so the reader knows where each one stops.bread, cheese, and a knife

By Heart: the Unit Poem

It is not a carol of joy or glee,
But a prayer that he sends from his heart’s deep core,
But a plea, that upward to Heaven he flings—
I know why the caged bird sings!Paul Laurence Dunbar, “Sympathy” (1899), the last four lines

On Days 2 and 4 your student says the four lines again. By now they should know them by heart.

How to help: Ask your student to say the lines to you without looking. Then ask why Dunbar never names the bird’s cage, and why one 1863 newspaper never named Harriet.

Ask at Home

Better than “How was school?”
  1. What happened on the Combahee River?Listen for: Harriet guided Union gunboats up the river at night, and more than 700 enslaved people were freed.
  2. What did the newspaper call Harriet instead of her name?Listen for: “a black woman,” and a “she-Moses.”
  3. Why did Harriet call the people she freed “livestock” in her letter?Listen for: she was mocking the slaveholders, who treated people as property.
  4. Which account would you trust most about that night? Why?Listen for: a reason about who wrote it, when, and how close they were.
  5. What is one thing none of the sources can tell us?Listen for: anything the writers could not see or know, such as what one freed person felt.
  6. Harriet could not read or write. How did her own words get printed?Listen for: she dictated her letter, and Franklin Sanborn printed it.
